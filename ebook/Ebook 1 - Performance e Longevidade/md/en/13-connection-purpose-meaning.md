***Pillar T — Tending Mind, Body & Bonds***

## Chapter 13 — Connection, Purpose, and Meaning — The Body Extended into the World

Ricardo came back to the office eighteen months after the heart attack.

You already know his story from Chapter 1. At 52, a tech executive, with an annual checkup "normal" by the lab eight months earlier, he collapsed in his office parking lot on a Tuesday morning with a near-total occlusion of the left anterior descending artery. He survived through the luck of a coworker who found him and called the ambulance in eleven minutes. In Chapters 4 and 5, he reappears on the side preventive medicine knows how to treat — expanded panel, ApoB, Lp(a), CAC of 187, arterial age. In Chapter 15, he returns as co-author of his own plan five years later.

In this visit, eighteen months later, the numbers were in order. LDL 62. ApoB 58. Lp(a), which had been one of the engines of the event, was at 117 nmol/L — still high, but down about 27% from the first post-heart-attack measurement, consistent with the median effect of a PCSK9 inhibitor (FOURIER trial). Combined management: optimized statin dose, ezetimibe, and evolocumab (Repatha) every two weeks (Ch 5). CRP 1.4 — a bit higher than I'd like, but much better than the 2.4 of the immediate post-heart-attack period. Blood pressure 122/78. Weight down 15 lbs, body composition with 11 lbs more lean mass than before the event. VO₂ max recovered, and even surpassed, the pre-event level — at his last cardiopulmonary exercise test, 38 ml/kg/min, above average for 53 years. Coronary angiography one year after the stent: no plaque progression.

By the labs, Ricardo was in better shape now than the week before the heart attack.

He sat in the chair, looked at the folder of test results in my hand, looked at me, and said the sentence I have heard for twenty years, in variations, from patients whose biological panel medicine has been able to fix:

> *"Doctor, the labs are fine. I'm not."*

I asked what he meant. He began, slowly, to describe each side of the sentence.

With his wife, Marina, the relationship had changed after the heart attack in a way neither of them knew how to name. She had received the call mid-morning that Tuesday, had crossed the city to the ER, had been with him through the procedure overnight, had spent nine months of rehabilitation by his side. And since that day, she had begun treating him as if he were fragile. She no longer argued, no longer complained, no longer pushed back when he worked late. He missed the arguments. Sex life between them had not returned. For eight months, neither of them had brought it up. Each thought it was the other who was afraid, or uninterested, or waiting for the right moment. The right moment never came.

The friends from his pre-heart-attack routine — college classmates, weekly squash partners, couples' dinners — had been disappearing. Some he avoided because they reminded him of the old life, too intense; others avoided him, lacking the courage to know what to say. Ricardo had three adult children who called on Sundays. He had his wife, fragile now. He had no one else to call at ten at night.

His phone had become a difficult companion. Since the heart attack, he checked the bank app, the health insurance app, his lab results, financial news, twice an hour. On insomnia nights — five or six per month — he scrolled international news, stock charts, crisis predictions from three to five in the morning. Sleep fragmented.

The professional ambition that had moved him for thirty years had emptied. The director role at the company, the board target three years out, the acquisition he had been coordinating before the heart attack — all of it had been talked through, returned to, and none of it felt central anymore. *"For what, doctor? I almost died trying to get that promotion. If I had died, no one would have said at my funeral 'such a shame, he was so close to the board.' No one."*

And, finally, something it took him a few minutes to say. He had never been religious. He had been pragmatic, skeptical, slightly ironic about people who were. Since the heart attack, he had been left with a feeling he didn't know what to call — it wasn't faith, it wasn't religion, but it was a question that wouldn't go away. *"I almost went. And then I came back. And what stays from that? I still don't know where to look when that question shows up."*

That is the map of this chapter. Ricardo brought, in order, the five themes The ACTS Method calls the relational and existential dimension of Pillar T. Sexual life. Bonds. Screens and attention. Purpose. Meaning and spirituality. And under all five, the bidirectionality fragmented medicine continues to ignore: anxiety-depression and inflammation feeding each other, even when the biological pillars are in order.

Chapter 12 covered the inner work — what the patient does with themselves. This chapter covers the body extended into the world: who you look toward, with whom you live, how you use attention, and why.

### Sexual Life: The Thermometer No One Measures

This is the section many readers expect to skip, and that some colleagues prefer not to address. I'll ask you to stay.

Sexual life is the only territory of the human body that depends, simultaneously, on intact cardiovascular system, endothelial function, balanced autonomic nervous system, hormonal signaling, consolidated sleep, stable mood, and functioning relational bond. When something starts failing in any of those layers, sexual life tends to be one of the earliest indicators — because it requires all of them to work together. Ignoring it in the office is missing one of the most sensitive integrated health thermometers the patient brings.

**Sexual activity after a cardiovascular event.** For Ricardo, the first question wasn't about desire, nor about erectile function — it was about fear. Eight months without sex, in large part, because neither he nor Marina knew if it "was allowed." The American Heart Association published a *scientific statement* in 2012 specifically on sexual activity in cardiovascular disease patients. The practical message is clear: **for the vast majority of post-heart-attack patients in adequate rehabilitation, sexual activity is safe starting 4 to 6 weeks after the event**, provided the patient tolerates moderate physical activity (climbing two flights of stairs without dyspnea or angina is the traditional clinical test). The metabolic cost of typical sex with a known partner is around 2 to 5 METs — equivalent to brisk walking or stair climbing. Absolute risk of a cardiac event during sexual activity is very low; a patient in complete rehabilitation has lower risk than walking on the street. The orientation had never been given to Ricardo because it had never been asked — neither by the cardiologist, nor by me, in that initial sequence of panel and rehabilitation visits. Eight months of silent abstinence for lack of a four-minute conversation.

**Erectile dysfunction: the artery's first warning.** The evidence is old, robust, and still poorly applied. The *Princeton Consensus*, reaffirmed in its third edition in 2012 and reinforced by subsequent cohorts, documented that new-onset erectile dysfunction (ED) in men over 40 precedes, on average, the first manifest cardiovascular event by 3 to 5 years. The mechanism is anatomical: penile arteries are 1 to 2 mm in diameter, while coronaries are 3 to 4 mm. Endothelial dysfunction — the biological event preceding atherosclerotic plaque — compromises smaller vessels first. Erection failure shows up before chest pain, the same way the fuel light comes on before the car stops. A man over 40 with new ED and no obvious local cause warrants a complete cardiovascular panel with ApoB, hs-CRP, and frequently a CAC score (Chapter 5). Treating the symptom with sildenafil without investigating the trajectory is wasting the only early signal the body was giving. In Ricardo's case, the warning had come and was not heard — looking back, there had been complaints of weaker erections in the two years before the heart attack, normalized as *"work fatigue."*

**PDE5 inhibitors in the post-event patient.** The PDE5 inhibitors — sildenafil (generic, Viagra), tadalafil (Cialis and generics), vardenafil — are the central pharmacological tools for ED, and are **safe** in the stable coronary patient, without rest angina, decompensated heart failure, or severe arrhythmia. Tadalafil 5 mg as a daily dose has the additional benefit of helping symptomatic prostatic hyperplasia and, in small studies, a favorable endothelial vascular signal. Sildenafil is the cheapest and has the longest market track record. **The absolute contraindication** is concomitant use with nitrates in any form (oral isosorbide, sublingual or transdermal nitroglycerin) — the combination can produce profound blood pressure drops with risk of coronary ischemia. A patient with a recent heart attack who uses rescue nitrate needs that conversation before any prescription. Caution also with alpha-blockers for the prostate (tamsulosin, doxazosin): the combination requires titration. Ricardo started tadalafil 5 mg/day at the same visit, with instructions to avoid rescue nitrate on dosing days. But the medication was only half the path.

**Female sexual dysfunction: the less recognized territory.** Historically, the office has heard more male than female complaints — and a large share of patients don't mention sexual difficulties until directly asked. Female sexual dysfunction has multiple components operating in different layers: **desire** (the fuel), **arousal** (the autonomic nervous system response), **lubrication** (the genital vascular response), **orgasm** (the resolution), **satisfaction** (the integrated judgment), and **pain** (the disruptor). Each layer has distinct determinants. Reduced desire is frequently hormonal and contextual. Lack of lubrication can be estrogenic and vascular. Anorgasmia appears in serotonergic dysfunction, especially with SSRI use. Pain on intercourse in a postmenopausal woman is almost always treatable vulvovaginal atrophy — and it is treatable with the simple intervention discussed in Chapter 10.

The traditional linear model of Masters & Johnson — desire → arousal → orgasm — is limited for many women, especially in long-term relationships. The circular model proposed by Rosemary Basson, more clinically useful in midlife women, recognizes that the intent of intimacy and the relational context frequently precede desire, rather than follow it. Knowing this changes the conversation. Asking a woman married for twenty years if she has "spontaneous desire" can be the wrong question; asking whether, in a favorable relational context, the bodily response appears, is the more useful question.

The treatments with evidence in female sexual dysfunction are distributed across different chapters of this book, and it is worth grouping them here. Systemic hormone therapy in the window improves arousal, lubrication, and mood, and is discussed in detail in Chapter 10. **Topical vaginal estrogen** (vaginal estradiol — Estrace, Vagifem, Imvexxy in the U.S.; estriol formulations available in Brazil and Europe) treats vulvovaginal atrophy with minimal systemic absorption — resolving the most common postmenopausal complaint with a safety that systemic HRT sometimes lacks (Chapter 10). **Low-dose transdermal testosterone** in women with hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD) and already-optimized estrogen has 2019 global consensus support as an evidence-backed option (Chapter 10). And **cognitive behavioral therapy focused on sexual function**, combined with couples therapy when there is a relational layer, has trials showing benefit in various dysfunctions. What is not on the radar — compounded hormone pellets, gestrinone — remains off the radar: not FDA-approved in the U.S., and prohibited in Brazil under Anvisa for cosmetic, athletic, and muscle-gain purposes by the November 2024 ruling (Chapter 10).

**Medications that silently sabotage sexual life.** A significant share of clinical sexual dysfunction is medication-induced and underrecognized because neither the doctor asks nor the patient mentions it. The main culprits:

- **Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)** — sertraline, fluoxetine, paroxetine, escitalopram. They cause sexual dysfunction in 40 to 70% of users, with reduced desire, delayed ejaculation, and anorgasmia. Bupropion, vortioxetine, and agomelatine have a cleaner sexual profile — legitimate alternatives in patients with relevant complaints.
- **Non-selective beta-blockers (propranolol in particular), high-dose thiazides, and central antihypertensives** (alpha-methyldopa, clonidine) are associated with sexual dysfunction. ACE inhibitors/ARBs and calcium channel blockers have a more sexually neutral profile — preferred options in the hypertensive patient with sexual complaints.
- **Finasteride and dutasteride** — discussed in Chapter 8, with explicit positioning on post-finasteride syndrome (PFS). Use for cosmetic alopecia carries the risk of persistent sexual dysfunction that can be irreversible.
- **Antipsychotics**, particularly those that raise prolactin (risperidone, haloperidol).
- **Endocrine oncology agents** — tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors, antiandrogens. Frequently associated with severe dysfunction; warrant explicit conversation with the oncologist about management.
- **Gabapentinoids** (gabapentin, pregabalin) and some anticonvulsants.
- **Combined birth control pills** in a subgroup of women, through SHBG elevation and reduced free testosterone. Not in all, but present.

The approach is not to automatically remove the medication — many are necessary. It is to recognize the cause, discuss alternatives when they exist, and not leave the patient in silent suffering believing it's *"just age."*

**The relational layer.** Marital satisfaction and sexual function form a well-documented bidirectional cycle. Relationships with chronic emotional distance tend to present sexual dysfunction before the biological one settles in; unaddressed sexual dysfunction corrodes the relationship. The useful question to the couple is different from the question to the individual: *"How would each of you describe your sexual life right now — separately and then together?"* Couples therapy with a sexual component has evidence in dysfunction of relational context, particularly in long-term couples. In couples where non-sexual intimacy (conversation, touch, shared time without an agenda) has deteriorated, working only on the sexual component without the base is inefficient — it's like treating the tip and ignoring the structure. Ricardo and Marina started couples therapy with a psychologist recommended by the team's cardiologist — someone accustomed to the psychosocial impact of a heart attack on the couple, which is greater than curative medicine usually recognizes.

**Frequency and health outcomes: what the data say.** The Caerphilly study, conducted by George Davey Smith and colleagues, followed Welsh men for ten years and showed that higher sexual frequency was associated with lower all-cause mortality — with a relative risk of approximately 0.5 in the most active compared to the least active. Subsequent work partially reproduced the finding and documented associations between intercourse frequency with orgasm and cardiovascular, immune, and life satisfaction markers. The correct interpretation isn't "prescribe sex": it is to recognize that an active sexual life works as an integrated indicator that the underlying systems are in order. Quality matters more than quantity. And, as in so many other pillars, the clinical question is whether what exists is satisfactory for the patient — not whether there is a number to hit.

**What I ask in the office.** The phrasing I learned to use is neutral and open: *"How is your sexual life right now? Is it the way you'd like it to be?"* If the answer is short, I record and move on — the patient opened the channel and will return when ready. If the answer brings content, I listen. In a patient with optimized biological pillars and persistent sexual complaint, the workup runs across three axes: **cardiovascular and endothelial** (especially in men), **hormonal** (in both, adapted to reproductive context), and **psychosocial and relational**. In a patient with new complaint and compatible age, the complete cardiovascular panel comes before the automatic PDE5 prescription — and, often, the workup completely changes what would have been a hurried script.

Sexual life is part of The ACTS Method because it is part of the body. Preventive medicine that ignores this layer is treating only half.

### Social Bonds and Support: The Ties No Treatment Replaces

There is a question I didn't have on my history-taking script until a few years ago — and that I now consider as important as any test: *"Who can you count on when things get hard?"*

The answer to that question has prognostic value rivaling the CAC score, ApoB, and VO₂ max. Ricardo, when I asked, took almost a minute to answer. Then he said: *"besides Marina, there's no one I would call at ten at night."* Three children, a broad professional network, more than two hundred contacts on his phone. And a list, by the ruler of the evidence, that was insufficient.

The **Harvard Study of Adult Development** is the longest longitudinal study of adult life ever conducted. It began in 1938, following 268 Harvard students. In the 1970s, it expanded to include 456 residents of poor Boston neighborhoods. In the following decades, it included spouses and children. After more than 85 years of uninterrupted follow-up, today under the direction of psychiatrist Robert Waldinger, it is still running. The most robust finding — replicated across cohorts, methods, and generations — is simple and counterintuitive: the strongest predictor of physical health, mental health, and longevity is not cholesterol, income, IQ, genetics, or lifestyle factors in isolation. It is the quality of relationships at age 50. Satisfaction with close relationships in midlife predicts health at 80 better than total cholesterol at 50.

This finding isn't idiosyncratic to a privileged cohort. Julianne Holt-Lunstad (Brigham Young University, *PLOS Medicine* 2010, 148 studies, 300,000 participants) showed that people with strong social connections have a 50% greater chance of survival. A subsequent meta-analysis (2015, 3.4 million people): loneliness raises the risk of premature death by 26%, social isolation by 29%, living alone by 32% — magnitudes comparable to smoking and greater than obesity. In 2023, the U.S. Surgeon General classified loneliness as a national-priority public health problem.

The Blue Zones data — regions of the world with the highest concentration of centenarians, which we already cited in Chapter 8 — converge. In Okinawa, there are *moais*: groups of friends formed in childhood that stay together throughout life, sharing meals, responsibilities, and emotional support. In Sardinia, in Ikaria, in Nicoya, in Loma Linda, the patterns repeat: densely connected communities, with daily rituals of meeting, mutual care, and belonging. It isn't the olive grove that makes the centenarians — it's the olive grove plus the network.

**Types of bonds and why each one matters.** Social connection isn't a single variable. It has at least four layers, and each protects in a different way.

The most intimate layer is the **life partner** — and, when present and functioning, is the most potent factor. The Harvard Study is clear in identifying marital satisfaction in midlife as the most robust predictor of late-life health. But there is a detail frequently lost in a hurried reading: a chronically unhappy marriage is, in terms of outcomes, worse than living alone. The metric that counts is relationship quality, not marital status. A patient who arrives saying *"we've been married twenty years"* with absent eyes is providing clinical information as relevant as a CRP of 2.5 — and, often, the two go together.

The layer of **close friendships** — three to five people you can call without warning to ask for real help — is the second. In the Blue Zones, those people are a daily institution. In contemporary urban life, they are rare and require active maintenance. The practical rule I have used in clinic: if you can't list three people, outside your home, that you could call at eleven at night in an emergency, there is an empty space in your health plan that no supplement will fill. Ricardo, that visit, couldn't list three.

The layer of **extended family and community of origin** weighs more for some and less for others, depending on biography. Belonging to a group with shared purpose — spiritual, professional, civic, athletic, volunteer — brings independent benefit, documented in studies of religiosity, volunteering, and community participation. It isn't about the nature of the group; it's about belonging and regularity.

The layer of **work** matters more than is recognized. A Japanese study with about twenty thousand workers showed that having at least one trusted colleague at work reduced cardiovascular risk over ten years. A hostile work environment, on the other hand, operates as front-line chronic stress. For most adults, work occupies half of waking hours — the affective climate of that environment is a public health variable.

**Midlife is a valley of silent erosion.** Around 40 and 50, many people notice that close friends from 20 or 30 have been thinning out — small children, long workdays, city moves, the pandemic, remote work that erased the accidental encounter at the office, social networks that simulate contact without delivering. Volume drops, the depth of the few who remain may hold, but contact frequency plummets. It's in this valley that social connection most needs to be actively cared for — and it is in it that most people fail. Treating friendship as scheduling, prioritizing low-stakes meetings (coffee, walks, dinners with no agenda), and taking initiative when no one else is — these are acts of primary prevention with impact equivalent to many of the biochemical protocols that dominate the office.

**Spouse trauma as a clinical variable.** A point that post-heart-attack medicine systematically underestimates: **the spouse went through the event too**. Marina received the call mid-workday, crossed the city not knowing if Ricardo would be alive when she arrived, accompanied the surgery overnight — and had been sleeping poorly since. The literature on caregiver strain after a cardiac event is clear: spouses of heart attack patients have elevated rates of anxiety, depression, insomnia and, in long-term follow-up, increased cardiovascular risk. When the spouse begins to treat the patient as fragile — avoiding conflict, suppressing their own emotional life, carrying vigilance alone — the couple enters a zone where both deteriorate in silence. The intervention that opened space for Ricardo and Marina was a couples therapy session in which, for the first time in eighteen months, Marina cried recounting what she had lived that morning. Ricardo heard things he didn't know she carried. The conversation opened the rest.

**The caregiver's overload in other geometries.** Ana, from Chapter 12, started caring for her mother with dementia in the middle of her own treatment adjustment. Caring for a family member with chronic illness, dementia, or functional dependence is one of the most potent documented stressors — it raises CRP, IL-6, cortisol, blood pressure, and shortens telomeres at magnitudes comparable to military combat stress in some studies. "Caregiver syndrome" isn't metaphor; it is biology. Recognizing it opens space for necessary conversations in the office: dividing tasks with other family members, accepting paid or volunteer outside help, preserving windows of personal respite, not delaying one's own health for after the family member improves — because in many cases they will not. A patient who cares alone for a dependent needs twice the preventive attention, because their system is under double load, and the double isn't always reversible.

**Grief, widowhood, and "broken heart syndrome."** The months after the loss of a spouse or primary bond are a period of documented elevated cardiovascular risk. Takotsubo cardiomyopathy — in which the left ventricle takes the shape of a Japanese ceramic vase (*tako-tsubo*) under a peak of emotional catecholamines — is the most dramatic expression of a broader phenomenon. Widows and widowers have elevated cardiovascular risk on average for twelve to twenty-four months after the loss. Closer clinical follow-up during that period, structured psychological support, and the gradual rebuilding of bonds (new friendships, grief groups, volunteering, therapy) are not soft advice — they are evidence-informed preventive medicine.

**What to ask, what to recommend.** There is no test for bonds. The tool is the direct question: *"Who can you count on when things get hard?"*, *"When was the last time you met a friend without a specific reason?"*, *"Do you feel part of any group?"* Evasive answers point to a layer that needs to enter the care plan. The practical recommendation isn't the abstraction "make more friends" — it is: schedule the next meeting before leaving this visit, and reserve a recurring weekly time for a non-transactional connection. What the calendar doesn't hold, life doesn't sustain.

For Ricardo, the concrete action left the visit with dates set. Three phone calls that week — three friends who had vanished — not to explain anything, just to set up coffee. One came back the same week. The other two, over the next three months.

### Screens, Fragmented Attention, and Contemporary Mental Hygiene

There is an environmental variable preventive medicine has had difficulty naming clearly, because it is new, because it affects physician and patient alike, and because asking the patient to *"use the phone less"* sounds moralistic. But the biology of intensive screen use is increasingly documented, and ignoring it is leaving out one of the most potent stressors of contemporary daily life. Ricardo, since the heart attack, was checking his phone about 200 times per day — a number he himself calculated when he activated the smartphone's screen-time monitor. Three and a half hours of active use. Another hour of passive use. The first gesture on waking, the last before sleeping.

**The biology of compulsive use.** Social networks and news apps work by **variable reward** — the same principle as slot machines. With each check, there is an unpredictable chance of finding something relevant: a like, a message, a piece of news that moves mood. That unpredictability engages the dopaminergic system in a way predictable rewards cannot. The result is compulsive behavioral patterns in healthy people without psychiatric diagnosis.

Gloria Mark, an attention researcher at the University of California, Irvine, documented in direct observation studies that average attention time on a single task fell from 2.5 minutes in 2004 to about 47 seconds in 2023. Each interruption requires on average 23 minutes to recover the previous level of focus — time that rarely exists before the next interruption. The pattern accumulates as elevated cortisol, cognitive fatigue, worse decisions, and — over the long term — symptoms that resemble generalized anxiety without identifiable cause. In 2023, Mark published *Attention Span*, synthesizing two decades of research with a clear thesis: modern human attention isn't being lost to decadence; it is being systematically exploited by product architectures that profit from fragmentation.

**Doomscrolling, social comparison, and anxiety.** Jonathan Haidt, in *The Anxious Generation* (2024), documented the impact of social networks on adolescent mental health — with particularly severe effects on girls. But the biology he describes is not exclusive to adolescence. Chronic social comparison (seeing peers' edited and apparently successful lives), excessive negative news (*doomscrolling*), and compulsive use in low-boredom-tolerance situations affect adults equally. Meta-analyses in adults show association between time on social networks and depressive symptoms, anxiety, and insomnia — modest in aggregate but significant in vulnerable subgroups.

**Phone before sleep.** Here the evidence is robust and the bridge to Chapter 14 is immediate: blue light from screens close to the eyes suppresses melatonin; cognitive activation by content (news, email, message) delays sleep onset; the notification arriving overnight fragments the cycle. Ricardo's pattern — checking the phone from 3 to 5 a.m. when he woke — is among the most damaging to sleep and cardiovascular recovery.

**Interventions that work.** The question isn't moralistic; it is architectural. The default smartphone design maximizes use. Individual environment design can maximize choice. Tools with evidence:

- **Phone-free bedroom.** Charger in the living room, analog alarm clock by the bed. Simplest change, biggest impact on sleep quality and the first minutes of the morning.
- **Grayscale mode.** Removing color from the smartphone reduces the visual appeal of notifications and scrolling. Small studies show 30–50% reduction in screen time without additional intervention.
- **Disable push notifications** for everything that isn't direct human communication (texts/WhatsApp from family). Financial apps, news, social networks: disable. The patient decides when to check, not the app.
- **Scheduled check-in windows** instead of continuous use. Email three times a day; social network at a specific moment; news in a short morning window.
- **Time limits per app** (Screen Time on iPhone, Digital Wellbeing on Android). Fifteen to twenty minutes daily for social networks, four hours total — limits the user sets and accepts.
- **Family dinner rules.** Phone off the table. A small gesture with cumulative impact on couple and family connection.
- **Detox period.** One day a week without social networks, one weekend a month without phones beyond strictly necessary use, one week a year fully disconnected. Small pauses recalibrate the dopaminergic system.

Ricardo, in the sequence of actions that left the office, chose three: an analog alarm clock by the bed (phone out of the bedroom), grayscale on the phone during the week, and automatic blocking of financial apps from 10 p.m. to 7 a.m. Three weeks later, the middle-of-the-night insomnia episodes dropped from six per month to two.

### Purpose and Meaning: Ikigai as a Hidden Biomarker

A question I started asking, after years of not asking: *"what is the reason that gets you out of bed in the morning?"*

Ricardo, when I asked, paused for a long time. Thirty years of career had been moved by a clear answer — ambition, promotion, results, recognition. The heart attack pulled that answer out from under him, and he hadn't found another. For eighteen months, he had returned to work and done what needed doing, but without the force that previously got him out of bed at six in the morning with enthusiasm. *"Doctor, I do it. I don't want to anymore."* It is a clinical formulation of a problem that has a name, evidence, and, surprisingly, treatment.

**The Ohsaki study and *ikigai* as a longevity factor.** Toshimasa Sone and colleagues, in northern Japan, followed more than 43,000 adults aged 40 to 79 for seven years. The variable of interest was a simple question: *"do you have ikigai?"* — a Japanese word that means, roughly, "that for which it is worth getting up in the morning." The result published in *Psychosomatic Medicine* in 2008: those who answered yes had **~33% lower all-cause mortality risk** (adjusted hazard ratio 1.5 for those answering no; 95% CI 1.3–1.7). The effect was larger still for cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.6, 95% CI 1.3–2.0 in those without *ikigai*, equivalent to ~37% lower risk in those with it) and for external causes, but not significant for cancer. A single question, a single word — with prognostic weight comparable to classic risk factors.

**Eudaimonia vs. hedonia: two kinds of well-being with different biology.** Andrew Steptoe, an epidemiologist at University College London, dedicated part of his career to distinguishing two types of subjective well-being. **Hedonic** — pleasure, momentary satisfaction, good experiences. **Eudaimonic** — sense of purpose, engagement with something larger than personal comfort, personal growth. Steptoe's studies with British cohorts (ELSA, *English Longitudinal Study of Ageing*) showed that eudaimonic well-being has a stronger association with hard health outcomes (mortality, cardiovascular disease incidence, physical function) than hedonic well-being. Having pleasure isn't a guarantee of long life. Having purpose is a stronger association.

**Viktor Frankl and the post-trauma psychiatry thesis.** In *Man's Search for Meaning* (1946), Austrian psychiatrist Viktor Frankl — Auschwitz survivor — proposed a thesis that modern biology has repeatedly confirmed: suffering without meaning is unbearable; suffering with meaning is bearable. Logotherapy, the psychotherapy school he founded, starts from the premise that the search for meaning is a primary human motivation, and that existential crises — midlife, post-trauma, or grief — are treatable when the question of meaning is taken seriously as a clinical issue, not deferred as private philosophy. Ricardo, post-heart-attack, was in one of Logotherapy's most classic situations: an event that interrupted the storyline and left the protagonist not knowing what the next chapter is.

**Retirement without preparation is a risk factor.** Cohorts show that abrupt retirement, without planning purpose for the free time, is associated with peak cognitive decline and mortality in the first two to five years following. The effect is mitigated when the retiree has structured post-career activity — volunteering, mentoring, personal project, art, learning. Professional transition asks for the same kind of preparation medicine treats as obvious in other transitions (pregnancy, newborn, loss): anticipation, structure, and support network.

**Blue Zones and the convergence of centenarian cultures.** In Okinawa, the word is **ikigai**. In Nicoya (Costa Rica), the expression is ***plan de vida***. In Ikaria, Loma Linda, and Sardinia, the concept appears with other names but the same function: a daily reason to get up, a continuous commitment to something larger than personal routine, belonging to a long-term project or community. It isn't coincidence. It is a biological variable.

**How to work on purpose in the office.** It isn't the clinician's task to deliver purpose; it is the clinician's task to open space for the question. Some formulations that help:

- *"If you stepped away from work for three months — without guilt, without pressure — what would you choose to do with that time?"*
- *"What did you do at 14 that wasn't an obligation, and that still feels meaningful when you remember it?"*
- *"Who, inside or outside the family, depends on you in a dimension that isn't financial?"*
- *"Is there something you would be furious about if you had to take to the grave without having done?"*

These questions belong in the office, not only on the couch. Evasive answers signal that the existential layer asks for attention — it can be with a psychologist, with a life coach in academic transitions, with an existentially oriented therapist (Logotherapy, existential analysis), or with a spiritual director, depending on the patient's inclination.

Ricardo, three months after the visit that opened this chapter, started giving an hour a week of voluntary mentorship to young executives at the same company where he worked — people between 28 and 35, on intense career trajectories, often without an available father or senior mentor. Financial return was zero. The impact on sleep, mood, and — six months later — his own inflammatory markers, was visible.

### Spirituality and Religiosity: A Variable Medicine Avoided

There is a terrain Western medicine avoided for decades for understandable reasons — separation between science and religion, fear of proselytism, discomfort with non-measurable categories — and that in the last twenty years has begun to be treated with more maturity. A reader of this book may have deep faith, may be agnostic, may be a convinced atheist. Nothing that follows depends on creed.

**Harold Koenig and the systematization of the evidence.** A psychiatrist at Duke University, Koenig has dedicated three decades to research on the relationship between religiosity, spirituality, and health. His *Handbook of Religion and Health*, in the third edition (2012) and subsequent updates, synthesized more than 3,000 empirical studies. The consolidated conclusion: religiosity and spirituality, measured by community involvement, regular contemplative practice, and structured beliefs, are associated with **lower incidence of depression, lower suicide, lower substance abuse, lower cardiovascular mortality, better recovery after events, and greater longevity**. Modest to moderate aggregate magnitudes, but consistent across studies, cultures, and demographic slices.

**The Adventist Health Study and the Loma Linda Blue Zone.** The only Blue Zone outside the Mediterranean or Pacific is **Loma Linda, California** — a Seventh-day Adventist community, with life expectancy roughly 10 years above the American average. The Adventist Health Study, ongoing since 1976, documented the factors at play: predominantly vegetarian diet, regular physical activity, no smoking, no alcohol, adequate sleep, intense community belonging with structured weekly rituals (Sabbath), and daily spiritual practice. Separating the diet effect from the community effect is methodologically difficult — but the convergence of factors points to the same place: lifestyle + belonging + structured contemplative practice.

**Contemplative practice independent of tradition.** For the patient who isn't situated in a formal religion — a profile common among urban midlife adults in many countries, including the U.S. and Brazil — there are secular contemplative paths with their own evidence. **Mindfulness meditation** (mindfulness, Ch 12). **Contemplative practices in nature** — silent walks, sitting in view, structured contemplation. **Reflective writing** (*journaling*) — Pennebaker's exercise showed reduction of inflammatory markers and mood improvement in controlled studies. **Deliberate silence** — one hour a week without music, news, conversation, or task. Simple, free practices that operate by a similar mechanism to religious traditions: creating space where attention slides from the immediate to the broader.

**The cultural context.** Both Brazil and the U.S. are among the most religiously plural societies in the world. In Brazil: Catholics, Evangelicals across many denominations, Spiritists, practitioners of Afro-Brazilian religions, Buddhists, the religiously unaffiliated growing fast, and various combinations. In the U.S.: Catholic, mainline Protestant, evangelical, Black church, Mormon, Jewish, Muslim, Buddhist, Hindu, and the religiously unaffiliated growing similarly. The clinician's posture, in either country, is not to recommend a specific religion — it is to recognize when the spiritual dimension is an active variable in the patient's life, to open space for it in the history-taking, and, when relevant, to suggest the patient connect more regularly with the practice that already makes sense to them. For the patient without creed, I don't insist; I offer secular contemplative paths. For the patient with creed, I don't proselytize and don't diminish; I recognize the practice as a health factor and encourage regularity and community.

**Ricardo and the secular ritual.** Ricardo didn't return to any religion. But after the heart attack, he and Marina began taking a silent one-hour walk on Sunday mornings, in a park near home. No phones, no forced conversation, no agenda. Just walking together, in silence, in the morning. They called it *"the ritual."* It wasn't prayer. It wasn't formal meditation. It was a pause. And it was, for the couple, the most healing moment of the week — more than any medical visit I could offer. Months later, he described it to me as *"the hour when I remember where I'm going."* It was secular spirituality, and it was preventive medicine. The two can be the same thing.

### Anxiety, Depression, and the Bidirectionality Fragmented Medicine Ignores

For a long time, medicine treated mental health as a consequence of physical health — *"he became depressed because he got sick"* — or as an isolated cause, treated only in the psychiatric sphere. Contemporary evidence shows something more complex: the relationship is bidirectional and biochemically mediated.

**Chronic inflammation causes depression.** Pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α, produced in states of obesity, diabetes, autoimmune disease, or chronic infection, cross the blood-brain barrier and alter neurotransmission — particularly reducing the availability of serotonin, dopamine, and BDNF. Patients with persistently elevated CRP have higher incidence of major depression. Anti-inflammatory treatments improve depressive symptoms in subgroups of patients. This is the territory of "inflammatory depression," recognized today as a legitimate phenotype.

**And the reverse path: depression causes inflammation.** Patients with major depression have, on average, higher CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α than controls. Chronic anxiety produces the same profile. The biology of discouragement, rumination, and constant worry is not ethereal — it is inflammatory.

**Post-heart-attack: a paradigmatic case of bidirectionality.** Roughly 15 to 20% of patients who survive a myocardial infarction develop major depression within twelve months. Ten to 15% develop anxiety disorder, including post-traumatic stress disorder focused on the cardiac event. These conditions, untreated, double the risk of a new cardiovascular event and mortality over the next five years, independent of other risk factors. It is one of the most robust associations in behavioral cardiology, and one of the most underrecognized in clinical practice — where post-heart-attack follow-up frequently focuses on lipids, blood pressure, and ventricular function, ignoring the psychological layer that is moving the persistent CRP and the fragmented sleep. Ricardo, with CRP of 1.4 eighteen months later and a clear pattern of post-trauma anxiety, was a classic example — what his biochemistry needed wasn't an additional statin adjustment, it was trauma-focused CBT.

That bidirectionality has an important practical implication. A patient with CRP of 2.5 who isn't responding to nutrition, sleep, and exercise interventions may be carrying a source of inflammation that no diet resolves: the chronic activation of the stress system itself. Treating anxiety — with CBT, with mindfulness, with medication when indicated — can reduce CRP more than any additional dietary change. And the opposite: a patient with treatment-resistant depression may be carrying an unrecognized systemic inflammation that sabotages any antidepressant. The two sides talk to each other.

The clinical consequence: it isn't *"mind OR body."* It is recognizing that when an inflammatory marker persists despite optimized pillars, the cause may not be on the plate — it may be in the nervous system. And vice versa.

### When to Refer — and Why There's No Shame in It

There is a clear limit to what the clinical physician can do in the psychological dimension. Talking about sleep, guiding breathing, recommending nature exposure, encouraging regular mindfulness practice — that fits in the office of the internist, the cardiologist, the nephrologist. Treating generalized anxiety disorder, major depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, complex trauma, panic disorder — that calls for a specific professional.

Referring to a psychologist or psychiatrist isn't a failure of preventive medicine. It is part of it. Just as I don't intend to treat a complex arrhythmia without partnership with an electrophysiologist, I don't intend to treat a long-standing anxiety condition without partnership with a psychologist or psychiatrist — even when the effects of that condition show up on my routine labs.

Stigma around mental health still cuts through clinical practice — in Brazil, in the U.S., and beyond. Ricardo, initially, resisted. *"Doctor, I'm not a therapy person. I solve my own things."* The answer I gave was the same one I have given in variations for twenty years: I'm not asking him to *"be a therapy person."* I'm asking him to recognize that his body went through an event that surgically was resolved, but psychologically was still open — and that ignoring that part was equivalent to ignoring an abnormal lab just because the complaint sounded "psychological." He agreed to go for three months, with the understanding that he could stop if he didn't see value. He stayed eighteen.

Patients hesitate to seek psychiatry out of fear of "having to take medication for life," or out of belief that their anxiety is "just personality." Joint work — the clinician caring for the biological pillars, the mental health professional caring for the psychological dimension — is what produces the best results in the most complex patients. Reductionism on either side compromises care.

For Ana, from Chapter 12, the conversation about referral opened after the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores, and she ended up accepting CBT + MBSR + an antidepressant for six months. For Ricardo, the conversation opened after the relational-sexual-purpose sequence described in this chapter, and involved trauma-focused CBT for cardiac trauma, couples therapy with Marina, and, given his resistance to pharmacology, a path without an antidepressant but with close follow-up. Both paths are valid. What is not valid is leaving the psychological layer out of the plan because it is "difficult," "uncomfortable," or "outside my specialty." It is part of the plan.

### Ricardo: Twelve Months Later

Ricardo came back to the office twelve months after the visit that opened this chapter — that is, thirty months after the heart attack. The labs, again, were in order — as they had been before. ApoB 56. LDL 58. CRP, this time, at 0.6 — finally in the optimal range. Morning cortisol at 15 (it had been at 21 at the eighteen-month return). Sleep consolidated, with only one episode of insomnia in the last ninety days. VO₂ max stable at 38 ml/kg/min — a value that, at 54, places Ricardo above the 75th percentile for his age band and, by Chapter 7's logic, already in territory of "preserved marginal decade."

The labs reflected what the conversations of the intermediate visits had shown. Sex life with Marina had returned — not at the frequency of youth, but with relational quality he described as *"better than before the heart attack, oddly enough."* Couples therapy had dismantled the silence that had settled in. Tadalafil 5 mg/day as a pharmacological tool, maintained, with full and comfortable sexual return. The three friends who had vanished came back into regular orbit — monthly couples' dinners, weekly squash with two of them. The phone hadn't been in the bedroom for ten months; the middle-of-the-night insomnia crises had practically disappeared. The volunteer mentoring of young executives at the company had become, for him, the most important part of the week — and not as euphemism; he counted the hours of Wednesday mornings. The ritual of the silent walk with Marina on Sundays held firm, 52 Sundays a year.

In the closing conversation, I asked if anything still bothered him. He thought. He said he still had hard days — the anniversary of the heart attack, for example, which had passed not long ago, always brought a kind of shadow. But he said something that stayed with me: *"Doctor, I thought I'd come out of this story more fragile. I think I came out more whole."*

### The Essential in 60 Seconds

- **Sexual life is an integrated health thermometer.** Sexual activity is safe starting 4–6 weeks post-heart-attack for most patients (AHA 2012, two-flight stair test). New-onset erectile dysfunction after 40 precedes a cardiovascular event by 3–5 years (Princeton Consensus) — it calls for a CV panel and CAC, not just sildenafil. PDE5 inhibitors are safe in the stable coronary patient, with absolute contraindication with nitrates. Female sexual dysfunction has six distinct layers (desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, pain); treatments in Chapter 10. Medications that silently sabotage: SSRIs, non-selective beta-blockers, high-dose thiazides, finasteride/dutasteride (PFS — Ch 8), prolactin-elevating antipsychotics, endocrine oncology agents. Office question: *"how is your sexual life? Is it the way you'd like it?"*

- **Social bonds** predict health and longevity at magnitudes comparable to conventional biochemical markers. The Harvard Study (88 years) and Holt-Lunstad's meta-analyses converge: social connection is a first-order factor. Four layers — partner, close friendships, community with purpose, work. Midlife is a valley of silent erosion; treating friendship as scheduling is primary prevention. **The spouse of a post-cardiac-event patient** also carries trauma — couples therapy recalibrates the pair. Caregiver overload and grief (takotsubo) are periods of high risk.

- **Screens and fragmented attention.** Variable reward engages the dopaminergic system compulsively; Gloria Mark documented the drop in average attention from 2.5 min to 47 s between 2004–2023. Phone before sleep suppresses melatonin and activates cognition — worse in post-event patients. Interventions: phone-free bedroom, grayscale, disable notifications, scheduled check-in windows, time limits per app, periods of disconnection. Architecture change, not moralism.

- **Purpose and meaning** have measurable biological effect. Ohsaki study (Sone 2008, Japan): *ikigai* associated with ~33% lower all-cause mortality risk (and ~37% lower in cardiovascular mortality) over 7 years. Steptoe (UCL): eudaimonic well-being > hedonic well-being in hard outcomes. Viktor Frankl in Logotherapy: suffering with meaning is bearable. Retirement without purpose planning is a risk factor. Blue Zones converge: *ikigai*, *plan de vida*, daily reason to get up.

- **Spirituality and religiosity** have modest but consistent evidence. Koenig (Duke) synthesized 3,000+ studies: religiosity associated with lower depression, lower suicide, lower mortality, greater longevity. Adventist Health Study / Loma Linda is an American Blue Zone. For the secular patient, contemplative practice (mindfulness, nature, *journaling*, deliberate silence) operates by similar mechanism. Clinician's posture: recognize the dimension, open space, don't proselytize.

- **Anxiety and depression have a bidirectional biology with inflammation.** Persistent CRP despite optimized pillars may be a nervous system on alert, not a plate error. Post-heart-attack, 15–20% develop depression and 10–15% cardiac PTSD — they double the risk of a new event if untreated. Treating the psychological layer can move CRP more than any dietary adjustment.

- **Referring to a psychologist or psychiatrist** isn't a failure of preventive medicine — it is part of it. Stigma still cuts through clinical practice. Joint work (clinician + mental health) produces results in complex patients that neither side alone reaches.

But there is another system, even more fundamental, that governs much of the biology we discussed in these two chapters and that operates largely below consciousness. Without it, none of the other pillars hold. It is the system that organizes time inside the body — and it is the subject of the next chapter.
