***Pillar A — Activity, Alimentation & Smart Adjuncts***

## Chapter 8 — Alimentation, Smart Adjuncts, and the Visible Layer

The previous chapter dealt with the first engine of Pillar A — exercise. This chapter covers the other two engines exercise needs to actually work: alimentation, which is fuel and information at the same time, and smart adjuncts, which correct what the lab shows is missing. And it closes with a layer that preventive medicine almost always ignores — the surface of the body, skin, hair, and nails — because it is where patients suffer most and where the market exploits most.

---

### SECTION 1 — Alimentation as Metabolic Strategy

### Food Isn't Fuel. It's Information.

The first thing I need to say about alimentation in this context is what it is not. It is not a diet. It is not caloric restriction as a goal. It is not fashion. It is a tool — probably the most powerful tool you handle every day without realizing it.

Each meal sends biochemical signals to the body. The composition of the plate — the proportion of protein, the type of carbohydrate, the quality of fat, the presence of fiber, the density of micronutrients — instructs genes, modulates hormones, nourishes or starves the microbiome. The insulin response to a meal heavy in refined starch at ten at night is radically different from the response to the same meal at seven in the morning. The body doesn't receive calories. It receives instructions.

When I reviewed Marcos's eating in that visit, I didn't hand him a printed diet with a Monday-to-Sunday menu. I handed him principles — the same ones I'll lay out here — and a transition plan adapted to his routine. The difference between principle and diet is that the principle survives the restaurant, the business trip, and the holidays. The diet does not.

### The Pattern with the Most Evidence: The Mediterranean Diet

If there is one eating pattern that has survived decades of scrutiny, it is the Mediterranean diet. It isn't the only possible healthy pattern — but it is the most studied, the most replicated in clinical trials, and the most consistent in results.

**PREDIMED** — the largest randomized trial ever conducted on diet and cardiovascular prevention (7,447 high-risk people in Spain, nearly five years) — compared the Mediterranean diet with extra-virgin olive oil, the Mediterranean diet with nuts, and a low-fat control. The two Mediterranean groups had a roughly 30% reduction in major cardiovascular events; in the *per-protocol* analysis, the reduction exceeded 50%. Republished in the *NEJM* in 2018 after methodological correction, with results essentially unchanged.

PREDIMED isn't the only argument. Data from the Blue Zones — the regions with the highest concentrations of centenarians, studied by Dan Buettner — converge in the same direction. Sardinia, Okinawa, Nicoya, Ikaria, Loma Linda: different continents, different cultures, but a notably similar dietary pattern. Abundance of vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts. Moderate protein, predominantly from fish and legumes. Ultra-processed foods nonexistent.

The Mediterranean pattern isn't a list of permitted and forbidden foods. It's a set of principles: a vegetable base, quality fat, adequate protein, minimally processed foods, and conviviality at the table. It can be adapted to any culture — including the Brazilian one, where rice and beans (a Latin American staple), pumpkin, okra, fresh fish, and olive oil already form an excellent base.

### The Protein Debate: How Much Is Enough?

Protein is the most-debated macronutrient in the longevity literature, and the debate deserves context — because both sides have evidence and the right answer depends on who is sitting in front of you.

Peter Attia, in *Outlive*, argues for an intake in the range of 1.6 to 2.2 g/kg/day for adults over 40. The argument is direct: sarcopenia — the progressive loss of muscle mass and strength we detailed in Chapter 7 — is one of the largest predictors of incapacity and mortality in aging. Without adequate protein and mechanical stimulus, the body loses the infrastructure that supports it. Sufficient protein, distributed across three to four meals each crossing the threshold of anabolic stimulation, is the substrate the muscle needs to repair and grow.

Valter Longo, on the other hand, defends periods of protein restriction — especially of animal protein — arguing that chronically elevated IGF-1 levels, stimulated by high protein intake, activate cellular growth pathways (mTOR) that favor accelerated aging and tumor proliferation. Epidemiological studies suggest that in middle-aged adults, very high protein intake is associated with greater cancer mortality — but that association reverses after age 65, when protein becomes protective.

The position I take is pragmatic: clinical context defines the dose. A 55-year-old patient with documented sarcopenia needs more protein, not less. A 45-year-old patient with persistently elevated IGF-1 and a family history of colorectal cancer may benefit from a different approach. Protocol without context is a generic recipe — and a generic recipe isn't precision medicine.

For most patients in the 40 to 60 range, the practical target sits between 1.2 and 1.6 g/kg/day as a starting point, adjusted by body composition, activity level, and biomarkers. What matters less is the exact number and more is consistency: quality protein at every meal, especially at breakfast — the meal at which most patients consume almost none. Marcos had coffee with a *pão de queijo* (Brazilian cheese bread). No real protein until lunch. Twelve hours between dinner and the first meal with amino acids. In a 57-year-old man who needed to preserve — and ideally gain — muscle mass, that was a silent and correctable problem.

The point that deserves attention, and that the "how much in total" debate often obscures, is *per meal*. Muscle protein synthesis is a trigger, not a continuous tap — each meal needs to cross a minimum threshold to drive repair. The central signal is **leucine**: in young adults, about 2 to 2.5 g of leucine per meal activates the mTOR pathway; over 50, the threshold rises to ~3 g (the so-called **anabolic resistance**). In total protein: 20–25 g per meal is enough at 30; at 55, the target is 30–40 g.

For scale: 5 ounces of chicken delivers ~30 g of protein; three eggs, ~20 g; 7 ounces of plain Greek yogurt, 18–22 g; 5 ounces of salmon, ~32 g; one cup of cooked lentils, 18 g (with less leucine density). Below the threshold, amino acids circulate, get oxidized, and little synthesis happens. Diluting the same total protein across six 10-gram snacks doesn't produce the same result as concentrating it in three meals above threshold — Mamerow et al. (*J Nutr* 2014) showed 25% more 24-hour muscle synthesis with even three-meal distribution versus the same total concentrated at dinner.

The typical Brazilian breakfast — bread, cheese, sweetened coffee, sometimes a piece of fruit — is the perfect example of the problem: it rarely crosses the threshold, and opens the day in a state of zero anabolic stimulation. The U.S. counterpart — bagel and coffee, or cereal with milk — has the same issue. In Marcos's case, designing a breakfast with three eggs or a large Greek yogurt plus an additional protein portion solved, by itself, much of the twelve-hour gap of zero stimulation that had been corroding his muscle mass for decades. In many real routines, however, eggs and yogurt don't fit into daily life — and that is where a specific protein-powder supplement solves the problem. I deal with that in the next section.

### What to Prioritize — and What to Eliminate

Detailing food by food would be reductionist and would fall into the nutritionism trap this book wants to avoid. But there are principles that the evidence supports consistently:

**Prioritize:** vegetables and legumes (fiber is the most effective prebiotic that exists — it feeds butyrate-producing bacteria that reduce intestinal inflammation and improve insulin sensitivity; most adults consume less than 15 g/day, the target is 30 to 40 g); extra-virgin olive oil; nuts; oily fish rich in EPA and DHA; whole fruit; whole grains in moderation; polyphenols (berries, cacao, green tea, turmeric — modulating cellular protection pathways and positively influencing the microbiome).

**Eliminate or minimize:** ultra-processed foods (the evidence is unequivocal — associated with greater risk of obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer, and all-cause mortality, through mechanisms beyond calories, including microbiome alteration and promotion of systemic inflammation); excess added fructose (metabolized almost exclusively by the liver, fueling the *de novo* lipogenesis we described in Chapter 6 as the engine of steatosis — fructose from whole fruit, accompanied by fiber, is another story); vegetable oils subjected to high temperatures and industrial trans fats.

### Time-Restricted Eating: A Brief Note

In Chapter 6, we saw how Fernanda's eating window contributed to her metabolic reversal. The principle is simple: concentrate food intake in the active phase of the day, avoiding eating in the last 2 to 3 hours before bed. Insulin sensitivity peaks in the morning and falls through the day — eating late is delivering glucose to the body at the moment it is least prepared to process it.

The evidence for time-restricted eating is solid and growing: improved glucose tolerance, reduced triglycerides and inflammatory markers, even without calorie reduction. Chrono-eating will be addressed in depth in Chapter 14, when we discuss circadian rhythm. For now, the principle: *when* you eat matters as much as *what* you eat. In Marcos's case, who routinely had dinner at ten at night, that single adjustment — moving the last meal to before eight — already changed the metabolic environment in which his body spent the night.

### SECTION 2 — Evidence-Based Smart Adjuncts

Supplementation is the territory in which most money is wasted in health. The market is enormous, lightly regulated, and driven much more by marketing than by science. Most people who take supplements take wrong doses, wrong forms, or substances that simply don't have evidence of working.

What follows are the four supplements with evidence solid enough to justify routine use in most patients focused on longevity. There are others with specific indication by goal — cognition, immunity, athletic recovery, sleep — that deserve in-depth treatment and that, by scope, don't fit in this chapter.

### Vitamin D

In Chapter 4, we established that the optimal range of 25-OH vitamin D is 40 to 60 ng/mL — well above the 20 ng/mL the lab calls sufficient. Deficiency is epidemic: most patients I see for the first time are below 30. Marcos was at 28.

Supplementation is simple, safe, and inexpensive. The usual dose for adults is between 2,000 and 5,000 IU/day, adjusted to serum level. Always paired with vitamin K2 in the MK-7 form (100 to 200 mcg/day), which directs mobilized calcium toward the bones instead of letting it deposit in the arteries. An important caveat: patients on warfarin or another vitamin K-dependent anticoagulant must avoid K2 supplementation — or adjust it in coordination with the cardiologist — because vitamin K antagonizes the effect of these medications. Retest every 3 to 4 months until reaching the target range, then twice a year.

### Magnesium

Magnesium participates in more than 300 enzymatic reactions — ATP production, heart rhythm regulation, muscle contraction, sleep quality. Subclinical deficiency is extremely prevalent: more than half the population fails to meet the recommended intake. And serum magnesium — the test that appears on the blood panel — is misleading: only 1% of body magnesium is in serum. You can have a result within range and still be depleted at the cellular level.

Form matters. Magnesium oxide, the cheapest and best-selling form, has low bioavailability and a predominantly laxative effect. The forms with the best evidence:

**Magnesium glycinate (or bisglycinate):** excellent absorption, relaxing effect, well tolerated. First choice for sleep and anxiety.

**Magnesium L-threonate (Magtein):** the only form with preclinical evidence of crossing the blood-brain barrier and raising brain magnesium. Particularly interesting for cognition and neuroprotection.

*Dose:* 200 to 400 mg of elemental magnesium per day, preferably at night, 30 to 60 minutes before bed.

### Omega-3 (EPA + DHA)

The long-chain omega-3 fatty acids — EPA and DHA — are structural components of cell membranes and precursors of resolvins and protectins, molecules that actively resolve inflammation. They are the natural anti-inflammatory with the most robust evidence in supplementation.

Don't confuse them with ALA (alpha-linolenic acid), present in chia and flax seeds, whose conversion to EPA and DHA in the human body is below 5%. For clinical effect, you need to supplement EPA and DHA directly — or eat oily fish at least three times a week.

*Dose:* 1 to 2 g/day of combined EPA+DHA for general maintenance. For elevated triglycerides or significant inflammation, 3 to 4 g/day under medical guidance. Prefer sources with purity certification (IFOS 5-star or equivalent).

### Creatine

Creatine monohydrate is the most-studied supplement in the history of sports nutrition — and perhaps the most underestimated in the longevity context. Most people still associate it exclusively with muscle gain in young adults. The evidence goes further.

Creatine works as a fast energy reservoir: it stores high-energy phosphate that regenerates ATP when cellular demand rises. That happens in muscle — but also in the brain, where neurons have very high energy demand.

A meta-analysis published in *Frontiers in Nutrition* in 2024 by Xu and colleagues, analyzing 16 randomized clinical trials with 492 participants, found moderate evidence that creatine improves short-term memory in adults. The effect appears more pronounced in older adults and in vegetarians — populations with lower endogenous reserves.

For longevity, the benefits converge on three fronts: preservation of muscle mass (direct synergy with strength training against sarcopenia), cognitive support, and improved work capacity in training.

*Dose:* 3 to 5 g/day of creatine monohydrate. No need for a loading phase for chronic use. Any time of day, with or without food. Excellent safety profile in individuals with normal kidney function — no demonstrated renal adverse effects across decades of studies, contradicting the persistent myth.

### Protein Powder — Whey, Casein, and Plant Alternatives

For many years I treated protein powder as an athlete's tool, not a patient's. I revised that position after accumulating clinical evidence of how difficult it is, in a real Monday-to-Friday rhythm, to hit 30 grams of protein at breakfast using only food. Three eggs and yogurt works on paper — it doesn't work in the routine of an executive who leaves home at 6:40 a.m. and steps off the elevator drinking coffee from a paper cup. For that reality, a shake with 25 to 30 grams of protein powder mixed in water or milk is the most direct way to cross the leucine threshold I discussed in the Protein Debate — and the only one that tends to last more than three weeks on a real calendar.

The most-studied form is **whey protein** — the protein from milk whey, with an exceptional essential amino acid profile, high leucine content (~11%), and rapid absorption. A 25 g dose of whey isolate delivers about 2.8 g of leucine — enough to cross the threshold even in adults over 50. It is the first choice when there is no lactose intolerance. Concentrate (80% protein) is cheaper; isolate (90%+) has less lactose and is indicated for sensitive patients.

Those who don't tolerate dairy or have chosen not to consume it have reasonable plant alternatives. **Pea, rice, and soy protein blends** in the right proportion approach the essential amino acid profile of whey when the dose rises to 30–35 g; pure plant proteins from a single source tend to be suboptimal in leucine. Potato and corn-derived proteins have leucine comparable to animal sources, but limited commercial availability outside specialty channels.

*Practical dose:* 25 to 30 g per serving for men over 130 lbs (60 kg); 20 to 25 g for women. One serving per day is the use I see deliver the most results — typically at breakfast or post-workout, filling the meal that on its own would hardly cross the threshold. It doesn't replace food; it complements it. Always prefer products with purity certification (Informed Sport or equivalent, for those subject to anti-doping testing; heavy-metal certification for the general reader).

An honest note on the market: most products that brand themselves "for health and longevity," at prices two to three times higher, deliver essentially the same biochemical profile as conventional sports products, with marketing tuned to the 40+ buyer. Reading the label and comparing grams of protein and leucine per serving solves most of the game.

### Essential Micronutrients Frequently Suboptimal

Beyond the four pillars above, there is a set of micronutrients that are rarely measured and frequently outside the optimal range — even in people who eat "well."

**B-complex vitamins** — especially B12, B6, and folate — are direct cofactors in the methylation cycle and the production of neurotransmitters. B12 deficiency is common in vegetarians, vegans, people who chronically take metformin or proton pump inhibitors, and older adults. It doesn't always show up as anemia; it often appears as fatigue, peripheral neuropathy, cognitive complaints, or elevated homocysteine. The ideal level is above 600 pg/mL — below that, even if the lab says "normal," there is already functional compromise. Correction is simple: methylcobalamin 1 mg/day orally or sublingually. B6 (pyridoxal-5-phosphate) is a cofactor in homocysteine metabolism and neurotransmitter synthesis; methylated folate (5-MTHF) is essential for those with MTHFR polymorphisms — as we saw in André's case.

**Iron and ferritin.** This is the most underdiagnosed nutritional gap in clinical practice, especially in women. Labs typically consider ferritin normal above 15 ng/mL — but that threshold was calibrated to avoid frank anemia, not to sustain optimal function. For energy, exercise capacity, cognitive preservation, and hair health, the target is **≥ 50 ng/mL in adult women** and a similar range in men, with special attention to overload (if ferritin > 300 with elevated transferrin saturation, screen for hemochromatosis). Menstruating women, endurance athletes, vegetarians, post-bariatric patients, and frequent blood donors are the highest-risk groups. Correction can be dietary (lean red meat, legumes with vitamin C, organ meats in moderation), oral with iron salts (sulfate, bisglycinate, chelated; take on an empty stomach or with vitamin C, avoid with coffee or calcium), or, in selected cases of slow response or poor GI tolerance, intravenous — a decision that warrants individualized consultation.

**Zinc** is a cofactor in more than three hundred enzymes and essential for immune function, wound healing, testosterone production, and neurotransmission. Relative deficiency is common in vegetarians, athletes who sweat heavily, people on prolonged proton pump inhibitor use, and people with excess phytates in their diet. Usual dose: 15 to 30 mg/day, preferably in chelated form (zinc bisglycinate).

**Iodine.** Underreported despite salt iodization, especially in those who use Himalayan pink salt, non-iodized sea salt, or who chronically restrict salt. Iodine is essential for thyroid function and cognition. Correction is simple when there is confirmed deficiency, but the safe window is narrow — excess also causes thyroid problems. Order 24-hour urinary iodine or spot urinary iodine when there is suspicion.

**Selenium** is a mineral with a narrow window — neither too low nor too high. Essential for thyroid function (cofactor of the deiodinase that converts T4 to T3), for glutathione peroxidase (one of the body's main antioxidant systems), and for immunity. Across various regions of Brazil and parts of the U.S. (especially the Pacific Northwest and Southeast), selenium-poor soils make deficiency relatively common. Usual dose: 100 to 200 mcg/day, for at most twelve months without reassessment — chronic excessive doses can increase diabetes risk.

**Coenzyme Q10 (ubiquinol)** is central in the mitochondrial respiratory chain and is frequently supplemented in patients on statins (which reduce endogenous synthesis) and in patients with documented or suspected mitochondrial dysfunction. Evidence for routine use in healthy populations is modest; for selected populations — especially over 50 with fatigue and on a statin — it's worth considering. Dose: 100 to 200 mg/day in ubiquinol form, with fat.

I don't prescribe any of these automatically. I measure, check where the patient is relative to the optimal range, and replace what needs to be replaced — in the right form, the right dose, for the right duration. That is the logic I detail just below.

### The Longevity Frontier — with Skepticism

It's worth being direct about the layer that has generated the most market noise in recent years. Resveratrol, taurine, nicotinamide riboside (NR), nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), spermidine, and urolithin A have been presented with enthusiasm in the longevity market, often as "the next generation of preventive supplementation." The evidence in animal models is promising; the hard human evidence — event reduction, life extension, measurable functional recovery — is still weak or nonexistent. It isn't that they are useless. It is that marketing is substantially ahead of the science. For the reader who wants to track that frontier, the orientation is simple: read with skepticism, prefer compounds with human clinical trials published in peer-reviewed journals, and treat them as conscious experimentation — not as a pillar of preventive strategy.

### What Doesn't Work

Hydrolyzed collagen for skin rejuvenation has weak evidence and a biologically implausible mechanism at the doses sold — I discuss it in the skin block below, where it fits in detail. Generic multivitamins provide suboptimal doses of almost everything and excessive doses of what isn't lacking. High-dose vitamin C does not prevent flu or strengthen immunity beyond what adequate nutrition already does. High-dose biotin interferes with lab tests — including troponin and TSH — without demonstrated benefit for hair and nails in the absence of real deficiency; I'll come back to this point in the hair section.

### How I Prescribe in Practice

The logic I apply to supplementation is the same one that runs through this entire book: I don't wait for the problem to settle in to act. The same way I don't wait for glucose to cross 126 mg/dL to treat insulin resistance, I don't wait for vitamin D to drop below 20 to replace it. The goal is to bring each marker to the optimal range — the one science associates with the lowest risk and the highest functionality over decades.

The process is direct: I order the labs, identify where the patient is relative to the optimal ranges discussed in Chapter 4, and design an individualized protocol — with dose, form, and timing defined for that person, at that moment. A patient with vitamin D of 28, depleted intracellular magnesium, an omega-3 *index* below 4%, and ferritin of 25 receives a completely different formula from another with vitamin D of 55 and ferritin of 180. It sounds obvious — but it is the opposite of what happens when someone walks into a pharmacy and buys the same multivitamin everyone else does.

Then, reassessment. Depending on the patient and the clinical moment, I review at one, two, three, or six months. A patient initiating correction of severe vitamin D deficiency needs a recheck at eight weeks. A stable patient with optimal ranges achieved can be reassessed semiannually. Each cycle, I look at the numbers again, adjust doses, remove what has hit target, add what has emerged. It is an iterative process, not a static prescription. It is precision medicine applied to supplementation.

And there is a practical problem worth resolving from the start: the pile of bottles. Vitamin D in one, magnesium in another, omega-3 in another, plus two or three someone else recommended. Eight capsules a day. Three weeks of adherence. What I do is consolidate as much as possible: I use compounded formulas to combine what can be combined, in the exact dose and correct form, reducing the number of capsules to the smallest viable amount for that patient. Not everything fits in one vehicle — omega-3 in a softgel doesn't blend with mineral powders, for example — but the goal is always the same: make the protocol as compact and practical as possible for each person's real routine. An executive who travels every week needs a different solution from someone who has breakfast at home every day. Personalization isn't only biochemical — it's logistical.

### SECTION 3 — The Visible Layer: Skin, Hair, and Nails

Skin, hair, and nails are the part of the body the patient sees every day — and, for that reason, where the cosmetic longevity market most exploits. Serious preventive medicine has little to say about rejuvenation promises and a lot to say about a few simple interventions with real evidence. This section separates the two.

### Skin: what has evidence, what is cosmetic, what is noise

**Daily sunscreen.** It is the only dermatologic intervention with a hard outcome. An Australian trial followed more than 1,600 adults for 4.5 years and showed that daily sunscreen use reduced melanoma incidence by about 50% and visible photoaging by about 24%, compared with discretionary use. It is the simplest, cheapest, and most effective measure an adult can take for their own skin. SPF 30 minimum; SPF 50 or higher for fair skin, prolonged exposure, or anyone with a history of premalignant lesions. Apply in the morning, reapply every two to three hours during sun exposure, and on every day — including cloudy ones, including indoors near a window. If I had to choose a single dermatologic intervention for any adult after 35, it would be this one.

**Topical retinoids.** The second class with robust evidence. Tretinoin (retinoic acid), retinol in stabilized formulations, and adapalene (originally approved for acne and now also used in photoaging) reduce fine wrinkles, improve texture, lighten dark spots, stimulate dermal collagen, and partially reverse signs of photodamage — documented in medium- and long-term randomized trials. Tretinoin is the gold standard and requires a prescription; retinol is over-the-counter, with a softer effect, dose-dependent on concentration; adapalene has less irritation and is a good alternative for sensitive skin. Apply at night, starting on alternate nights until tolerance stabilizes. Significant results require continuous use for at least three to six months.

**Topical vitamin C and niacinamide.** Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) at a 10 to 20% concentration, in a stable formulation, reduces hyperpigmentation, improves tone uniformity, and shows antioxidant signal against UV-induced cutaneous oxidative damage. Niacinamide (topical form of vitamin B3) at 4–5% reduces redness, improves skin barrier, and is particularly useful in sensitive skin or mild rosacea. These are cosmetic interventions with an excellent safety profile and modest but consistent evidence.

**Botulinum toxin, fillers, biostimulators, and lasers.** Botulinum toxin (Botox, Dysport, Xeomin) on dynamic wrinkles has predictable, reversible, and safe effect when applied by a qualified physician. Reversible hyaluronic acid fillers have a place in specific volume correction — and real risk when used in excess, in critical vascular areas, or by untrained hands (arterial occlusion, necrosis, retinal embolism). Injectable biostimulators (poly-L-lactic acid, calcium hydroxylapatite) stimulate endogenous collagen with modest evidence. Fractional lasers and pulsed light have reasonable trials in photoaging, dark spots, and texture. None of these interventions, on their own, modifies a health trajectory — they are cosmetic. With restraint and a trained professional, they are acceptable; in excess, they distort facial architecture. If you are going to enter that territory, do it with judgment, with a board-certified dermatologist or plastic surgeon — and never as a substitute for sunscreen and retinoid, the two gestures with real biological effect.

### Hair: androgenetic alopecia and the position on post-finasteride syndrome

Androgenetic alopecia — male-pattern baldness and female diffuse thinning — has a genetic and hormonal basis, with dihydrotestosterone (DHT) as the final mediator at sensitive follicles. It is the most common cause of hair loss in adults, and also the complaint where the market lies the most.

**Clinical position on finasteride and dutasteride for cosmetic alopecia — and on post-finasteride syndrome (PFS).** Finasteride and dutasteride are 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors, the enzyme that converts testosterone to DHT. I prescribe both for symptomatic benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), where there are decades of efficacy data and the indication is medically consistent. **I do not prescribe finasteride or dutasteride for cosmetic alopecia.** The reason has a clear regulatory and clinical basis. The FDA added, in 2011, a warning about depression risk and, in 2022, formally updated the label to alert about suicidal ideation. The EMA (European agency) recognized in 2025 post-finasteride syndrome (PFS) as a real condition — characterized by persistent sexual dysfunction (reduced libido, erectile dysfunction, anorgasmia), neuropsychiatric symptoms (depression, anxiety, cognitive blunting, anhedonia), and physical symptoms that can persist for months to years even after the medication is stopped. The absolute prevalence is low, but the effect in those who develop it is severe, can be irreversible, and the decision to accept that risk in exchange for a strictly cosmetic outcome is, at minimum, debatable. For BPH — where the indication is functional and symptomatic — the calculus changes. For hair — where the complaint is aesthetic — it doesn't seem prudent, and that is the position I offer to patients who ask.

**What I prescribe for androgenetic alopecia.** The alternatives with reasonable evidence and a more favorable risk profile are:

- **Topical minoxidil** (2% or 5%, solution or foam). Vasodilator mechanism that prolongs the anagen phase of the follicle. Decades of evidence. Applied twice a day to a dry scalp. Visible results in 4 to 6 months. When stopped, the effect reverses over months.
- **Low-dose oral minoxidil** (0.25 to 2.5 mg/day in women; 1.25 to 5 mg/day in men, depending on clinical context). Off-label use with growing evidence, particularly useful in patients with topical intolerance, insufficient efficacy, or where applying it daily isn't feasible. Monitoring of blood pressure and signs of fluid retention is required; contraindicated in heart failure and hypotension.
- **Hair PRP** (platelet-rich plasma). Reasonable-quality randomized trials show modest benefit in hair density. Three to four sessions in the first year, then maintenance. Depends on the technique and platelet concentration of the center performing it.
- **Microneedling** alone or combined with minoxidil/PRP, with signal in small trials.
- **Correction of ferritin to ≥ 40 ng/mL.** Low ferritin is a common reversible cause of hair loss, especially in women, and frequently coexists with androgenetic alopecia, amplifying the problem. Ordering ferritin before any treatment for hair loss is the technical minimum. Section 2 of this chapter covers correction of iron deficiency.
- **Correction of iron, vitamin D, zinc, B12, and thyroid function.** Any one of these deficiencies, on its own, can worsen hair loss. Treating them changes the game in a sizable share of patients.

**A final observation about the market.** Clinics offering "hormonal hair transplant" that pair finasteride with the surgical procedure rarely discuss PFS with the patient before consent. If you are considering treatment for hair loss in any format, the question you have a right to ask is: *"what is this clinic's position on post-finasteride syndrome, and what is the plan to monitor neuropsychiatric and sexual effects if I develop symptoms?"* If the answer is evasive, the clinic is not the place.

### Nails: what they say and don't say about the whole body

Nails are a surface mirror of systemic health. Iron deficiency causes koilonychia (spoon nails); biotin deficiency (rare) produces brittleness; hypothyroidism shows up as brittle nails; psoriasis can affect nails before skin; peripheral vascular changes appear as splinter hemorrhages. A healthy nail typically corresponds to a well-nourished body.

**Alert about high-dose biotin.** Biotin is sold as a "hair, skin, and nails" supplement in capsules of 5,000 to 10,000 mcg (the actual need is about 30 mcg/day). The clinical problem: those very high doses interfere with multiple immunoassay-based lab tests, including **cardiac troponin** (potentially masking an evolving heart attack), **TSH and thyroid hormones** (mimicking or masking thyroid disease), **PTH**, **25-hydroxyvitamin D**, **testosterone**, and other relevant markers. The FDA issued a formal alert about this risk. If a patient uses high-dose biotin and needs labs, stopping at least 72 hours in advance is the minimum. And before that, the question: why high-dose biotin at all? The evidence that it improves hair or nails in people without documented deficiency is essentially nonexistent. It is a supplement I prescribe rarely, almost never, and with explicit warning about lab interference.

Skin, hair, and nails well cared for are a reflection — not a substitute — of the work the other pillars of this book do on the inside. Daily sunscreen, retinoid at night, correction of nutritional deficiencies identified by lab work, and rejection of what has no evidence solve most of what can be solved without fooling anyone.

### The Three Engines Together

Eight months after that visit — eight months after the CAC of 412 and the plan we built that day — Marcos returned for full reassessment.

His eating followed a Mediterranean pattern with a 10-hour eating window. Training had been structured across the four pillars: Zone 2 twice a week on the cycle ergometer, strength training three times with a coach guided by the clinical context, HIIT once a week from the second month on, and mobility integrated into the warm-up. His vitamin D had risen from 28 to 52 ng/mL. Magnesium L-threonate at night improved a sleep he didn't know was bad — we found out at the first visit that he averaged five and a half hours and woke up twice during the night.

The numbers told the story. Insulin from 11 to 6. CRP from 1.6 to 0.6. ApoB at 58 — well below target. TG/HDL ratio normalized. In body composition, 11 fewer pounds of fat, 4.5 more pounds of lean mass. And the exercise stress test showed a 1.8 MET gain over the test from eight months earlier. Translating into the data from the previous chapter: **1.8 MET equals something between 25% and 30% additional reduction in death risk — a gain no pharmacological adjustment alone would have produced.**

### The Essential in 60 Seconds

- Alimentation isn't a diet — it is a metabolic tool. The pattern with the most evidence is the Mediterranean diet, validated by PREDIMED (~30% reduction in cardiovascular events) and convergent with the Blue Zones data. Eliminating ultra-processed foods and aligning meal timing with circadian rhythm matter as much as what is on the plate.

- Smart adjuncts with solid evidence: vitamin D (target 40–60 ng/mL) with K2, magnesium glycinate or L-threonate (200–400 mg/day), omega-3 EPA+DHA (1–2 g/day), creatine monohydrate (3–5 g/day). Everything else requires individual justification — guided by biomarkers, not by hype.

- **Iron and ferritin** form the most underdiagnosed nutritional gap, especially in women. Target ≥ 50 ng/mL for optimal function (the lab usually says "normal" from 15 onward). Screen vegetarians, athletes, post-bariatric patients; in men, the inverse attention to overload.

- The popular longevity frontier (resveratrol, taurine, NR, NMN, spermidine, urolithin A) has market enthusiasm well ahead of human evidence. Track it with skepticism — these are not pillars of preventive strategy.

- The three engines of Pillar A — activity, alimentation, smart adjuncts — work in synergy. None replaces the others. Together, they transform trajectories that medication alone cannot change.
