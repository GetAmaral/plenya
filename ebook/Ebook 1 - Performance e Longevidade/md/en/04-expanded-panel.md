# PART II — THE MAP

## Chapter 4 — The Expanded Panel: The Map the Standard Checkup Doesn't Deliver

Fernanda was 41 when she came to my office at her husband's insistence. She had no complaints. No history of any chronic disease. She exercised three times a week, ate "well by typical middle-class standards," and slept reasonably. Her most recent checkup, six months earlier, had come back "all normal." Her husband, who was already my patient, had a specific concern: Fernanda's father had a heart attack at 58, and her mother had been diagnosed with type 2 diabetes at 62.

*"She says everything is fine because the test said everything is fine,"* he told me. *"But I've already seen what 'fine' meant in Ricardo's case."*

I ordered the panel I use for any patient who wants to understand what's actually going on inside — not the standard checkup, but the expanded panel that includes the biomarkers that really matter for longevity.

Fernanda's results:

- Fasting glucose: 94 mg/dL. Normal.
- HbA1c: 5.5%. Normal.
- Total cholesterol: 201 mg/dL. Normal.
- LDL: 119 mg/dL. Normal.

Up to here, the previous doctor was right — all within range. But the story changed when I looked at the tests that hadn't been ordered:

- Fasting insulin: 13 µIU/mL. The lab reference goes up to 25.
- HOMA-IR: 3.0. No alarm in the report.
- ApoB: 108 mg/dL. Within reference.
- hs-CRP: 1.9 mg/L. Below the "high risk" cutoff.
- Homocysteine: 12.4 µmol/L. Normal.
- Vitamin D: 22 ng/mL. "Sufficient" by lab standards.
- Triglycerides: 180 mg/dL. HDL: 46 mg/dL. TG/HDL ratio: 3.9 — above the 3.5 threshold.

All normal. All within range. And all telling a story no one was listening to.

Fernanda's insulin — 13 µIU/mL — was more than 60% above what we consider optimal for longevity. It indicated insulin resistance forming, at least three to five years before any change in glucose. The ApoB of 108 meant an excessive number of atherogenic particles penetrating her arteries, despite "normal" cholesterol. The CRP of 1.9 revealed chronic low-grade inflammation — not enough for a diagnosis, enough to feed atherosclerosis and neurodegeneration over decades. The vitamin D of 22, although "sufficient" by the lab, was well below the range associated with optimal immune and muscular function.

Fernanda had no disease. But she was heading toward at least two — exactly like her father and her mother — and no one would have noticed for another five or six years. Because no one had ordered the right tests. And no one had interpreted the results against the right ranges.

### Normal vs. Optimal: The Difference the Lab Doesn't Show

This is perhaps the most important concept in this book, and it's worth repeating because it changes everything in how you read your labs.

When the lab prints "reference range" next to your result, that interval was calculated based on the 2.5 to 97.5 percentile of a population sample. That means 95% of the people tested fall within that range. It sounds safe.

The problem is who is in that sample. It includes sedentary people. People with chronic inflammation. People with undiagnosed insulin resistance. People with silent fatty liver. People who will receive a diabetes diagnosis five years from now, but who today are still "within range."

The reference of normality is the average of a population that isn't optimized. It's where most people are — it's not where you want to be.

The **optimal range** is something else. It's derived from studies of centenarian populations, longitudinal longevity data, and evidence about the level at which each biomarker is associated with the lowest risk of disease and death. The difference between "normal" and "optimal" can look small on paper — but over decades, that difference translates into years of healthy life.

A concrete example: fasting insulin. The lab accepts as "normal" any value up to 25 µIU/mL. That means someone with insulin of 22 — practically in frank insulin resistance — gets the "normal" stamp and goes home reassured. In the optimal range for longevity, the limit is 8. **The difference between 8 and 25 is not gradation — it's a chasm.**

### The Biomarkers That Really Matter

Below is the panel I use in clinic — the biomarkers that, together, draw the most complete map of what is happening inside your body. For each, I show the range the lab considers "normal" and the range longevity science considers "optimal."

**HbA1c (hemoglobin A1c)**
*It is the percentage of hemoglobin — the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen — that has a glucose molecule stuck to it. Because red blood cells live about 2 to 3 months, HbA1c reflects average blood sugar over that period — not a point-in-time snapshot like fasting glucose. That's why it's the most useful marker to see the real metabolic trend.*
Lab normal: < 6.5%. Optimal range: ≤ 5.4% (ideal 4.8–5.2%). What it reveals: long-term metabolic health. Studies of centenarians consistently show values below 5.2%. The difference between 5.5% and 5.0% looks minimal — over decades, it isn't.

**ApoB (apolipoprotein B)**
Lab normal: < 130 mg/dL. Optimal range: < 90 mg/dL (ideal < 80). What it reveals: real atherosclerotic risk. Each ApoB particle is a "truck" that can penetrate the arterial wall and start a plaque. ApoB counts the number of trucks — LDL only measures the cargo they carry. Two patients with the same LDL can have radically different particle counts — and it's the particle count that determines risk. The 2024 National Lipid Association consensus recognized ApoB as a marker superior to LDL for cardiovascular risk assessment. European guidelines (ESC/EAS 2021) include ApoB as a therapeutic target. Despite this, most standard checkups — in Brazil, in the U.S., and elsewhere — don't include it.

**ApoB/ApoA1 ratio**
Optimal range: < 0.6 (low risk). Above 0.9: high risk. What it reveals: the balance between arterial attack and defense. ApoB counts the particles that deposit cholesterol in arteries. ApoA1 counts those that remove it (reverse cholesterol transport). The ratio captures a risk spectrum that neither marker alone detects — especially in patients with acceptable ApoB but low ApoA1, a hidden vulnerability. The AMORIS study, with 137,100 people followed for nearly 18 years, demonstrated that an elevated ApoB/ApoA1 ratio was already visible up to 20 years before the first cardiovascular event.

**hs-CRP (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein)**
Lab normal: < 3.0 mg/L. Optimal range: < 1.0 mg/L. What it reveals: low-grade systemic inflammation — the inflammaging we discussed in Chapter 3. Above 1.0, the arteries are in a chronic inflammatory state that, sustained for decades, fuels atherosclerosis, neurodegeneration, and cancer. The distance between "normal" (up to 3.0) and "optimal" (below 1.0) is enormous — and most physicians only worry once it crosses 3.0.

**Homocysteine**
Lab normal: < 15 µmol/L. Optimal range: < 10 µmol/L. What it reveals: vascular and cognitive risk. The Smith and Refsum review (2021) identified more than 100 diseases associated with elevated homocysteine and demonstrated documented prevention in 5 conditions. It is a marker that connects cardiovascular, brain, and kidney health in a single number. Frequently elevated due to deficiency of vitamins B6, B9 (folate), and B12 — something simple to correct when detected.

**Fasting insulin**
Lab normal: < 25 µIU/mL. Optimal range: < 8 µIU/mL. What it reveals: early insulin resistance. It is the first sign of metabolic derailment — it rises 5 to 10 years before glucose changes. By the time glucose finally rises, the pancreas is already exhausted. If no one orders fasting insulin, no one sees the problem coming. Ricardo had 14. Fernanda had 13. Both "normal" by lab standards. Both well above the ideal. This is, in my clinical experience, the single most underordered test in preventive medicine.

When fasting insulin is *borderline* — between 6 and 10 µIU/mL — and HOMA-IR looks normal, there is a tool that goes further: the OGTT with insulin curve (insulin at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes). While fasting insulin is a static photograph, the curve is the film of pancreatic response to a glucose load. Insulin resistance is, initially, a postprandial disorder — by the time it shows up in the fasting state, the process is already years old.

The classic work of Joseph Kraft, who analyzed more than 14,000 tests, demonstrated that 75% of people with normal glucose on the OGTT had abnormal insulin patterns — compensatory hyperinsulinemia that kept glucose low at the cost of excessive insulin production. Kraft called this "diabetes in-situ": the disease already present, with glucose still masked. From the OGTT, other indices can be derived with accuracy superior to HOMA-IR: the Matsuda index (which integrates glucose and insulin over the 120 minutes) and the TyG index (derived from triglycerides and fasting glucose), both validated against the euglycemic clamp. It's not a test for every patient — but when fasting raises suspicion without confirming, the insulin curve is what separates "probably normal" from "definitely not." In Chapter 6, we'll see this in practice.

**Triglycerides are how your body stores and transports fat.** Every time you eat more calories than you burn — especially refined carbs and fructose — the liver converts the surplus into triglycerides, which circulate in the blood before being deposited in adipose tissue. High triglycerides aren't "fat in the blood" in the colloquial sense: they are the most direct marker that metabolism is converting food into storage instead of burning it.

**Triglyceride/HDL ratio**
Optimal range: < 2.0. Above 3.5: probable insulin resistance. What it reveals: an accessible proxy for insulin resistance and for the small dense LDL particle pattern — the most atherogenic. You probably already have these two numbers on your last lipid panel. Divide one by the other. If the result is greater than 3.5, your metabolism is signaling dysfunction — even if each number, alone, is "within range." It requires no additional test: it's information already in your labs that almost no one calculates.

**Lp(a) — lipoprotein(a)**
Lab normal: varies. Optimal range: < 30 mg/dL (or < 75 nmol/L). What it reveals: genetic cardiovascular risk. Determined almost entirely by genetics, Lp(a) does not change with diet, exercise, or statins. About 20% of the population has elevated levels. It needs to be measured at least once in a lifetime — because if it's elevated, it completely changes risk stratification, the need for pharmacological treatment, and the urgency of controlling all the other modifiable factors.

**Vitamin D (25-OH)**
Lab normal: > 20 ng/mL. Optimal range: 40–60 ng/mL. What it reveals: immune, bone, and muscle function. The physiological rationale for the optimal range comes from its relationship with parathyroid hormone (PTH): when vitamin D is low, PTH rises to keep blood calcium stable — at the expense of bones. Studies show that PTH only reaches maximum suppression when 25(OH)D reaches 40 ng/mL or higher. Below that, the parathyroid glands are working harder than they should — even if the lab says "sufficient." The 40 to 60 ng/mL range is the point where PTH is suppressed, intestinal calcium absorption is optimal, and immune and muscular function are at the best documented level. Deficiency is epidemic — and "sufficiency" by lab standards (> 20 ng/mL) is well below that level.

**Ferritin**
Lab normal: 12–300 ng/mL. Optimal range: 40–150 ng/mL (context-dependent). What it reveals: iron stores and inflammation. Too low compromises energy and cognition. Too high may indicate iron overload or chronic inflammation. The lab "normal" range is absurdly wide — 12 to 300 covers almost any value.

**eGFR by cystatin C (estimated glomerular filtration rate)**
Lab normal: > 60 mL/min. Optimal range: > 90 mL/min. What it reveals: real kidney function. Cystatin C is more accurate than creatinine for estimating glomerular filtration, especially in muscular people or in those with high-protein diets. Creatinine depends on muscle mass — a patient with 95 kg of lean mass may have elevated creatinine without any kidney problem; a sarcopenic elderly woman may have deceptively low creatinine while her kidneys are already failing. Cystatin C is produced at a constant rate by all nucleated cells and doesn't suffer that interference. As a nephrologist, this is a biomarker I consider non-negotiable — the kidneys are silent until it is too late.

**Microalbuminuria (urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio in a spot sample)**
Lab normal: < 30 mg/g. Optimal range: < 10 mg/g. What it reveals: the first sign of glomerular injury — when the kidney's filter starts to leak protein that shouldn't pass. It is an extremely early marker of kidney disease, uncontrolled hypertension, and cardiovascular risk. Values between 10 and 30 mg/g already predict bad outcomes, even without meeting the formal "microalbuminuria" criterion. In patients with diabetes, hypertension, or metabolic syndrome, it is a test to order and follow over the years. Never ignore borderline values.

**Serum albumin**
Lab normal: 3.5–5.2 g/dL. Optimal range: ≥ 4.5 g/dL. What it reveals: nutritional status, hepatic function, and vascular integrity. Albumin persistently below 4.0 g/dL in a person without acute illness is a sign of protein malnutrition, chronic inflammation, or loss (renal or intestinal). Low albumin is an independent predictor of mortality in multiple populations, even when the other markers look fine. It's a simple test that appears on any lipid panel or hepatic routine, almost always ignored.

**Non-HDL cholesterol**
Optimal range: < 100 mg/dL. Above 130: caution; above 160: risk. What it reveals: the entire atherogenic fraction of cholesterol in a single number. Subtract HDL from total cholesterol — what remains is non-HDL, and that value includes LDL, VLDL, IDL, and chylomicron remnants. It's a particularly useful marker for patients with elevated triglycerides, where the Friedewald LDL calculation loses precision. It's already on your lipid panel — just do the subtraction.

**High-sensitivity troponin I**
Lab normal: varies by assay (hs-cTnI Abbott: < 16 ng/L women / < 34 ng/L men; hs-cTnT Roche: < 14 ng/L). Preventive optimal range: < 6 ng/L on any assay. What it reveals: subclinical myocardial injury. Troponin is traditionally associated with the diagnosis of acute heart attack, but modern high-sensitivity assays detect values much lower than the heart attack cutoff. And research has shown something important: elevated values within the "normal" range predict future cardiovascular events. In population cohorts, hs-troponin in the upper normal range is associated with greater risk of heart attack, heart failure, and mortality over three to five years. It is a marker beginning to be incorporated into preventive risk assessment — and it makes a difference in patients who already have other signs of accumulated vascular damage.

**NT-proBNP**
Lab normal: depends on age. Optimal range: < 50 pg/mL (< 50 years), < 75 pg/mL (50–75 years). What it reveals: cardiac wall stress, volume overload, early ventricular dysfunction. BNP (B-type natriuretic peptide) is released by the heart when the chambers are under excessive pressure or volume. In patients with hypertension, obesity, sleep apnea, or incipient diastolic dysfunction, NT-proBNP rises years before symptoms of heart failure appear. As a preventive marker, it is underordered and highly informative — especially in patients with multiple cardiometabolic risk factors.

**A note on adiponectin, leptin, and other longevity-media markers.**
Despite the appealing physiology (adiponectin as a "healthy fat" marker, leptin as a satiety signal), neither passes the three criteria I use to validate a routine biomarker: incomplete standardization across labs, problematically context-dependent interpretation (in older adults and in cardiac patients, *elevated* adiponectin predicts **worse** outcomes — the "adiponectin paradox"), and absence of clear clinical action from an abnormal result. They remain *research-forward* markers, outside the preventive panel. If they reach formal guidelines, they enter the panel.

### Two Markers Already on Your Labs — That No One Interprets

Beyond the biomarkers above, there are two that probably already appear on your routine labs — but that are interpreted only as "liver function" or ignored entirely. With the right ranges, they tell a different story.

**GGT (gamma-glutamyl transferase)**
Lab normal: < 60 U/L (men), < 40 U/L (women). Optimal range: < 25–30 U/L. What it reveals: much more than "liver function." GGT is a marker of oxidative stress, exposure to toxins, and early metabolic dysfunction. An Austrian study with nearly 164,000 participants followed for 12 years demonstrated that GGT values within the "normal" range were already associated, in a dose-dependent way, with greater cardiovascular mortality risk. It is also one of the first signs of fatty liver disease — the metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease that follows insulin resistance and that affects roughly a third of the world's adult population.

**Uric acid**
Lab normal: 3.5–7.2 mg/dL (men), 2.6–6.0 mg/dL (women). Optimal range: < 5.5 mg/dL. What it reveals: metabolic stress. Elevated levels are associated with hypertension, chronic kidney disease, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular risk. The mechanism includes inhibition of endothelial nitric oxide and activation of inflammatory pathways. A note of transparency: the association is robust, but the debate over direct causality is still ongoing. What is clear is that uric acid persistently above 6.0 mg/dL in patients without gout deserves attention — especially if accompanied by other signs of metabolic dysfunction.

### The Biomarker You Measure at Home: Grip Strength

Among all the markers that best predicted mortality in large international cohorts, one requires no blood, no lab, and costs less than a single office visit: hand grip strength, measured by a hand dynamometer.

The PURE study, published by Leong and colleagues in *The Lancet* in 2015, with 139,691 adults across 17 countries and a median follow-up of 4 years, demonstrated that every 5 kg reduction in grip strength is associated with a 16% increase in all-cause mortality (HR 1.16; 95% CI 1.13–1.20), 17% in cardiovascular mortality, 7% in heart attack, and 9% in stroke. In adjusted analyses, the predictive power of grip strength was superior to that of systolic blood pressure — a marker measured at every visit, while grip strength is measured at almost none. A systematic review with dose-response meta-analysis published in 2022 by López-Bueno and colleagues in *Ageing Research Reviews*, gathering 48 studies and more than 3.1 million participants, confirmed the pattern: the relationship is continuous and practically linear in the 26 to 50 kg range — every additional kilogram reduces risk.

The reason grip strength predicts so much isn't muscular. It's systemic. Grip strength is the final expression of several systems operating together: adequate muscle mass (sarcopenia decreases it), preserved neuromuscular function (neurodegeneration decreases it), sufficient nutritional status (protein malnutrition decreases it), inflammation control (chronic inflammation decreases it). A low number means one of these systems is yielding — even when none of the lab biomarkers has flagged it yet. It is, in practice, a thermometer of overall functional reserve.

The formal cutoffs from the European sarcopenia consensus (EWGSOP2, 2019) are **maximum grip below 27 kg in men and below 16 kg in women** — below these values, probable sarcopenia is an objective diagnosis. But these are the floor, not the target. Healthy adults between 40 and 60 generally reach 35–45 kg (women) and 45–60 kg (men), and that upper range is where we want to keep the person who plans to age on their feet. In my clinic, I integrated grip strength into periodic follow-up alongside body composition and blood pressure — five seconds per hand, three measurements, best result counted.

Standard Jamar hydraulic dynamometers are the office reference, but portable digital devices at the price of a single private visit allow home tracking. Measuring once per quarter and following the trend — exactly as we do with ApoB or HbA1c — turns a parameter almost no one measures into a functional compass of aging. And, unlike blood biomarkers, you watch the number change week by week when Pillar A of Chapter 7 enters the picture.

### The Test That Delivers Three Biomarkers in One: DEXA

Up to this point, the biomarkers we've discussed require blood or functional testing. But there is an imaging exam that, in a single 10- to 15-minute pass and with minimal radiation dose — between 1 and 5 microsieverts, equivalent to half a day to two days of natural background radiation — delivers three independent clinical biomarkers that blood doesn't show: dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, or DEXA. It is the exam that, in preventive longevity medicine, does for the body what the expanded panel does for metabolism.

The first piece of data is **appendicular muscle mass** — the weight of muscle in the arms and legs, divided by height squared, forming the Appendicular Skeletal Muscle Index (ASMI). This is the number that objectively defines whether sarcopenia is forming, with the European consensus cutoffs we'll detail in Chapter 7: above 7.0 kg/m² in men and 5.5 kg/m² in women. Bioelectrical impedance gives a useful estimate, but for a precise number — and for tracking the trajectory over years — DEXA is the gold standard. Every kilogram of lean mass lost between 40 and 65 is a kilogram less of glucose absorption capacity, fall resistance, and cognitive support in the second pillar of aging.

The second piece, perhaps the most undervalued in standard practice, is **visceral fat** (visceral adipose tissue, VAT). It is the fat that deposits around organs — liver, pancreas, gut, heart — and that behaves like an active endocrine organ, secreting inflammatory cytokines and flooding the liver with fatty acids through the portal vein. It is physiologically distinct from subcutaneous fat, which is relatively benign. No scale measures VAT. Waist circumference is a rough approximation — better than BMI but still confuses skin fat with deep fat. DEXA quantifies VAT in grams, with small error. In adults over 40, European population studies (Tromsø, 2015–2016) show medians of about 1,650 grams in men and 850 grams in women, with the 75th percentile around 2,340 g and 1,235 g respectively — and it's from that 75th percentile upward that cardiometabolic risk consistently rises. Absolute values vary by manufacturer and ethnicity, so the number to track is the **patient's percentile in the report**, not a fixed universal cutoff; what matters is that the report includes that percentile and that it falls over time. This is, in my clinical experience, the first number to move when a patient begins to reverse metabolic dysfunction — even before insulin drops.

The third piece is **bone mineral density**, measured at the lumbar spine and proximal femur, expressed as T-score (comparison to a healthy young adult) and Z-score (comparison to peers of the same age). By WHO criteria, T-score between −1.0 and −2.5 characterizes osteopenia; at or below −2.5, osteoporosis. In postmenopausal women and men over 60, this is the data point that determines whether a routine fall ends in a hip fracture — an event whose first-year mortality ranges between 15% and 30% in older adults, depending on cohort and prior clinical condition. But the preventive value doesn't begin at 60: knowing the T-score at 45, 50, 55 allows intervention with mechanical loading (Pillar A of Chapter 7), vitamin D, adequate protein, and — when indicated — bone pharmacology, while the window is still open.

Three exams in one, on a machine that exists in practically every mid-sized diagnostic clinic, often covered by health insurance from age 50 onward or when there is clinical indication. My recommendation: a complete DEXA (whole body + lumbar spine and femur densitometry) once every two or three years from age 40 — and annually after the first abnormal finding.

### The Body Speaks Too — Signs You Read in the Mirror

Biomarkers don't live only in the test tube. Some of the most valuable information about systemic health is in the mirror, on the toothbrush, and in the toilet — in signs the attentive patient observes every day and the trained physician interprets in seconds. They don't replace the lab panel. But they add to it, and often arrive earlier.

**The mouth is a biomarker.** Chronic periodontitis — that gum that bleeds when you brush, that persistent bad breath, that tooth that started to loosen — is much more than a dental problem. It's an active source of systemic inflammation. Cohort studies show that patients with moderate to severe periodontitis have higher hs-CRP, higher ApoB, and greater 10-year cardiovascular risk. The mechanism goes further: bacteria like *Porphyromonas gingivalis* have been identified in beta-amyloid plaques in the brains of Alzheimer's patients. Treating periodontitis with a periodontist — not just a general dentist — reduces CRP comparably to some anti-inflammatory medications. If your gums bleed regularly, your inflammatory panel is being fed every day by a source no blood test will treat.

**Skin, hair, and nails also speak.** Hair that breaks easily, nails with longitudinal grooves, and diffuse hair loss in women — once hormonal causes are ruled out — usually align with ferritin below 40 ng/mL or zinc deficiency. Dry, flaky skin points to low omega-3 or vitamin A. And there's a particularly underappreciated cutaneous sign: persistent acne in adult women often indicates subclinical insulin resistance, because hyperinsulinemia stimulates ovarian androgen production. These are clues — not diagnoses — that, added to the lab panel, help see the picture before it makes itself plain.

**Urine is the next exam.** Color, appearance, and foam are observable at no cost. Persistently foamy urine may indicate proteinuria — which crosses directly with the microalbuminuria we discussed earlier. Very dark urine outside dehydration suggests concentration of hepatic pigments. Cloudy urine deserves investigation. The basic urinalysis remains one of the most undervalued tests in preventive medicine — it costs little, detects early kidney problems, hidden infections, and metabolic changes that no other test will flag. As a nephrologist, it's a test I almost never skip.

Observing the body doesn't replace the panel. It adds information — what the mirror delivers for free.

### Patterns and Clusters: Read the Numbers Together

A common error — by both patients and physicians — is to look at each number in isolation. *"My insulin is a little high, but the rest is fine."* *"My ApoB is elevated, but my cholesterol is normal."* Biomarkers don't work that way. They tell a story together — and certain combinations reveal specific processes.

**Elevated fasting insulin + high hs-CRP + rising triglycerides** is a cluster that screams insulin resistance with inflammation — exactly the combination that feeds the four silent killers of Chapter 2. When I see insulin of 13, ApoB of 108, and CRP of 1.9 — as in Fernanda's case — I'm not looking at three separate problems. I'm looking at a single dysfunctional metabolic process manifesting in three different domains. The intervention isn't to treat each number individually — it's to attack the common root.

Another pattern I see often: **elevated homocysteine + low vitamin D + ferritin at the lower limit**. This cluster suggests nutritional deficiencies that compromise repair mechanisms — DNA methylation, immune function, energy production. The person isn't sick. But their protective systems are operating at reduced capacity. It's like driving with bald tires and worn brakes: it works, until the day it doesn't.

Ricardo, from Chapter 1, had exactly the first pattern: insulin 14, ApoB 118, CRP 2.4, homocysteine 13.8 — and, when I ordered Lp(a) for the first time after the heart attack, 160 nmol/L (above 75 is already considered high risk). Lp(a) had never been measured before. It was an invisible inherited risk that only appeared when he hit the parking lot. No single number triggered an isolated alarm. But the entire cluster — now with the genetic Lp(a) in the equation — told the story of a heart attack waiting to happen.

### Trends: The Film Matters More Than the Photograph

The second error is to look only at today's result, without comparing it to previous ones. A test is a photograph — a snapshot of a single moment. But what matters for longevity is the film: the trajectory of your numbers over the years.

The rising HbA1c curve we saw in Chapter 1 is the clearest example. Each isolated point is "normal." Together, they sketch a prediabetes arriving — announced for years, but invisible to anyone who looks at one test at a time.

That is why at Plenya, the team that follows each patient — physician, nutritionist, psychologist, and exercise specialist — reviews the key biomarkers every three to six months, not once a year. Not to generate anxiety — to detect trends before they become diagnoses.

Fernanda began to be followed at this level of attention. In six months, with adjustments to nutrition, the addition of strength training, and correction of vitamin D, her insulin dropped from 13 to 7, her CRP from 1.9 to 0.8, and her vitamin D rose to 48 ng/mL. Six years before any conventional diagnosis, she was already reversing the process. No medication. No drama. With the right information and early action.

### What If It Were You?

By this point in the book, you already know the question. Take your last checkup result and look not at what was written — but at what wasn't ordered. How many of the biomarkers in this chapter are there? In most cases, the answer is two or three. The others are missing. And those are precisely the ones that tell the most important story — the story that precedes the diagnosis by years or decades.

This chapter isn't a critique of your doctor. It's an invitation for you to demand more from your follow-up. Take this list to your next visit. Ask for the tests. Compare with the optimal ranges, not just the "normal" ones. The difference between the two can be measured in years of life.

### The Essential in 60 Seconds

- Lab reference ranges are based on the average of a population that includes sick and sedentary people. "Normal" doesn't mean optimal — it means you are average.

- Optimal ranges for longevity are derived from centenarian studies and longitudinal data: HbA1c ≤ 5.4%, ApoB < 90 mg/dL, hs-CRP < 1.0 mg/L, fasting insulin < 8 µIU/mL, homocysteine < 10 µmol/L.

- ApoB is superior to LDL for assessing cardiovascular risk. The ApoB/ApoA1 ratio goes further — capturing the balance between particles that attack and particles that protect arteries. Below 0.6 is the target.

- Fasting insulin is the dashboard's yellow light — it rises 5 to 10 years before glucose. The triglyceride/HDL ratio is the free proxy: above 3.5 indicates insulin resistance even with a "normal" lipid panel.

- Biomarkers should be read together (clusters) and over time (trends). The film matters more than the photograph — and this applies as much to a single marker as to the entire cluster.

- Lp(a) is genetic and needs to be measured at least once — if it's high, it changes the entire prevention strategy.

- Two markers you probably already have and don't know how to interpret: GGT (oxidative stress and early steatosis — optimal below 30 U/L) and uric acid (metabolic stress — optimal below 5.5 mg/dL).
