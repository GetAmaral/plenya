***Pillar T — Tending Mind, Body & Bonds***

## Chapter 12 — The Inner Work — What Only You Can Do

Ana was 44 when she came back for the sixth follow-up visit. An architect, a partner in a firm, married, two children aged 8 and 11. She had arrived eighteen months earlier determined to optimize everything that could be optimized. And she did.

From the first visit, the history had included stress, sleep, routine, and emotional life — as I do with every patient. Ana described an intense life, short sleep, background anxiety. But she returned each of those topics with the same sentence: *"I've always been like this, doctor. It's my personality. My father was the same."* When the patient naturalizes the psychological picture as a character trait, the door to targeted intervention closes — not from physician inattention, but from lack of a key. What you do in that scenario is what we did: register the point, open the parallel conversations (sleep hygiene, rhythm, basic regulation techniques), and keep working the pillars the patient is willing to address — waiting for some objective marker to open the door back up.

The biological markers responded. Vitamin D, from 22, climbed to 54. Ferritin, from 35, to 82. Fasting insulin dropped from 11 to 7. ApoB, from 102, to 78 with dietary adjustment. Homocysteine, after methylated B12 and folate, went from 12 to 8. She started strength training three times a week and Zone 2 twice more. She was in bed by 10:30 p.m. She eliminated ultra-processed foods.

But two numbers resisted. High-sensitivity CRP, which had started at 2.1, oscillated between 1.6 and 1.9. Morning cortisol, measured at the first visit at 21 µg/dL, stayed at 22. With everything else optimized, those two markers were the missing key — now, with an objective argument to put the psychological layer at the center of the plan. That's what I did at the sixth visit: instead of ordering more tests, I returned to the conversation about her day with the clinical weight she hadn't accepted before. Ana described it — site meetings, briefings, spreadsheets at night, the children, her husband also under pressure, her mother showing early signs of dementia, the decision to switch schools, financial pressure. She talked for ten minutes. She said she hadn't been sleeping well for years, that she woke several times thinking about problems, that she "dreaded" Friday afternoons. She also said she couldn't remember the last time she had felt the day was over — there was always something more.

That was where the case turned. Ana had optimized everything precision medicine knew how to measure, but there was a variable no test was capturing well that was sabotaging all the others. The hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis was in constant alarm. As long as it was, cortisol would stay elevated, CRP would not fall, sleep would not consolidate, and no further biochemical intervention would change that.

The pillar missing from Ana's plan is what this chapter and the next one cover: **Tending Mind, Body & Bonds**. The part of The ACTS Method that fragmented medicine ignores, that no single blood test reveals on its own, and that — when untreated — compromises every other pillar. Because of the breadth of the topic, I split the pillar into two chapters. This first one is the inner work — what the patient does with themselves: how the body translates stress into disease, how to assess the psychological layer in the office, which regulation techniques have evidence, and how to protect cognitive function across the decades. Chapter 13 covers the relational and existential dimension — bonds, sexual life, screens, purpose, and meaning — with another patient from the book, Ricardo, as the through-line.

Ana's path begins here.

### Psychoneuroimmunology: The Biology Already Knows What Medicine Pretends Not To

For decades, mind and body were treated as separate domains. The body doctor treats the body; the psychologist or psychiatrist treats the mind. Two offices, two languages, two agendas. That divide, however convenient for the organization of medical schools, has no biological basis.

Psychoneuroimmunology — the discipline that emerged in the 1980s and matured over the last twenty years — has demonstrated at the molecular level what was intuitively obvious: psychological states directly alter immune, hormonal, and inflammatory function. Not as metaphor. As mechanism.

The central circuit is the **hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis**. When the brain perceives a threat — whether a lion on the savanna or a Sunday-night email from the boss — the hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH). CRH stimulates the pituitary to release ACTH. ACTH stimulates the adrenals to produce cortisol. Cortisol mobilizes glucose, raises blood pressure, suppresses functions not essential to immediate survival — like digestion, reproduction, tissue repair — and, in the brain, amplifies vigilance circuits and reduces hippocampal plasticity.

That works well for the lion on the savanna. The threat appears, cortisol rises, the animal fights or flees, the threat passes, cortisol returns to baseline, the system rests. The problem is that the boss's email doesn't end with a clear resolution. Tomorrow's meeting doesn't end. Worry about the children doesn't end. Financial pressure doesn't end. The modern human brain frequently activates the HPA axis to stressors that have no immediate resolution — and keeps the activation going for weeks, months, years.

Chronically elevated cortisol has documented biological consequences that mirror almost point by point the markers we've discussed throughout this book. It induces insulin resistance, promotes visceral fat accumulation, suppresses T lymphocytes and NK cells, raises inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, CRP), reduces sex hormone production, disorganizes circadian rhythm, compromises the hippocampus — the region of memory and learning. A review published in *Nature Reviews Immunology* consolidated decades of evidence showing that chronic psychological stress produces a measurable pro-inflammatory profile in the blood, and that this profile is causally associated with cardiovascular disease, diabetes, dementia, and cancer.

In other words: chronic anxiety is not just a psychological annoyance. It is a biochemical pathway feeding exactly the same cascades that fuel accelerated aging and the four chronic diseases this book has been treating. The HPA axis in permanent alert is one of the "silent killers" of Chapter 2 — only it operates through the back door.

### Trauma Lives in the Body

There is a dimension of chronic stress that is rarely addressed in the preventive medicine office and that, when ignored, can keep the HPA axis activated regardless of how many *mindfulness* sessions the patient practices: **trauma.**

The ACE study — *Adverse Childhood Experiences*, conducted by Vincent Felitti and Robert Anda at Kaiser Permanente in the 1990s — demonstrated unavoidably that adverse childhood experiences (physical, emotional, or sexual abuse, neglect, witnessed domestic violence, household substance abuse, parental mental illness) have dose-response effects on adult health outcomes. Patients with an ACE score of 4 or more have substantially elevated risk of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, depression, and premature mortality — even when the statistical adjustment controls for adult behavioral factors. The magnitude of the effect rivals classic biological risk factors.

The mechanism isn't only psychological — it is measurable biology. Childhood trauma reprograms the HPA axis, alters DNA methylation patterns in cortisol-regulating genes (NR3C1), and leaves the nervous system in a state of persistent vigilance that manifests decades later as chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular risk. Bessel van der Kolk, in *The Body Keeps the Score*, exhaustively documents that biology. Trauma in adult life — acute grief, accident, violence, significant loss — operates through the same mechanism, with the difference that an adult's nervous system is more structured and post-event reorganization is often more recoverable.

The clinical implication is important. In patients with resistant CRP, chronically elevated cortisol, and fragmented sleep despite all the pillars apparently addressed — like Ana, whom we are getting to know in this chapter — it is worth including in the history a question about trauma. Not to psychologize the biological picture, but because trauma may be exactly the variable keeping the stress system on. And because treatment of that layer — with specialized psychotherapy (EMDR, trauma-focused CBT, *somatic experiencing*) — can move inflammatory biomarkers in ways no diet or supplement can.

The screening question I learned to ask is discreet but direct: *"Has anything serious happened to you in childhood or adult life that you feel still weighs in the body?"* The answer, when it comes, changes the direction of the clinical partnership.

### Telomeres, Stress, and the Biological Clock

In Chapter 3, we discussed telomeres — the protective ends of chromosomes — and the pioneering work of Elizabeth Blackburn and Elissa Epel, which demonstrated that chronic psychological stress accelerates telomere shortening. It is worth returning to that data here, in the chapter where it structurally belongs.

The 2004 study published in *PNAS* compared mothers caring for chronically ill children for several years with mothers caring for healthy children. The longer the mother had been caring for the sick child — a situation of intense and prolonged stress — the shorter her telomeres. The women with the highest perceived stress had telomeres equivalent to at least a decade more of biological aging compared with the women under less stress. The variable wasn't the objective situation — it was subjective perceived stress.

The revolutionary finding, as we already said, wasn't that telomeres shorten, but that the speed of shortening is modifiable. Subsequent research has shown that interventions like meditation, regular exercise, adequate sleep, and relational quality directly influence telomerase activity — the enzyme that repairs chromosome ends. The same logic applies to the epigenetic clocks discussed in Chapter 11: people in chronic stress have an epigenetic age greater than chronological; people who learn to regulate the nervous system have an epigenetic age lower than chronological.

That means mind-body integration isn't a "quality of life" issue — it is a matter of objectively measurable slowing of biological aging. Ignoring this dimension is accepting an acceleration that could have been avoided.

### Psychological Assessment: The Instruments the Non-Psychiatric Office Needs

Across eighteen months, Ana's visits mapped the body with precision and consistently registered the complaints of the nervous layer — tension headaches, neck stiffness, fragmented sleep, intermittent epigastric burning, anxiety she attributed to personality. When, at the sixth visit, it became clear that this layer needed to leave the narrative record and enter the formal plan, I administered two short psychological screening instruments that structure the conversation differently. On the **PHQ-9**, with nine validated questions for depression, she scored 14 — moderate range. On the **GAD-7**, with seven questions for generalized anxiety, she scored 16 — severe range. Those two numbers, which no laboratory prints, were as informative as the CRP of 1.8 — and they quantified on a validated scale what the open-ended history had been finding for months.

Psychological assessment in the non-psychiatric office does not replace specialized treatment. What it does — and does well — is **quantify** a layer that free-form history underestimates, **document evolution** over time, and **identify** when referral stops being optional and becomes essential. The instruments below are free, short, and validated in multiple languages and widely available in English. They should be in any office that treats adults with unexplained chronic inflammatory markers.

**PHQ-9 — Patient Health Questionnaire (9 items).** Depression screen. Score from 0 to 27. Practical cutoffs: 5 (mild), 10 (moderate), 15 (moderately severe), 20 (severe). Score ≥10 has sensitivity and specificity around 85% for major depressive episode. Question 9 — about thoughts of death, self-harm, or "would be better off dead" — deserves special attention: any positive response demands a direct conversation with the patient at the same visit, before they leave the office. I never crossed that line, even when the number was 1 on a scale of 0 to 3.

**GAD-7 — Generalized Anxiety Disorder (7 items).** Generalized anxiety screen. Score from 0 to 21. Cutoffs: 5 (mild), 10 (moderate), 15 (severe). Score ≥10 suggests a disorder warranting specialized evaluation. The GAD-7 captures background chronic anxiety well — the state Ana described as "I've always been anxious" — which is frequently normalized as personality and never treated.

**AUDIT — Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (10 items).** Screening for problem alcohol use, validated by the WHO and translated into multiple languages. Score ≥8 indicates risk drinking; ≥16, harmful use or dependence. It is especially useful in patients who describe their own use as "social" or "only on weekends" — the objective screen often finds what open-ended history doesn't. For this book's audience — adults 40–60 under chronic professional stress — alcohol is one of the most underrecognized coping mechanisms and one of the most damaging to sleep, mood, and metabolism.

**PCL-5 — PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (20 items).** PTSD screen. Longer, reserved for contexts where the history suggests significant childhood or adult trauma. Cutoff ≥33 indicates need for psychiatric evaluation. Apply to patients who responded affirmatively to the trauma question in the previous section.

**UCLA Loneliness Scale (short version, 3 items).** Three simple questions that quantify perceived loneliness — a variable with prognostic weight I discuss in detail in Chapter 13. Especially useful in patients who say *"I have lots of friends"* but describe a life suggesting otherwise.

**Professional burnout (ICD-11, code QD85).** Formally recognized by the WHO in 2019 as an occupational phenomenon with three dimensions: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization (cynicism toward work and colleagues), and reduced perceived efficacy. The most-used tool is the *Maslach Burnout Inventory* (MBI); shorter versions like the *Copenhagen Burnout Inventory* (CBI) are also valid. It is especially relevant in this book's target audience — executives, professionals, physicians, educators, and managers between 40 and 60. The picture frequently overlaps with depression and anxiety, but has a specific environmental trigger (chronic work environment) and responds to interventions involving environmental modification, not just individual treatment. Diagnosing burnout without confronting the environment that produces it is treating the temperature while ignoring the oven.

**When the psychological layer becomes a plan priority.** Three situations make this question automatic. **First:** persistent inflammatory markers (CRP > 1.5 chronically, elevated cortisol, fragmented sleep not explained by apnea or medication) despite optimized biological pillars — this was Ana's case. **Second:** PHQ-9 ≥10, GAD-7 ≥10, AUDIT ≥8, PCL-5 ≥33, or elevated loneliness scale score. **Third:** any positive response to the trauma question or any suicidal ideation, at any intensity. In those three scenarios, the psychological layer isn't adjunctive — it is the principal work of the plan.

### Relaxation Techniques and Nervous System Regulation

None of the interventions I describe below is miraculous. All require time, practice, repetition. But all have controlled evidence showing measurable impact on biological markers and relevant clinical outcomes. When psychological assessment indicates that the nervous dimension needs to enter the plan, these are the tools shared with psychologist and psychiatrist.

**Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).** It is the form of psychotherapy with the largest body of evidence for anxiety, depression, insomnia, and somatic symptoms associated with stress. It works by identifying automatic thought patterns that feed the state of permanent activation and training the patient to recognize them, question them, and respond differently. It isn't motivational advice — it is structured cognitive training, typically 12 to 20 weekly sessions. Consolidated meta-analyses show significant effects on anxiety and depression reduction, and efficacy comparable to medication in many cases — with the advantage that the effect persists after treatment ends, while medication requires continuous use. For patients with chronic anxiety, CBT is the first-line treatment with the most solid evidence. Ana started with a CBT-trained psychologist the same week as the sixth visit.

**Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR).** An eight-week structured program (Jon Kabat-Zinn, University of Massachusetts, 1979) combining mindful meditation, gentle yoga, and psychoeducation. 2015–2024 meta-analyses show that MBSR reduces cortisol, IL-6, CRP, and other inflammatory markers, in addition to improving anxiety, depression, and sleep with moderate to large effect sizes. A 2025 meta-analysis with 89 randomized trials confirmed reduction of inflammatory markers and increase of anti-inflammatory cytokines and BDNF. It isn't imported Eastern philosophy — it is a clinical intervention with a measurable biochemical basis. Ana started MBSR alongside CBT.

**Exercise as a neuroendocrine regulator.** We already discussed exercise in Chapter 7 as a biomechanical and metabolic medication. Worth repeating here in another key: regular exercise is one of the most potent regulators of the HPA axis. It reduces baseline cortisol over the long term, increases BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) production, improves hippocampal neurogenesis, and has documented efficacy in mild to moderate depression comparable to antidepressants. Zone 2 cardio has a calming effect on the autonomic nervous system; strength training releases endorphins and improves perceived self-efficacy. The two complement each other.

**Structured breathing and cardiac coherence.** Heart rate variability (HRV) is a direct marker of the balance between the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems. Low HRV is associated with chronic stress, inflammation, and cardiovascular mortality. Slow, rhythmic breathing techniques — for example, six breaths per minute with equal inhalation and exhalation — induce cardiac coherence and raise HRV in minutes. It doesn't replace therapy or psychiatric treatment, but it is a simple, free tool, practicable anywhere, that the patient can use to regulate the nervous system during moments of higher activation. Five minutes, two or three times a day, generates measurable benefit in weeks.

**Nature exposure.** The Japanese concept of *shinrin-yoku* — "forest bathing" — has been validated in controlled studies. Regular walks in natural settings reduce cortisol, blood pressure, heart rate, and inflammatory markers. It isn't exotic: any wooded park works. The practical recommendation is at least two hours per week in contact with nature, distributed however the patient prefers. For those living in urban centers, it's enough to prioritize the nearest park, a balcony with plants, or a monthly weekend in a less urban setting.

None of these interventions replaces the others. The question isn't "which is best?", but "which one will the patient actually do?". The best intervention is the one that fits in the person's real life and that they can sustain.

### Cognitive Function: From Reserve to Early Screening

Ana's mother, mentioned at the start of this chapter, began showing early signs of dementia at 72. For Ana, at 44, that wasn't only a caregiving responsibility — it was a personal shadow. In one visit, after describing in detail her mother's cognitive decline, she asked the question that has accompanied me for years in clinic: *"Doctor, am I going to follow the same path?"*

It is the question a large share of readers of this chapter brings to the office, in different formulations. A mother with Alzheimer's. A father who started forgetting names. A spouse who repeats the same story. The question behind it is always the same: **what can I do, now, while cognition is still mine?** The answer of the last five years has changed substantially — and it's worth recovering it here with honesty.

**Cognitive reserve: the concept that changed the question.** The neuropsychologist Yaakov Stern, of Columbia, documented in the 1990s something autopsy had hinted at before: two people with the same degree of brain pathology — the same amyloid load, the same neurofibrillary tangles, the same atrophy — can have radically different clinical presentations. One is demented; the other, functional. The variable explaining the difference is **cognitive reserve** — the brain's capacity to compensate for damage through alternative neural networks, built over a lifetime by education, professional complexity, bilingualism, reading, continuous learning, and social engagement. Cognitive reserve doesn't prevent the pathology; it postpones the clinical manifestation by years or decades. For a reader asking *"what can I do?"*, the answer begins before medication: each additional year of intellectual learning, each new language, each cognitively demanding activity added in midlife is a deposit in an account that will be drawn down decades later.

**Early detection: what the attentive patient can measure in 2026.** Cognitive longevity medicine has changed considerably in the last three years. Three tools are on the radar today.

The **MoCA (Montreal Cognitive Assessment)** is a 30-point screening test, applicable in 10 minutes, validated in multiple languages including English. Score <26 suggests mild cognitive impairment; <21, probable dementia. It is insufficient as an isolated diagnosis, but useful as a baseline in patients from age 50 onward, to monitor evolution over the years. Ideally, repeat every 1 to 2 years.

**Subjective cognitive decline (SCD)** — when the patient feels memory has worsened, even with normal exams — has recognized prognostic weight today. Meta-analyses show that people with SCD have double the risk of progression to MCI (mild cognitive impairment) and triple the risk of dementia in the next 5 to 10 years. Listening to the patient who says *"I'm forgetting names"* has stopped being automatic reassurance and become a warning sign worth careful evaluation.

**Longitudinal digital tests** — platforms like Creyos (formerly Cambridge Brain Sciences), Cogstate, and CogniFit — allow baseline and individual follow-up with good sensitivity for subtle changes in processing speed, working memory, and executive function. They don't replace formal neuropsychological evaluation, but they are useful monitoring tools for engaged patients who want to track their own trajectory.

**The 2024–2025 milestone: plasma p-tau217.** For decades, biological diagnosis of Alzheimer's depended on lumbar puncture (CSF analysis) or amyloid PET — invasive or expensive. In 2024, the FDA cleared the first plasma p-tau217 tests (Labcorp, Fujirebio Lumipulse, C2N) with high correlation to brain amyloid pathology. The test identifies, from a simple venous draw, whether the brain is carrying significant amyloid load — including in the preclinical phase, before symptoms. By 2026 it is available through major reference laboratories in the U.S. and Brazil. In patients with APOE4 (Chapter 11), strong family history of dementia, or persistent SCD, p-tau217 is today a decision exam: a negative result rules out the main hypothesis; a positive result raises the aggressiveness of multidomain prevention and justifies specialized neurological evaluation. It isn't a population-screening test — it is a tool for selected patients with a concrete clinical question. Ana, given her family history, opted to do it: result negative. It was the first time in 18 months she cried in the office from relief instead of exhaustion.

**MCI — the intermediate window.** Mild cognitive impairment is the state between normal cognition and dementia. The patient recognizes the change, performs below expected on formal tests, but maintains functional autonomy. The conversion rate to dementia is 10 to 15% per year in amnestic MCI, against 1 to 2% in the general population of the same age. It is the most important intervention window — and within it, multidomain intervention has the largest documented effect.

**FINGER, MAPT, POINTER: the evidence that the integrated plan works.** The **FINGER** trial (*Finnish Geriatric Intervention Study to Prevent Cognitive Impairment and Disability*), published in 2015 in *The Lancet*, was the first to show that multidomain intervention — Mediterranean diet, regular exercise, structured cognitive training, rigorous control of vascular factors, and clinical monitoring — produced significant cognitive gain in adults aged 60 to 77 at elevated risk, over two years of follow-up. The French **MAPT** and others partially replicated. In 2025, **US POINTER** (*US Study to Protect Brain Health Through Lifestyle Intervention to Reduce Risk*) was published in *JAMA* — a larger trial in the American context, confirming the benefit of the structured package and identifying that even the "self-guided" version (with less intensive accompaniment) produced gain — albeit smaller than the version with structured coordination. The convergent conclusion of the three trials: **what protects cognition is what protects the rest of the body**, and no one does it alone — structured accompaniment changes the size of the effect.

For APOE4 carriers (Chapter 11), the 2024 meta-analysis in *Alzheimer's & Dementia* showed that the benefit of multidomain intervention is even greater than in the general population — meaning APOE4 loads the gun, but lifestyle substantially reduces the chance of pulling the trigger.

**Structured cognitive training: what works and what is marketing.** The **ACTIVE** study (Rebok et al., *JAGS* 2014, 10-year follow-up) tested three modalities in adults 65+: reasoning, memory, and processing speed. The specific analysis of dementia incidence (Edwards et al. 2017) showed the strongest result for processing speed: just 10 to 14 sessions of 60 to 75 minutes, with periodic boosters, reduced dementia risk by 29% over ten years. It isn't any activity — it is specific, progressive, repeated training. Daily Sudoku is better than nothing, but it isn't structured training. **BrainHQ** (Posit Science, based on ACTIVE) and **Lumosity** have similar protocols. *Dual-task training* — combining cognitive challenge with motor activity (walking while counting backward from 100 by 7s) — has signal in older adults and is easy to incorporate.

**Warning signs for the attentive patient.** Forgetting where you put your keys is normal at any age; forgetting *what keys are* is not. Below, the signs that warrant formal evaluation, not reassurance:

- Difficulty performing previously automatic tasks (a known recipe, operating the microwave, driving on a familiar route)
- Repeating the same question or story several times in the same day, without realizing it
- Progressive difficulty finding common words mid-sentence
- Getting lost on familiar routes, or in your own neighborhood
- Forgetting important (non-trivial) appointments with growing frequency
- Personality or mood changes that those close to you notice
- Difficulty following a conversation with more than two people

The difference between normal forgetfulness and a warning sign is often a question of trajectory. *"I've always been distracted"* is one profile; *"my memory has gotten substantially worse in the last year"* is another. The second calls for a formal baseline — MoCA, digital tests, eventually p-tau217, with planned follow-up.

**Cognitive pharmacology and supplementation: what has reasonable evidence.** The question patients ask — *"is there a pill that helps?"* — has an honest answer: lifestyle pillars move cognitive trajectory far more than any supplement. Consolidated sleep (Chapter 14), regular exercise, rigorous control of cardiovascular factors (Chapters 5 and 9), apnea treatment, Shingrix vaccination (with emerging signal of reduced dementia risk in recent cohorts), social connection (Chapter 13), multidomain interventions like FINGER/MAPT/POINTER — all have far more solid evidence than any isolated molecule.

Within what is supplement, only a few warrant serious mention:

- **Citicoline (CDP-choline),** 500 to 2,000 mg/day, has meta-analyses showing modest benefit in mild cognitive impairment of vascular origin. Good safety profile.
- **Bacopa monnieri** has signal in working memory, but is dependent on formulations standardized to bacosides (50% minimum). Doses of 300 mg/day in 8- to 12-week studies.
- ***Lion's Mane*** (*Hericium erinaceus*) generates considerable market enthusiasm. Human evidence is still small and short-term — it is a frontier, not an established recommendation. I don't prescribe it routinely; I don't discourage attentive patients who want to try it for 3 to 6 months.
- ***Ginkgo biloba*** was the cognitive supplemental icon of the 1990s–2000s. The large subsequent trials (GEM Study, GuidAge) were negative for dementia prevention. It left my repertoire.

None of these replaces the pillars. And none compensates for poor sleep, sedentary lifestyle, isolation, and elevated ApoB. The cognitive preventive medicine of 2026 is, above all, systemic preventive medicine applied to the brain.

### Adaptogens and Nervous System Regulation Nutraceuticals

Adaptogens are a territory where science and commerce are frequently confused. The word itself — "adaptogen" — isn't an official pharmacological category, but a concept from traditional Asian medicine adopted by the supplement industry to describe compounds that, in theory, help the body deal with stress nonspecifically. Most products sold under that label have weak or nonexistent evidence. Some, however, have randomized trials that justify a serious discussion. Here are the ones I consider with weight enough to enter the conversation — always with the qualification that none replaces psychotherapy, consolidated sleep, or psychiatric treatment when that is what the case requires.

**Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*, KSM-66 formulation).** It is the adaptogen with the best human evidence package. Meta-analyses and randomized trials show reduction of perceived stress symptoms and mild to moderate anxiety, with clinically relevant magnitude in eight- to twelve-week trials. One of the objective markers that tends to move is morning cortisol — which, in several trials, drops about 20 to 30% versus placebo. A note of honesty here: traditional adaptogen literature often describes these plants as "bidirectional modulators" — capable of reducing cortisol when high and raising it when low. That discourse is common in the market, but solid human evidence only exists for reducing elevated cortisol in chronic stress contexts. There is no clinical trial convincingly showing that ashwagandha raises cortisol in patients with adrenal deficiency — and if the picture is real adrenal insufficiency, the right path is formal endocrine investigation, not supplementation. Usual dose: 600 mg per day of the standardized KSM-66 formulation, in one or two intakes. Two alerts matter: rare reports of idiosyncratic hepatitis appeared in pharmacovigilance databases between 2020 and 2023 — reason to monitor liver enzymes with prolonged use; and avoid use in pregnancy, lactation, and in patients with hyperthyroidism, because the plant can stimulate the thyroid.

**On low morning cortisol.** The reverse of Ana's picture — flattened morning cortisol, persistent fatigue, non-restorative sleep, generally in patients with a long history of chronic stress — appears with some frequency in clinic. With endocrine causes excluded (primary adrenal insufficiency, pituitary failure, suppression by prior corticosteroid use), what remains is what the older literature called "adrenal fatigue" and that we now better understand as functional dysfunction of the HPA axis. It isn't resolved with a supplement. The interventions with evidence are those of circadian rhythm, in order of impact: consolidated sleep with a regular schedule, sun exposure in the first sixty minutes after waking, moderate-intensity morning exercise, breakfast with protein, and, when the psychological layer is the principal driver, structured psychotherapy. Licorice — the plant I mentioned in Chapter 5 as a cause of hypertension when consumed in excess — works through the same mechanism that makes it an adjunctive tool here: it prolongs the half-life of endogenous cortisol. In selected patients, with documented low cortisol and normal-low blood pressure, it can have a punctual role, with rigorous monitoring of blood pressure and potassium. DHEA replacement enters when laboratory DHEA-S is low, under follow-up. Hydrocortisone without clear indication and "anti-adrenal-fatigue protocols" with eight or ten supplements stay out: they suppress more than help, or cost a lot without moving the needle.

**Saffron (*Crocus sativus*, *affron* preparation).** Not to be confused with turmeric (which we discussed in Chapter 5) — they are different plants. Saffron, extracted from the stigma of the *Crocus* flower, is expensive because each flower yields only three threads. At a standardized dose of 28 to 30 mg per day of the *affron* preparation, meta-analyses show efficacy in mild to moderate depression comparable to low-dose fluoxetine in small studies. It is one of the few supplements with consistent signal in this territory. Side effects are rare; there is no clinically relevant interaction with antidepressants at standard dose, but it is worth mentioning to the psychiatrist in patients already on pharmacological treatment.

**L-theanine.** An amino acid present in green tea, especially in higher concentrations in *matcha*. At 200 to 400 mg per day, it raises alpha-wave activity on the electroencephalogram — the pattern associated with relaxed alertness. Short-term studies show reduction of acute stress and subjective improvement in focus. Very safe, with no significant hypnotic effect, with good synergy with caffeine for those who use it during the day. It is the kind of tool that enters as "fine-tuning" in patients who have already optimized the essentials.

**Rhodiola rosea (SHR-5 extract).** A traditional European-Asian adaptogen with heterogeneous evidence in fatigue and mild stress symptoms. Some trials show benefit, others are neutral — and product quality varies considerably across manufacturers, which makes reproducibility difficult. I consider it a reasonable option in patients with mild cognitive fatigue without another identified cause, preferring products standardized to salidrosides and rosavins.

**Phosphatidylserine.** A cell membrane phospholipid with modest evidence in reducing cortisol response, especially in the context of intense exercise. Usual dose: 100 to 300 mg per day. Today derived from soy in most products (older formulations, derived from bovine tissue, were abandoned due to historical epidemiological precaution). A niche tool, useful in athletes or in professionals under chronic stress with hyperactive adrenal response.

**Aromatherapy.** Has a specific, modest but reproducible space, especially in **acute situational anxiety** — pre-operative, dental visit, pre-exam. **Inhaled lavender** (sachet, focused diffuser, a few drops on a handkerchief or collar) has the best evidence package in meta-analyses for this use. **Silexan** (standardized oral lavender, *Lasea* in Europe) has trials in mild generalized anxiety comparable to lorazepam and paroxetine — currently available in Europe; not available in the U.S. or Brazil at the time of writing. A few practical cautions worth remembering: oral ingestion of pure oils is not recommended (hepatotoxicity risk); citrus oils on sun-exposed skin cause photosensitization; diffusers in homes with cats are toxic to the animal; in infants, eucalyptus or menthol oils near the airways carry bronchospasm risk. It enters as a punctual adjunct, not a substitute for cognitive behavioral therapy, structured *mindfulness* programs, or psychiatric treatment when those are what the case calls for.

**Psilocybin in resistant depression — adjacent territory.** It is not a supplement; it is a controlled substance with strictly regulated clinical use. Trials in *NEJM* and *JAMA* have shown reductions in depressive symptoms in patients refractory to traditional antidepressants, in regimens of one to two doses paired with structured psychotherapy. Australia and the U.S. have opened specific regulatory paths; in Brazil, in 2026, clinical use is still experimental. I mention it for completeness — it is a frontier the reader will encounter in the press. It is not a recommendation, not for self-medication, and safety depends rigorously on context.

The lesson of this chapter is that the health of the mind-body axis is worked above all in structured habits — regular sleep, morning natural-light exposure, regular nervous-system regulation practice (breathing, mindfulness, MBSR), psychotherapy when indicated, and psychiatric treatment when needed. The adaptogens above are reasonable adjuncts in patients already in integrated care, not substitutes for the work Ana's case exemplified. Using a supplement to avoid therapy is the shortcut that leaves the problem in the same place.

### Ana: Six Months Later

Ana came back to the office with results that, by this point in the book, you can read on your own. CRP dropped from 1.8 to 0.7. Morning cortisol fell from 22 to 14. Sleep consolidated, without the middle-of-the-night awakenings. PHQ-9, which started at 14, went to 6 — minimal range. GAD-7, which started at 16, went to 5. On follow-up MoCA, two points more than baseline — likely from improved sleep and reduced anxiety, which degrade executive function when chronic. Epigenetic age, which had stayed flat across the eight months of exclusively biochemical optimization, slowed by two years. p-tau217 remained negative.

The path involved CBT with a psychologist experienced in generalized anxiety; a complete eight-week MBSR program; daily twenty-minute morning mindfulness practice; exercise maintained; two friendships restored to weekly structured contact; a referral to a psychiatrist with acceptance, after conversation, of a low-dose antidepressant for six months — a shared, revisable decision, and one that was eventually withdrawn at the twelfth month. The other pillars of the plan didn't change. What changed was the pillar that had been missing.

But there was a question the tests didn't capture, and that Ana also carried without quite knowing how to formulate. With whom would she live these extra years she was building? For what? Those are questions of a different nature — questions for the next chapter, where another patient from this book, Ricardo, opens the conversation about the relational and existential dimension of Pillar T.

### The Essential in 60 Seconds

- **Mind and body are integrated systems**, not separate domains. Chronic psychological stress activates the HPA axis, raises cortisol, increases inflammatory cytokines, and accelerates measurable biological aging — including telomere shortening and epigenetic acceleration. Biomarkers that don't improve despite optimized biological pillars signal that this dimension needs to be addressed.

- **Trauma lives in the body.** ACE score (Felitti) is an independent predictor of chronic outcomes and mortality. Discreet screening question in the office: *"Has anything serious happened in your childhood or adult life that still weighs in your body?"* An affirmative answer calls for specialized psychotherapy (EMDR, trauma-focused CBT, *somatic experiencing*), not psychologization of the biological picture.

- **Psychological assessment in the non-psychiatric office.** Short, free instruments, validated in multiple languages: **PHQ-9** (depression, cutoff ≥10), **GAD-7** (anxiety, cutoff ≥10), **AUDIT** (alcohol, cutoff ≥8), **PCL-5** (PTSD, cutoff ≥33), **UCLA-3** (loneliness), and **burnout** screening (ICD-11 QD85 — exhaustion, cynicism, reduced efficacy). Three situations make the psychological layer a priority of the plan: persistent inflammatory markers despite optimized pillars; elevated scores on the scales; any reported suicidal ideation or trauma.

- **Relaxation techniques with evidence:** cognitive behavioral therapy (first line for anxiety and depression), MBSR (8 weeks, measurable effect on cortisol and inflammatory markers), exercise (potent regulator of the HPA axis, with BDNF and neurogenesis), structured breathing (raises HRV in minutes), regular nature exposure (*shinrin-yoku*).

- **Cognitive function is protected before it fails.** **Cognitive reserve** (Yaakov Stern) — education, bilingualism, professional complexity, continuous learning, social engagement — postpones the clinical manifestation of pathology. Early screening includes MoCA, SCD (with its own prognostic value), longitudinal digital tests (Creyos, Cogstate), and — the 2024–2025 milestone — **plasma p-tau217**, available through major reference labs in the U.S. and Brazil, for selected patients with APOE4, strong family history, or persistent SCD.

- **MCI (mild cognitive impairment)** has a 10–15%/year conversion to dementia — a critical intervention window. The **FINGER (2015)**, **MAPT**, and **US POINTER (JAMA 2025)** trials converge: multidomain intervention (exercise, diet, structured cognitive training, vascular control, accompaniment) produces measurable cognitive gain, with greater effect in APOE4 carriers. The **ACTIVE trial (Rebok et al., *JAGS* 2014; Edwards et al. 2017)** showed that processing-speed training reduces dementia risk by 29% over 10 years — not just any game; it is specific, progressive training.

- **Cognitive supplementation is an adjunct, not a substitute.** Citicoline (500–2,000 mg, vascular MCI), Bacopa standardized to bacosides, *Lion's Mane* on the frontier. Ginkgo left the repertoire after GEM and GuidAge. None compensates for poor sleep, sedentary behavior, isolation, or elevated ApoB.

- **Adaptogens with reasonable evidence:** ashwagandha KSM-66 (best evidence; alert for hepatotoxicity and hyperthyroidism), saffron (*affron*) in mild depression, L-theanine for acute stress and focus, rhodiola in mild cognitive fatigue, phosphatidylserine in hyperactive adrenal response. Psilocybin in resistant depression is a regulated clinical frontier, not a supplement. Low morning cortisol of functional cause is treated with circadian rhythm, psychotherapy, and specific adjuncts — not with "anti-adrenal-fatigue protocols."
