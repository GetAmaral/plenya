***Pillar A — Activity, Alimentation & Smart Adjuncts***

## Chapter 7 — Activity — The Most Powerful Medicine We Have

At the end of Chapter 5, with Marcos's complete panel in hand — CAC of 412, ApoB of 82, insulin of 11, CRP of 1.6 — we adjusted the strategy: we intensified the statin, added ezetimibe, and started monitoring every three months. But pharmacology was only one part of the plan. The sentence I used in the visit was: *"The medication will take care of ApoB. Now we need to take care of everything the medication can't reach."*

Marcos asked what I meant.

I meant that an insulin of 11 wasn't going to drop on a statin. That a CRP of 1.6 wasn't going to normalize on ezetimibe. That the insulin resistance no one had investigated needed tools no prescription writes: what he ate, how he moved, and what was missing in his body that the lab showed but no one had corrected.

I meant we needed to start the three engines of Pillar A — activity, alimentation, and smart adjuncts. This chapter is about the first of them, the most underestimated in standard preventive practice and, at the same time, the one with the strongest evidence of all: physical activity. Alimentation and smart adjuncts — the other two engines — come in the next chapter.

---

If a drug existed that reduced all-cause mortality by up to 80%, prevented cardiovascular disease, diabetes, dementia, and at least 13 types of cancer, improved mood, cognition, and sleep, and cost nothing — what physician wouldn't prescribe it?

That drug exists. It's called exercise. And most physicians still don't prescribe it with the same precision they prescribe a statin.

When I wrote *"structured exercise focused on VO₂ max and strength"* in Marcos's plan, it wasn't a generic phrase. Each word had a precise clinical meaning. And the reason I prescribed those modalities — and not simply "exercise" — is in the data that follows.

### VO₂ Max: The Number That Matters Most

Of all the mortality predictors studied in medicine — smoking, hypertension, diabetes, coronary disease, dyslipidemia — one outranks them all: cardiorespiratory fitness, measured by VO₂ max.

VO₂ max is the maximum volume of oxygen the body can take in, transport, and use during intense exercise. It integrates, in a single number, the efficiency of the heart, lungs, vessels, and muscles. It is the most direct measure of the body's capacity to generate aerobic energy — and that capacity is intimately linked to survival.

The study that best demonstrated this relationship (Mandsager et al., Cleveland Clinic, *JAMA Network Open* 2018) analyzed 122,007 adults with median follow-up of 8.4 years. Compared to the elite group, individuals with low fitness had **five times the risk of death** (HR 5.04) — higher than smoking (HR 1.41), diabetes (HR 1.40), and coronary disease (HR 1.29). **Low fitness was the most lethal risk factor on the list — and the most modifiable.**

![Figure 7.1 — The Risk Factor No One Prescribes For. Horizontal bars comparing all-cause mortality hazard ratios: low cardiorespiratory fitness (extreme sedentary, 5.0x) versus end-stage renal disease (2.0x), smoking (1.4x), diabetes (1.4x), and coronary disease (1.3x). More lethal than smoking, diabetes, or heart disease.](../Cap07%20Fig01.PNG)

And the most surprising finding: there was no ceiling to the benefit. Even among the already very fit, climbing one more rung was still associated with lower mortality. Even in those over 70 and in those with hypertension, elite fitness conferred a significant advantage over high fitness.

The Copenhagen Male Study, conducted by Clausen and colleagues and published in *JACC* also in 2018, added a temporal dimension that makes the data concrete. It followed 5,107 Danish men for 46 years — almost half a century. Each 1 ml/kg/min increase in VO₂ max in midlife was associated with a gain of 45 days of life (95% CI: 30–61 days; p<0.001). The fittest 5% lived approximately five years longer than the least fit 5%.

**Five years. For being physically fit in midlife.**

Imboden and colleagues, also in 2018 in *JACC*, confirmed the relationship in 4,137 healthy adults with VO₂ max measured directly by cardiopulmonary exercise testing and a 24-year follow-up. The consistency among these three large studies — Cleveland Clinic, Copenhagen, Ball State — is remarkable. The message is the same: cardiorespiratory fitness isn't an athletic curiosity. It is the most powerful survival predictor in existence.

A question naturally follows — and patients ask it every week in the office when they receive their CPET report: *"is my VO₂ max good?"* The answer requires the same logic as the calcium score in Chapter 5: there is no absolute "good" number; there is a number good **for your age and sex**. The most-used reference today is the FRIEND database, built by Kaminsky and colleagues and published in *Mayo Clinic Proceedings* in 2015, which measured VO₂ max by direct CPET in more than 7,700 healthy adults. From it, two anchors are enough to locate yourself. A man between 50 and 59 has a population median of about 32 ml/kg/min and enters the "elite" range above 45. A woman in the same age band has a median around 24 and an elite range above 35. The natural decline with aging is approximately 10% per decade — and the clinical target I propose isn't simply being in the 75th percentile of where you are today, but aiming, already in midlife, at the 75th percentile of the age band **a decade younger**. It's the same logic as the 85-Year-Old Decathlon: training the reserve today that will guarantee independence three decades from now. For those who want the exact percentile, FRIEND-based apps — and the ACSM's own calculator — give the value in thirty seconds from age, sex, and measured number.

### The Most Important Data Point for Non-Athletes

If you are reading this book and don't exercise regularly, the numbers above may seem distant — marathon-runner stuff. But the most freeing piece of this entire literature is precisely the opposite.

The biggest jump in benefit happens on the first step. Going from sedentary to a minimum level of activity — 150 minutes per week of moderate exercise, as international guidelines recommend — produces a disproportionately large risk reduction. Additional gains are real, but incremental. As Carl Lavie, one of the commentators on the Mandsager study, observed: the largest return on investment is simply moving out of the lowest group into the next one, which is generally achievable by following basic physical activity guidelines.

In the literature, every 1 MET gain in cardiorespiratory fitness — equivalent to about 3.5 ml/kg/min of VO₂ max — is associated with a 13 to 15% reduction in all-cause mortality risk. One MET is the difference between climbing a flight of stairs without stopping and arriving at the top out of breath. It is measurable, achievable, and accessible.

The news is at once disturbing and freeing: disturbing because being sedentary carries risk comparable to smoking; freeing because starting already changes the game.

### The Marginal Decade: Training for Who You'll Be at 85

Before going down to the technique of training, it's worth pausing on a concept Peter Attia describes in *Outlive* and that I adopted in clinic because it completely changes how the patient engages with exercise. He calls it the **marginal decade** — and roughly: the last decade of your useful life, generally the one between 80 and 90, is the decade that decides whether you are going to *live* or merely *breathe*. It's the period in which most people lose functional independence, stop driving, stop traveling, stop playing on the floor with grandchildren, and spend the last five or ten years in progressively diminished capacity. It's the decade no one plans for — and that is exactly why it happens the way it happens.

The proposal of the decathlon is simple. Ask the patient to write a short list of specific physical tasks they want, non-negotiably, to be able to do at 85. Not abstractions like "stay active." Concrete tasks, with numbers. For example: get up off the floor without using your hands; carry a 33-pound suitcase up a flight of stairs; play with a grandchild on your lap for ten minutes; walk 3 miles on a sunny Sunday without exhaustion; rise from a low chair without support; swim 200 meters continuously.

Once the list is written, the equation flips. The person stops training "for today" and starts training so that, at 85, they have the physical capacity those tasks demand. And here the cold math enters: from peak strength and VO₂ max around age 30 until 85, we lose on average 30 to 50% of maximum strength and about 50% of VO₂ max — if we do nothing. That means that to carry 33 pounds up a flight of stairs at 85, I need, today at 50, to be able to carry 55 to 65 pounds with margin. To swim 200 meters continuously at 85, I need to swim 400 to 500 today. Midlife training, in this logic, isn't for the aesthetics of next summer. It's the reserve that ensures, after decades of inevitable decline, enough remains to be independent in the last decade of life.

When Marcos understood this calculation, training stopped being an obligatory weight on the executive's calendar and became, in his own words, *"the only thing that depends on me alone to decide how I'll be at 85."* It is the same awareness the four pillars below translate into practice.

### The Four Pillars of Exercise for Longevity

Exercise for longevity is not the same as exercise for aesthetics or athletic performance. The goal is to maximize functional capacity over decades, preserve muscle mass, keep the cardiovascular system efficient, and prevent falls and loss of independence. The prescription needs to cover four complementary domains — and that is exactly how I structured Marcos's plan.

Before going down into each one, it's worth naming the biological principle that connects the four — and that, in fact, connects much of what this book proposes. It's called **hormesis**: the idea that **acute, intense, brief stimuli followed by adequate recovery produce adaptation**, while the same stimuli sustained chronically produce damage. Exercise is the most-studied example — an acute strength session damages muscle microfibers; the muscle repairs stronger. But the same logic explains the intermittent fasting of Chapter 8 (acute metabolic stress that triggers autophagy and sensitizes insulin), the morning sun exposure of Chapter 14 (circadian signal that anchors the SCN — suprachiasmatic nucleus, the central hypothalamic clock, detailed in Chapter 14), the controlled cold or heat exposure of saunas and cold plunges (brief thermal stress that mobilizes heat-shock proteins and activates brown adipose tissue). Chronic stress breaks. Acute stress, with recovery, builds. The four pillars below apply this principle to exercise — each on a different axis.

**Pillar 1 — Cardio Zone 2: The Aerobic Base**

Zone 2 is the intensity at which you can still hold a conversation — with some effort, but without getting out of breath. Technically, it is the range where blood lactate stays below 2 mmol/L and metabolism predominantly uses fat as fuel.

Training in Zone 2 builds the aerobic foundation on which everything else rests. Sessions of 45 to 90 minutes at this intensity increase the number and efficiency of mitochondria in muscle cells, expand the capillary network that delivers oxygen to tissues, and improve the capacity to oxidize fatty acids. Healthy and numerous mitochondria are one of the central markers of healthy aging — and Zone 2 is the most consistent stimulus for building them.

There are three practical ways to know whether you are actually in Zone 2 — and they're worth presenting in decreasing order of precision. The first, the gold standard of exercise physiology, is **direct blood lactate measurement** with a portable lactate meter. These are devices the size of a glucometer, with disposable strips that read a drop of blood from a finger or earlobe stick and deliver lactate in mmol/L in ten seconds. In the middle of an aerobic session, with the body warmed up for at least 20 minutes, Zone 2 corresponds to values between **1.7 and 2.0 mmol/L** — above 2, metabolism is already migrating toward growing dependence on glycogen and you've left the range. The lactate meter is, today, what separates those who really train in Zone 2 from those who train thinking they are. For people who take conditioning seriously, it's a one-time investment that pays dividends in every session.

The second method is the **Maffetone formula**, useful for those who want a numerical target without pricking a finger. Calculate **180 minus your age**, with small adjustments: subtract 5 for those with chronic disease or returning after a long pause; add 5 for those who have trained for more than two years without injury. The result is the ceiling of aerobic heart rate — and 10 to 15 bpm below that ceiling, on average, corresponds to the center of Zone 2. For a 50-year-old without chronic conditions, the ceiling would be 130 bpm, and the heart of Zone 2 would sit around 115–125 bpm. It's not a lab method — the correlation between heart rate and lactate varies from person to person — but it's reasonably close in most healthy adults and works for those without access to a lactate meter.

The third method is the **talk test**, and it is the most accessible of all: if you can speak full sentences with some effort, but can't sing or hold a fully comfortable conversation, you are in Zone 2. Coarser than the previous two — it can err by 10–15% in either direction — but useful as rough calibration in walking, light running, or cycling. The three methods together cover the spectrum: the lactate meter for those who want precision, Maffetone for those who want a number without a needle, and the talk test for those who want to start today with what they have.

Elite endurance athletes spend more than 80% of training volume in this zone. Not because it's easy, but because it's efficient: it builds base with low injury risk and allows volume accumulation without overtraining. The same logic applies to non-athletes.

*Practical recommendation:* 2 to 3 sessions per week, 45 to 60 minutes each. Brisk walking, cycling, swimming, elliptical — the modality matters less than the consistency.

**Pillar 2 — HIIT / Zone 5: Raise the Ceiling**

If Zone 2 builds the base, high-intensity interval training raises the ceiling. Short intervals of maximum or near-maximum effort — from 30 seconds to 4 minutes — followed by recovery, are the most effective stimulus for raising VO₂ max itself.

The most-studied protocol is the Norwegian 4×4: 4 intervals of 4 minutes at 85–95% of maximum heart rate, with 3 minutes of active recovery between them. For those with less time or more experience, shorter and more intense intervals also work.

There is a fitness parameter that VO₂ max alone doesn't show and that, in midlife clinical practice, matters as much as the peak: the *time* you can sustain a high intensity before yielding. It's what some physiologists call **time above threshold** — the area under the curve, not just its top. Two patients can have the same VO₂ max of 42 ml/kg/min and absurdly different performance on a stress test: one sustains three minutes in the high zone before stopping; the other sustains eight. That difference isn't in the peak — it's in mitochondrial density, in lactate buffering capacity, and in the efficiency with which the body recycles what it produces. It's what separates someone who merely "touches the ceiling" from someone who lives a bit up there. In daily life, it's what decides whether you climb three flights of stairs carrying a suitcase and arrive intact, or arrive winded before the second floor. Zone 2 training is what builds that time — because new mitochondria, created by the aerobic base, are what allow lasting effort. That is why HIIT and Zone 2 are not competing stimuli; they are two sides of the same coin. HIIT raises the ceiling. Zone 2 raises the time you can stay near it. Ignoring either front compromises the other — and that is why this chapter's prescription calls for a high aerobic base *before* the dose of HIIT becomes truly useful.

The dose matters: HIIT is a potent stimulus that demands recovery. More than two sessions per week increases injury risk and accumulated fatigue without proportional benefit.

*Practical recommendation:* 1 session per week. At most 2.

**Pillar 3 — Strength Training: The Most Neglected Pillar**

If I could prescribe only one form of exercise to all patients over 40, it would be strength training.

Not because cardio doesn't matter — as I just showed, it matters enormously. But because the loss of muscle mass is the process that most directly converts aging into incapacity, and it is the most neglected by conventional medicine and by the population at large.

Sarcopenia begins silently around age 30 and accelerates after 50. Estimates put the loss at 3 to 8% of muscle mass per decade from the fifth decade of life onward. By 80, a sedentary person may have lost 30% or more of the muscle mass they had at 30. And the loss of strength is even faster than the loss of mass.

The consequences cascade: less muscle means lower glucose uptake — skeletal muscle is the body's largest glucose consumer — worse insulin sensitivity, lower basal metabolic rate, greater visceral fat accumulation, higher fall and fracture risk, and progressive loss of functional independence. In Chapter 6, we saw how strength training was central in Fernanda's metabolic reversal. The mechanism is direct: contracting muscle absorbs glucose without depending on elevated insulin to do the work.

The good news is that sarcopenia can be measured objectively. The most-used parameter is the Appendicular Skeletal Muscle Index (ASMI), which divides limb lean mass by height squared and is obtained by bioelectrical impedance or, more precisely, by DEXA. In men, ASMI above 7 kg/m² is considered adequate; in women, above 5.5 kg/m². Below those values, the diagnosis of pre-sarcopenia or sarcopenia is objective. Measuring periodically — every six to twelve months — is the way to know whether the trajectory is preserved or quietly regressing. Another complementary marker is phase angle, obtained by bioimpedance, which reflects cellular integrity and is an independent predictor of mortality in multiple populations. Values below 5.5° in men and 4.8° in women are warning signs.

Add a third marker, simpler and perhaps the most underused in clinical practice: **hand grip strength**, measured with a hand dynamometer. It was introduced in Chapter 4 as a mortality predictor in the PURE study — every 5 kg less means 16% greater all-cause mortality risk — and it enters the European consensus (EWGSOP2) as an objective criterion for probable sarcopenia: below **27 kg in men** and **16 kg in women**. It's worth repeating here because, within the context of Pillar 3, grip strength has a distinct clinical function: it is the functional test that confirms whether the gain in lean mass (captured by ASMI) is translating into usable strength. Mass without strength is cosmetic mass. Five seconds per hand, three measurements, best result counted — once per quarter, alongside body composition.

Strength training also protects bone mineral density, reduces osteoporosis risk, and improves joint stability. Recent meta-analyses associate resistance exercise with lower mortality from all causes and from cancer.

*Practical recommendation:* 2 to 3 sessions per week, involving all major muscle groups. Progressive load — the principle of overload that drives adaptation. It doesn't have to be gym training; it can be with dumbbells at home, resistance bands, kettlebells, or body weight. What matters is that resistance increases over time and that the stimulus challenges the muscle sufficiently.

**Pillar 4 — Stability, Grip, and Mobility: The Insurance Against Falls**

Falls are the leading cause of fatal injury in adults over 65. Most are preventable — but preventing them requires more than "doing a little stretching." It requires treating stability as the genuine fourth pillar, with specific stimuli and markers you can test at home.

Stability is the body's capacity to resist disturbances without losing postural control. In practical terms: it's what allows you to step on a crooked rug, trip over a curb, or descend an unexpected step without falling. It depends on deep core strength (transversus abdominis, pelvic floor, diaphragm), fine neuromuscular control, proprioceptive integrity of the feet and ankles, and — something standard practice almost never measures — hand grip strength. Each of these domains has both a training exercise and a testing exercise.

The first is the **passive hang** (*dead hang*): hanging from a fixed bar with arms extended, without pulling up, and measuring how long you can hold. It trains grip, decompresses the spine, stimulates shoulder stabilizers, and is a functional test that predicts longevity with notable consistency in cohort studies. An adult in midlife should sustain at least 30 seconds (women) and 60 seconds (men). Anyone who falls short of that has a clear sign of deficient physical reserve.

The second is the **asymmetric loaded carry** — the so-called *farmer's carry* (carrying heavy dumbbells along the sides of the body for 30 to 60 meters) and its unilateral variations (*suitcase carry*, with the weight on one side only). These are exercises that unify stability, postural strength, and grip in a single functional gesture — the same gesture as carrying heavy groceries to the car.

The third is **single-leg balance**: standing on one leg, eyes open and then closed. A healthy 50-year-old should be able to manage at least 30 seconds with eyes open and 10 seconds with eyes closed; below that, fall risk in the coming years rises substantially. It is a two-minute test any patient can do at home.

The fourth is **specific foot and ankle work** — active toe flexions (technically called *toe yoga*), unilateral heel raises, ankle mobility against a wall. It looks like decorative exercise, but stiff feet and locked ankles are among the leading mechanical causes of falls in older adults.

And the fifth, that almost no one trains, is **diaphragmatic breathing**. The diaphragm is the central muscle of trunk stability. People with a high-thoracic breathing pattern — typical of those living under chronic stress — have a less stable core, even with strong abs. Ten minutes a day of conscious diaphragmatic breathing (hand on the abdomen, 360° expansion, prolonged exhale) reeducates that pattern.

*Practical recommendation:* 10 to 15 minutes integrated into the warm-up of training days, alternating the five domains across the week. And, once a month, repeat the three tests — *dead hang*, single-leg balance, and grip — to track the trajectory. It is not an extra session. It is the pillar that keeps the other three working without injury and that decides the difference between aging on your feet and aging on the couch.

### When One Exercise Hits Three Pillars: The Case of *Rucking*

A practical observation that stands on its own. You just read about four pillars as if they were four separate workouts. For prescription purposes, that works. But some exercises hit more than one pillar at the same time — and that changes how you fit training into the real routine of someone working ten hours a day. The most complete of them, and probably the most underestimated in standard preventive practice, is *rucking*: walking with a loaded backpack. The idea is old, military, and technically banal. The physiological effects are not. A 45- to 60-minute walk with 15 to 25 pounds on your back produces work in Zone 2 (the heart works in the aerobic range), load stimulus for postural and lower-limb musculature (with much less joint impact than running), and gentle repeated impact — which is the most efficient stimulus for preserving bone density in men and women over 50. Three pillars in one exercise that doesn't need a gym, doesn't require technical learning, and is perfectly compatible with walking the dog or strolling in the park at the end of the day. For those starting out, it is probably the most efficient entry — and for those already training, it is the best way to add Zone 2 volume without adding training time.

### The André Case: When Training A Lot Isn't Training Right

In Chapter 3, we met André — 45, amateur triathlete, vegetarian, daily meditation, owner of a watch that measured heart rate variability in real time. He looked 40. But his biomarkers told another story: CRP of 2.1, homocysteine of 14, and a fasting insulin of 8.5 that looked normal but, as we saw in Chapter 6, hid a profoundly altered postprandial response to a glucose load. Estimated biological age above 55. André did everything — and was still aging faster than he should.

When I analyzed his training, the problem became evident. André trained ten hours a week — athletic volume — but almost all in Zones 3 and 4: race pace, constant stimulus, insufficient recovery. The Zone 2 base was practically nonexistent. Strength training? One "functional" session per week, with a band and light body weight — far from the stimulus needed to preserve muscle mass in a 45-year-old man. Stability and mobility? Nothing structured.

The result was a body with reasonable VO₂ max but chronically inflamed by excess stimulus without recovery, with incipient sarcopenia hidden under an athletic appearance, and mitochondria stressed by overtraining. André was training too much — and in the wrong pillars.

We reorganized: we reduced total volume, raised the proportion of Zone 2 to more than 70% of aerobic training, included strength training with progressive load twice a week, and structured two days of real rest. In six months, CRP dropped from 2.1 to 0.7. Homocysteine from 14 to 9 — corrected in part by the methylcobalamin and methylated folate supplementation his vegetarianism required and that no one had prescribed. Body composition changed: less visceral fat, more lean mass. He was training less — and getting more.

### Weekly Template

**For someone starting from zero (4 training days, 3 rest days):**

| Day | Activity | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Brisk walk + mobility | 30 + 10 min |
| Tuesday | Strength — full body (bodyweight) | 30 min |
| Wednesday | Rest | — |
| Thursday | Brisk walk + mobility | 30 + 10 min |
| Friday | Strength — full body | 30 min |
| Saturday | Rest | — |
| Sunday | Rest | — |

If on the weekend you feel like walking, biking, or playing a sport — great. But as a bonus, not as an obligation. Four sessions per week, completed consistently, produce more results than seven planned and abandoned by the third week.

**For someone already training regularly (5 training days, 2 rest days):**

| Day | Activity | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Zone 2 (light cardio) | 50–60 min |
| Tuesday | Strength — lower body + core | 45 min |
| Wednesday | Rest | — |
| Thursday | Strength — upper body + mobility | 45 min |
| Friday | Zone 2 | 50–60 min |
| Saturday | HIIT 4×4 + light strength | 50–60 min |
| Sunday | Rest | — |

Two days of complete rest are not laziness — they are part of the program. Muscle does not grow during training; it grows during recovery. The cardiovascular system adapts between sessions, not during them. Whoever trains without resting isn't training more — they are recovering less.

These are models, not fixed prescriptions. The structure that matters: aerobic base as the largest volume, ceiling stimulus once a week, regular strength training, respected rest, and mobility integrated into training days.

The plan I designed for Marcos followed the logic of the intermediate template, adapted to his reality: Zone 2 on the cycle ergometer (it protected his knees), strength training with a coach who understood the clinical context, and HIIT introduced gradually from week six onward, when the aerobic base was already more solid.

### The Essential in 60 Seconds

- VO₂ max is the strongest predictor of all-cause mortality — superior to smoking, diabetes, and coronary disease. Each 1 MET gain equals a 13–15% reduction in death risk. The greatest benefit is in the first step: leaving sedentary status.

- Exercise for longevity has four pillars: Zone 2 cardio (aerobic base and mitochondrial efficiency), HIIT (raise VO₂ max), strength training (counter sarcopenia and improve insulin sensitivity), and stability/mobility (prevent falls and maintain independence).

- Training a lot ≠ training right. André's case — triathlete with elevated CRP and hidden sarcopenia — shows that volume without structure can age rather than protect. Zone 2 base, regular strength, respected rest.

- Sarcopenia can be measured: ASMI below 7.0 kg/m² in men and 5.5 in women is a warning sign. What matters is the trajectory — reassess every six to twelve months.
