***Pillar S — Sleep, Rhythm & Recovery***

## Chapter 14 — Sleep, Rhythm, and Recovery — The Invisible Conductor

Paulo came back to the office eighteen months after the first visit. As we saw in Chapters 10 and 11, at 48 he had arrived with total testosterone of 310, free at 4.8, asking only for "optimization." In the next six months, without hormone replacement, we had recovered most of the panel — total testosterone at 485, free at 11.2, vitamin D at 58, CRP at 0.9, epigenetic age slowing by two years. The next return, at twelve months, held the gains. Everything looked fine.

In this new visit, however, eighteen months in, some numbers had regressed. Total testosterone: 410. Free: 8.4. CRP: 1.3. HbA1c: had risen from 5.2 to 5.5. Stable weight, training kept up, nutrition still good. No obvious reason for the slip.

I asked the question I usually ask when the panel regresses without clear cause: *"How is your sleep?"* Paulo answered that "it stays around six hours, with some awakenings, nothing very different." I had recorded exactly that eighteen months earlier. At the first visit, we had worked on a few improvements — going to bed earlier, reducing screens before sleep — and Paulo had reported subjective gain. But the numbers hadn't changed. Six hours stayed six hours.

I asked whether his wife complained about anything during his sleep. He laughed.

> *"She's been telling me for years that I snore a lot. I've always joked it's age."*

In that instant, two pieces fell into place. What I had called "non-consolidated sleep" in Chapter 10 and superficially corrected with vitamin D and better training wasn't only poor sleep quality — it could be a specific condition, never investigated, that now offered itself as the probable explanation for everything that had regressed. I referred Paulo for polysomnography that same week.

The result confirmed the suspicion: moderate obstructive sleep apnea, with an apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) of 22 events per hour and oxygen desaturations down to 86% in some episodes. Paulo had never heard of apnea. He met the diagnosis with disbelief: *"Doctor, I sleep well. I wake up rested. I don't have daytime sleepiness."*

Apnea rarely presents with dramatic daytime sleepiness in high-performing executives. The signs appear elsewhere — in the regressing hormonal panel, in the rising CRP, in the metabolic marker that doesn't improve despite all other pillars optimized. Sleep isn't a pillar like the others. **It is the substrate on which the other pillars operate.** When the substrate is compromised, everything built above it begins to crack.

Paulo's case is the entry point for this chapter, which completes the four pillars of The ACTS Method: **Sleep, Rhythm, and Recovery**. The most fundamental — and paradoxically the most neglected — part of the entire system. Because when circadian rhythm is ignored and sleep is inadequate, none of the other pillars works fully. Perfect nutrition doesn't consolidate gains. Exercise produces half the benefits. Hormones regress. Anxiety rises. The body isn't a sum of isolated parts — it is a temporal system. And it is that system that governs everything we have discussed so far.

### The Conductor and the Orchestra: How the Circadian System Works

Hidden in the hypothalamus, just above the optic chiasm, lies a structure of about 20,000 neurons called the **suprachiasmatic nucleus — SCN**. It is one of the smallest decision centers in the human body and also one of the most powerful. The SCN is the central clock that synchronizes, through neural and hormonal signals, all the other biological clocks scattered through tissues and organs: liver, muscle, pancreas, gut, heart, skin, kidney, adipose tissue. Every cell in the body has its own molecular clock — a genetic circuit with a roughly 24-hour cycle. The SCN is the conductor. The cells are the orchestra.

The conductor must be constantly adjusted by external cues. Those cues are called *zeitgebers* — German for "time-givers." The main ones are **light** (especially morning sunlight, which reaches retinal photoreceptors and signals to the SCN that the day has begun), **food** (each meal is a signal to the peripheral clocks of the liver and gut), **exercise** (morning activity anchors the clock; evening activity delays it), **temperature** (the body cools to begin sleep and warms to wake), and **social interaction** (less than the others, but real).

When those signals arrive on predictable schedules, the orchestra plays in harmony. Insulin sensitivity, cognitive function, blood pressure, hormonal secretion, immune activity, intestinal motility — all follow a coordinated score. When the signals arrive at unpredictable times, the orchestra goes off-key. The hepatic cell expects nutrients at eight in the morning and gets them at ten at night. The pancreas prepares for the breakfast insulin peak and receives a pizza at midnight. The heart, which should be in rest mode, gets an alert signal because of a tablet's blue light. That chronic state of contradictory signals between the SCN and the peripheral clocks has a name: **circadian disruption.**

The consequence of circadian disruption isn't generic malaise. It is a series of documented biochemical changes affecting exactly the same mechanisms we've discussed across the entire book — insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, uncontrolled blood pressure, visceral accumulation, immune dysregulation, accumulation of toxic proteins in the brain.

### The Cardiovascular Cost of a Broken Rhythm

Night-shift workers are the clearest human laboratory of circadian disruption. A meta-analysis in the *European Journal of Preventive Cardiology* (2018) showed that night work raises cardiovascular events by ~15% and cardiovascular mortality by up to 25%; UK Biobank analysis (238,000 participants) confirmed +11% events and +25% mortality. The 2025 AHA Scientific Statement formally recognized circadian health as an independent cardiometabolic risk factor.

But the most relevant data for the reader who probably is not a night-shift worker comes from Windred et al. (*Sleep* 2024, UK Biobank, 60,000 participants with accelerometry): **regularity of sleep is a stronger predictor of mortality than sleep duration itself.** Regular patterns were associated with 20–48% less risk of premature death, with the most pronounced reduction in cardiometabolic mortality.

In the MESA study (*Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis*), published in *JACC* in 2020 and reanalyzed in 2024, sleep timing variability measured by actigraphy prospectively predicted cardiovascular events. People whose sleep schedule varied by more than 90 minutes between days had significantly greater cardiovascular risk than people with regular schedules, even when adjusted for total sleep duration, traditional risk factors, and sleep disorders.

![Figure 14.2 — Regularity beats duration. 2×2 matrix showing relative mortality risk: regular + adequate sleep (reference 1.0), regular + inadequate (+15–20%), irregular + adequate (+25–30%), irregular + inadequate (+40–48%). Arrow highlights that irregular sleep with normal duration is worse than regular sleep with short duration.](../Cap14%20Fig02.PNG)

There is yet another layer — the diurnal variation in blood pressure. Normally, blood pressure rises in the morning, peaks in the afternoon, and falls during the night by about 10% of the daytime value. That nocturnal fall is called *dipping*. When it does not occur — *non-dipper* pattern — cardiovascular risk rises by about 15% for events and 22% for mortality. Among the many effects of sleep apnea, one of the best documented is precisely converting the *dipper* pattern into *non-dipper*: the heart never rests at night, blood pressure stays elevated when it should be low, and vascular risk silently accumulates.

The practical lesson: you don't have to work at night to suffer the damage of circadian misalignment. Schedule irregularity — going to bed at 10 p.m. on weekdays and 3 a.m. on weekends; waking at 6 a.m. some days and noon on others — is enough to activate part of the pathogenic mechanisms described in night workers. And untreated apnea can erase the benefit of regular hours.

### Sleep Architecture: What Happens in the Hours You Aren't Conscious

Sleeping isn't switching off. It is putting body and brain into functional modes that aren't possible during wakefulness. Sleep has architecture: a sequence of four to six cycles across the night, each about 90 minutes, alternating phases of deep non-REM sleep (also called N3 or slow-wave sleep) and REM sleep phases. Each phase has specific, irreducible functions.

**N3 (deep) sleep**, concentrated in the first half of the night, is when most physical repair occurs. Growth hormone is secreted in pulses. Muscular and bone tissues consolidate. The immune system performs maintenance. Declarative memory — facts, concepts — is consolidated. It is also the phase when the glymphatic system is most active.

**REM sleep**, concentrated in the second half of the night, is when emotional consolidation occurs, experiences are processed, and most vivid dreams happen. Brain activity in REM is similar to wakefulness; the body is paralyzed. It is the phase when procedural learning — skills, patterns — is consolidated. REM is also a phase sensitive to alcohol, cannabis, and many antidepressants, which partially suppress it.

When someone sleeps six hours instead of seven and a half, most of what they lose is REM — concentrated in the last hours. When someone fragments sleep with frequent awakenings, they mainly lose N3, which depends on the continuous progression of the cycle to deepen. Sleep quality isn't only "how many hours did I sleep." It is "which phases did I reach and maintain." In Paulo's case, the polysomnography showed something specific: total sleep of six hours fifteen minutes, but with N3 reduced to less than 5% of the total — when normal for his age is 15 to 20%. Each apnea episode, by fragmenting sleep before N3 could deepen, hijacked the most restorative phase of the night. Paulo was sleeping, but not resting.

### The Glymphatic System: Why Your Night Cleans Your Brain

In 2013, Maiken Nedergaard's team at the University of Rochester published in *Science* a paper that redefined our understanding of sleep. Using two-photon microscopy in mice, they demonstrated that during sleep the brain's interstitial space expands by about 60%, allowing cerebrospinal fluid to flow much more efficiently through brain tissue, removing metabolic waste accumulated during wakefulness — including, among others, the beta-amyloid protein. That drainage system was named the **glymphatic system**, by analogy to the lymphatic system the rest of the body has and the brain formally lacks.

The discovery had profound implications. Subsequent research in humans, using contrast-enhanced MRI and indirect *clearance* measures, confirmed that the glymphatic system operates primarily during slow-wave sleep (N3), and that its efficiency drops dramatically with sleep deprivation. A study published in January 2026 in *Nature Communications* demonstrated, for the first time in humans, that glymphatic *clearance* during a normal night of sleep raises morning plasma levels of Alzheimer's biomarkers — beta-amyloid and tau — compared with a night of deprivation. In other words: while you sleep well, the brain is literally discarding toxic proteins that, accumulated over decades, form the plaques of Alzheimer's disease.

The implication is direct. The pattern of fragmented and insufficient sleep across the last twenty years of adult life isn't a "discomfort." It is an accumulated failure in a fundamental cleaning process. People with untreated sleep apnea, chronic insomnia, or irregular shift work are accumulating waste in a system whose cleaning capacity already declines with age on its own. In Chapter 3, when we discussed Alzheimer's as "type 3 diabetes" and neurodegeneration as the result of multiple converging factors, sleep entered as one of the accelerators. Now we have the detailed mechanism: compromised glymphatic *clearance* allows beta-amyloid accumulation; sustained accumulation across decades produces the disease.

In Paulo's case, the issue was exactly that. N3 reduced to less than 5% of the total meant a glymphatic system underused for years, probably decades. The regression in testosterone and the rise in CRP were just the visible markers of a larger process. His brain, silently, was losing nights of cleaning.

### Sleep Apnea: The Forgotten Diagnosis of Medicine

Obstructive sleep apnea is the most clinically relevant sleep disorder — and also the most underdiagnosed. It is estimated that between 20 and 30% of adult men and 10 to 15% of adult women have sleep apnea, and the absolute majority have never been formally investigated. The classic signs are loud snoring, breathing pauses observed by a partner, excessive daytime sleepiness, nighttime awakenings with a sense of choking. But often the signs are more subtle: hard-to-control hypertension, new-onset atrial fibrillation, unexplained low testosterone, morning fatigue even with eight hours in bed, teeth grinding, frequent middle-of-the-night bathroom trips. Apnea is one of the leading hidden causes of resistant high blood pressure and low testosterone in men over 40.

And there is one of the best anthropometric predictors, as simple as a tape measure and as undervalued as the waist-to-height ratio: **neck circumference**. Above 16 inches (40 cm) in men and 14 inches (35 cm) in women, the risk of apnea rises substantially. The neck-to-height ratio (circumference in cm divided by height in meters) is a still better refinement, with a preventive cutoff around 21 for men and 20 for women. It is a measurement any patient can do at home in thirty seconds, and that precedes the formal polysomnography diagnosis by years.

The biological consequence is severe and cumulative. During each apnea episode — which can occur hundreds of times per night in moderate to severe cases — oxygen saturation drops, heart rate spikes, cortisol rises, blood pressure spikes, and sleep is fragmented before deep phases complete. The result is a night that appears to have happened but didn't fulfill its functions. The glymphatic system didn't clean. The heart didn't rest. Physical repair didn't occur. Hormones didn't rebalance.

The clinical management is straightforward: suspicion leads to polysomnography. Confirmed diagnosis leads to treatment — the most effective is continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), which normalizes nighttime breathing and reverses most of the consequences. Some mild cases can be treated with oral appliances or weight changes. Initial resistance to CPAP is common — *"I'm not going to sleep with a mask"* — but adaptation is possible in most patients, and patients who adhere often report, within a few weeks, the first real night of sleep in years.

For Paulo, CPAP was prescribed after polysomnography confirmation. Adaptation took about three weeks — he hesitated, tried sleeping without the device on the weekend, returned to using it. Persistence paid off. By the fourth week, for the first time since his early 30s, he described the sensation of waking rested. *"I didn't know good sleep was this."*

### Sleep Hygiene: What Works and What Is Myth

The expression "sleep hygiene" has become magazine cliché — and much of what is sold under that name has little evidence. I'll highlight what has solid support in the literature and what can be discarded without loss.

**What works, in approximate order of impact:**

**Absolute schedule regularity.** As we saw, consistency of bedtime and wake time is the strongest predictor of health outcomes. Going to bed at 10:30 p.m. every night, including weekends, is more important than sleeping eight hours with three-hour variability between days. The body responds to predictability.

**Morning sun exposure**, within the first 60 minutes after waking. Ten to twenty minutes outdoors or near a wide window, without sunglasses, robustly synchronizes the SCN. It is the highest-cost-benefit intervention: free, fast, with measurable effect within days.

**Progressive evening dimming** in the last two to three hours before bed. Low, warm, indirect lighting. If possible, avoid blue light from screens. If impossible, reduce brightness and use night mode. Endogenous melatonin begins to be secreted when ambient light drops; intense light at night suppresses that secretion.

**Cool ambient temperature** — between 65 and 68°F (18–20°C). The body needs to lose about 1.8°F (1°C) of internal temperature to begin sleep. A warm bedroom sabotages that mechanical process.

**Eating window closed at least two to three hours before bed.** Active digestion during sleep compromises quality and fragments the cycle. In Chapter 8, Marcos had the habit of having dinner at 10 p.m.; one of the most productive adjustments was moving the last meal to before 8 p.m.

**Caffeine limited to the first half of the day.** Caffeine's half-life is five to six hours in adults — half is still in the system six hours later. A coffee at 4 p.m. means relevant levels at midnight. For those sleeping poorly, removing caffeine after 2 p.m. frequently solves half the problem without the patient noticing the connection.

**Alcohol reduced or avoided**, especially in the three hours before bed. Alcohol helps falling asleep but destroys architecture — it suppresses REM, fragments the cycle, increases awakenings in the second half of the night. It is one of the main hidden saboteurs of sleep in midlife adults who don't perceive themselves as "sleeping poorly."

**What doesn't have strong evidence or is overestimated:**

Miracle teas, commercial herbal *blends* without dose standardization, and most sleep apps that promise "optimized cycles" with smart alarms. "Sleep supplements" with generic ingredients and sub-therapeutic doses generally have no effect beyond placebo. Melatonin deserves its own discussion of dose and timing, treated in detail in the pharmacotherapy section ahead — I'll only foreshadow that the high commercial doses (5–10 mg) are widely sold but poorly effective in the way patients expect.

For supplementation with some evidence in adults with sleep difficulty that isn't apnea, two agents deserve case-by-case consideration: **L-theanine** (200 mg, calming effect via GABA) and **apigenin** (50 mg, a flavonoid with affinity for GABA receptors). **Magnesium**, already incorporated into the basic supplementation of Chapter 8, has a specific adjunctive role for sleep in glycinate form (200–400 mg, 30–60 minutes before bed), particularly in patients with relative deficiency. None is a miracle. All work at the margins. None replaces adequate sleep hygiene — and none replaces apnea treatment when present.

**Aromatherapy for sleep.** Ambient lavender before bed has modest evidence in institutionalized older adults and in adults under acute stress — small but consistent signal across several trials. It enters as an optional adjunct in the pre-sleep routine, not as a principal intervention given the pillars discussed in this chapter.

### The Environment You Live In Is Part of Your Metabolism

The discussion of sleep and circadian rhythm frequently stops at the bedroom door. But the quality of the broader environment in which the patient lives — the air they breathe for sixteen hours a day, the water they drink, the noise around them, the light they're exposed to throughout the workday — has continuous and cumulative biological impact, adding to the chronic inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and circadian disruption this book has been mapping. For Paulo, the focus stayed on the bedroom and on regularity — that was where the bottleneck was. For other patients, the bottleneck is at the office, in the commute, or in the neighborhood, and the bedroom is already in order.

**Air quality** matters. Environmental pollution (PM2.5, nitrogen dioxide, ozone) is classified by the IARC as carcinogenic and a proven cause of cardiovascular disease. But *indoor* air is frequently worse than outdoor — and patients don't know it. Mold behind furniture, deficient ventilation, a smoker at home, aggressive air fresheners and cleaning products, seasonal wildfire smoke that reaches urban centers: all contribute to chronic inflammatory respiratory load. The practical measures are cheap and measurable — HEPA filters, daily cross-ventilation, humidity monitoring, bedroom away from heavy-traffic streets. It isn't luxury; it is documented exposure reduction.

**Water quality** is frequently assumed. In large urban centers, tap water passes through adequate treatment, but contains residual chlorine and frequently traces of metals from household plumbing — especially in older properties with lead solder. In areas where water comes from a well, quality testing has stopped being fastidious and has become minimum prudence: arsenic, nitrate, and bacteria are routinely identifiable contaminants. An activated-carbon filter resolves chlorine and organochlorines; reverse osmosis covers heavy metals. Annual testing by an accredited lab costs less than many tests we order without thinking — and crosses directly with the toxic panel discussed in Chapter 11.

**Noise** is an underestimated cardiovascular stressor. The WHO published 2018 guidelines recognizing noise pollution as an independent cardiovascular risk factor, with cutoffs of 53 dB during the day and 45 dB at night. Homes on heavy-traffic avenues, near perennial construction, or in neighborhoods with intense nighttime traffic expose residents to continuous inflammatory and sympathetic load — elevated cortisol, elevated heart rate during sleep, *non-dipper* pressure. Moving isn't always feasible, but simple measures — acoustically sealed windows, white-noise machines, ear protectors for sleep — measurably mitigate the impact.

**Lighting** deserves specific treatment beyond what we've already seen. Morning sunlight, as discussed, anchors the SCN. But ambient light throughout the entire day also matters. Workers who spend the day under cool-spectrum artificial lighting (offices with fluorescents or cool LEDs) without real sun exposure have worse circadian consolidation, worse vitamin D synthesis, and worse cortisol regulation. Prioritizing outdoor breaks, working near wide windows, and taking part of lunch in direct sun exposure are free interventions with cumulative effects.

The environment isn't background. It is an active biological variable — and part of the protocol.

### Chrono-Eating: When You Eat Matters as Much as What You Eat

In Chapter 8 we briefly introduced the concept of time-restricted eating (TRE — *time-restricted feeding*): concentrating all daily caloric intake in an 8- to 10-hour window, leaving 14 to 16 hours of overnight fasting. The evidence for metabolic benefits is solid: improved glucose tolerance, reduced triglycerides, reduced inflammatory markers, even reversal of hepatic steatosis — all without calorie reduction. The mechanism is in part circadian.

The logic is the following: the peripheral clocks of the liver, pancreas, and intestine are strongly synchronized by meal *timing*. When the person eats aligned with the active phase of the day — say, between 8 a.m. and 6 p.m. — those clocks are in phase with the central SCN and the metabolic machinery operates in harmony. When the person eats at any time, including late at night, the peripheral clocks become desynchronized from the central one. The same meal eaten at 10 a.m. and at 10 p.m. produces very different glycemic, insulinemic, and hepatic responses — the late meal is worse on almost every parameter.

The practical recommendation I use with most patients is simple: concentrate meals in a 10- to 12-hour window, ending at least two to three hours before bed. For Paulo, whose routine included meetings with international teams across time zones, the agreement was: eating window from 8 a.m. to 7 p.m. as the standard, with flexibility on travel days, but dinner always before 8 p.m. on home days.

### Chronopharmacology: Why the Timing of Medication Matters

A last, less-known aspect deserves mention. The time at which a medication is administered can significantly change its efficacy and side-effect profile. That science is called **chronopharmacology**, and it is maturing rapidly.

A few practical examples: **statins** with short half-lives like simvastatin have greater efficacy when taken at night, because hepatic cholesterol synthesis peaks in the early morning hours. Long-half-life statins like atorvastatin and rosuvastatin can be taken any time, because serum levels remain stable. **Antihypertensives** have mixed evidence on nighttime versus morning administration — the MAPEC trial suggested benefit from nighttime administration in reducing cardiovascular events, but the TIME trial, published in *The Lancet* in 2022, did not confirm. Current recommendation is to individualize based on the patient's pressure pattern (24-hour ABPM) and personal preference.

**Systemic corticosteroids**, when prescribed long-term, should be taken in the morning to mimic the physiological cortisol peak and suppress the HPA axis less. **Thyroid hormone** (levothyroxine) on a morning empty stomach, 30 to 60 minutes before breakfast, for ideal absorption. **Sedative medications** at night, obviously, but also **melatonin** timed to the goal — sleep induction or phase adjustment.

This kind of detail rarely appears in package inserts or in hurried visits, but it makes measurable clinical difference over time.

### Paulo: Six Months Later

Six months after the apnea diagnosis and CPAP initiation, Paulo returned for a complete evaluation. In addition to CPAP, we added a circadian protocol: bedtime at 10:45 p.m. and wake at 6:15 a.m., including weekends; morning light exposure for fifteen minutes immediately after waking; eating window until 7 p.m. on home days; coffee only until noon; alcohol reduced to no more than two glasses of wine per week; training always in the daytime; bedroom darkened and at 66°F (19°C).

The results: CRP from 1.3 fell to 0.6. Total testosterone rose from 410 to 540 (above what he had reached at six months in Chapter 11). Free testosterone, from 8.4, rose to 12.1. HbA1c, from 5.5, fell to 5.1. Blood pressure on ABPM confirmed an adequate *dipper* pattern — pressure was again falling at night. Epigenetic age, which had stagnated in the previous two years after the initial gain, resumed its slowing and dropped another year. On follow-up polysomnography with titrated CPAP, the AHI fell from 22 to 2, and N3 rose to 17% of total sleep time — within the expected range for his age.

What Pillar C had begun, Chapter 14 completed. Testosterone hadn't regressed due to some mysterious hormonal involution. It had regressed because an unidentified apnea, operating at the edges of all the other pillars, was silently sabotaging every gain made on other fronts. **Pillar S — sleep, rhythm, and recovery — isn't an optional fifth pillar. It is the soil in which the other three pillars grow.**

### Modern Sleep Pharmacotherapy

When the sleep hygiene plan, circadian adjustment, and apnea treatment don't resolve the problem — or while they are being implemented — there is a pharmacological layer that has been completely renewed in the last five years. It is good news, because the era of benzodiazepines for insomnia — clonazepam, alprazolam, used as "the sleep pill" — is ending. And it has been replaced by classes with substantially better safety profiles.

**The newest class is called DORA** — short for "dual orexin receptor antagonist." Orexin is a neuropeptide produced in the hypothalamus that **promotes wakefulness**. Drugs in this class don't sedate, like the older medications. Instead, they selectively block the "stay awake" signal, allowing natural sleep to set in. Three DORAs are approved internationally.

**Lemborexant (Dayvigo)**, from Eisai, is FDA-approved in the U.S. since 2019 and was approved by Anvisa in Brazil in September 2025 — it is the first DORA available in the Brazilian market. Usual dose: 5 to 10 mg, taken thirty minutes before bed. Large clinical trials (the SUNRISE study) showed improvement both in time to fall asleep and in maintenance of sleep through the night, with a safety profile superior to classic hypnotics. **Daridorexant (Quviviq)**, from Idorsia, is the most recent and has a shorter half-life — less morning grogginess — FDA-approved in the U.S. in 2022; not yet available in Brazil. **Suvorexant (Belsomra)**, from Merck, was the first DORA approved by the FDA in the U.S.; current status in Brazil should be confirmed at each new printing of the book.

The advantages of the DORA class over benzodiazepines and over the so-called Z-drugs (zolpidem, zopiclone) are concrete. They preserve sleep architecture, including REM sleep. They have lower risk of classic physical dependence. Lower fall risk in older adults. And they don't leave the morning cognitive hangover that benzodiazepines frequently deliver. Main contraindications: narcolepsy (DORAs worsen cataplexy), simultaneous use of strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (ketoconazole, clarithromycin, some antivirals), and pregnancy or lactation — where safety data are still limited.

**Melatonin — with honesty about the dose.** Melatonin is the hormone that signals to the body it is time to sleep, produced by the pineal gland at night. It is sold over the counter in the U.S. and, since Anvisa regulated it in 2021, also in Brazil — typically in doses of 3 mg, 5 mg, 10 mg — sometimes more. Few patients know that **those high doses are not more effective as hypnotics, only more expensive**. The evidence shows that melatonin works well as a **zeitgeber** — a circadian signal that resets the internal clock — at doses much lower than the commercial ones: 0.3 to 0.5 mg, taken four to five hours before the desired bedtime, helps advance the sleep phase in those with circadian delay (adolescents, late-phase people, jet-lagged patients). As a direct hypnotic — "make me fall asleep now" — melatonin is less effective than marketing suggests. Small dose, right timing, modest expectations.

**Glycine.** A common amino acid, sold in powder or capsule form, no prescription needed. At a dose of 3 grams taken just before bed, studies show improvement in subjective sleep quality and next-morning mood. The mechanism appears to be distal vasodilation — the hands and feet warm a little — which facilitates the central temperature drop accompanying falling asleep. Cheap, safe, with modest but consistent evidence.

**What was left in the past.** A clear line is worth drawing. Benzodiazepines — clonazepam (Klonopin), bromazepam, alprazolam (Xanax) — used chronically as a "sleeping pill" are bad medicine since at least 2015. They cause physical dependence, tolerance, fragmentation of sleep architecture (suppress REM), cognitive hangover, increased fall and fracture risk in older adults, and are associated in population studies with greater dementia risk when used for years. The same applies to chronic Z-drugs — zolpidem (Ambien), zopiclone (Lunesta) — in older adults. None of them is a modern choice for chronic insomnia.

Chronic insomnia deserves a specific diagnosis and a structured plan — always starting with circadian rhythm, sleep hygiene, investigation of apnea, and of psychological causes, as we saw across Paulo's arc. Once that base is in place and something is still missing, modern pharmacology has tools that can help without leaving a mark. Different from the previous generation, which helped for a few weeks and charged dearly afterward.

### The Essential in 60 Seconds

- The circadian system is governed by the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus and synchronized by *zeitgebers* — light, food, exercise, temperature, social interaction. Chronic misalignment (circadian disruption) produces documented biological consequences: insulin resistance, inflammation, *non-dipper* hypertension, immune dysfunction, accumulation of toxic proteins in the brain.

- Sleep schedule regularity is a stronger predictor of mortality than sleep duration (Windred et al., *Sleep* 2024, UK Biobank, 60,000 participants). Schedule irregularity of more than 90 minutes is associated with elevated cardiovascular risk even in people with adequate duration.

- The glymphatic system, discovered by Nedergaard and colleagues (*Science* 2013), operates primarily during deep sleep and removes beta-amyloid and other toxic proteins. Sleep deprivation directly compromises this mechanism — and recent human evidence (*Nature Communications* 2026) confirms *clearance* during normal sleep.

- Obstructive sleep apnea is underdiagnosed in 70–80% of cases. Signs: snoring, breathing pauses, resistant blood pressure, morning fatigue, unexplained low testosterone, regression of optimized markers. Treatment (CPAP) reverses most consequences and is frequently the highest-impact adjustment in midlife patients.

- What works: schedule regularity, morning sunlight, evening dimming, cool temperature, eating window closed 2–3 hours before bed, caffeine before 2 p.m., reduced alcohol. Supplementation with evidence in specific cases: magnesium glycinate/L-threonate, L-theanine, apigenin. Melatonin in small doses (0.3–0.5 mg) for phase adjustment, not as a sedative at high dose.

- Chrono-eating: concentrate intake in 10–12 hours, aligned with the active phase of the day, ending 2–3 hours before bed. Improves glucose tolerance, reduces triglycerides and inflammation even without caloric reduction.

- Modern sleep pharmacotherapy has left the benzodiazepine era. The DORA class blocks orexin — the wakefulness signal — without sedating or leaving a hangover; lemborexant (Dayvigo) is FDA-approved since 2019 and Anvisa-approved in Brazil since September 2025, the first DORA available there. Melatonin in small dose (0.3–0.5 mg) works as a circadian signal, not as an effective hypnotic. Glycine 3 g at bedtime is a safe adjunct with modest evidence. Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs in chronic use are bad medicine.
