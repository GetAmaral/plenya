***Pillar C — Clinical Optimization***

## Chapter 10 — Biochemical and Hormonal Panels — What Blood Says Before the Symptom

Paulo was 48 when he walked into the office for the first time. A tech executive, two children, married for twenty years, hitting the gym four times a week. No specific complaints. *"I'm fine — I just want to understand if I'm optimized."* That was exactly the word he used.

I ordered the full panel. When the results came back, one number didn't fit with the rest. Total testosterone: 310 ng/dL. Free testosterone: 4.8 pg/mL. By the lab's reference range, total testosterone below 300 is hypogonadism; above that is "normal." Paulo was within range — but only barely. And his free testosterone, which is the biologically active form, was at a value typically seen in men in their 70s.

No apparent symptoms. Libido preserved. Reasonable energy. Muscle strength — at least the strength he could measure at the gym — stable. But under that appearance of normality, his endocrine system was aging much faster than the calendar.

When I showed him the numbers, he asked the question I hear every week: *"But if I don't have symptoms, does it matter?"*

It matters. Because low testosterone isn't an isolated number — it correlates with mortality, cardiovascular risk, loss of muscle mass, cognitive decline, and quality of life. A 2024 meta-analysis in the *Annals of Internal Medicine* (more than 24,000 men, at least five years of follow-up) showed that total testosterone below 213 ng/dL was associated with greater all-cause mortality; below 153 ng/dL, the risk extended to fatal cardiovascular events. It wasn't only symptoms — it was trajectory.

And Paulo wasn't an exception. He was the pattern. Male testosterone begins falling about 1 to 2% per year after 30, and the decline accelerates after 40. Most 50-year-old men have been losing testosterone for more than a decade without knowing it. Many only find out when symptoms explode — fatigue, erectile dysfunction, loss of muscle mass, low mood. But the decline started twenty years earlier.

![Figure 10.1 — The Testosterone No One Was Measuring. Descending curve of average total testosterone by age group (20 to 80), with Paulo (48 years, 310 ng/dL) highlighted as a blue dot — crossing the population mean line at the typical value of men 80+.](../Cap10%20Fig01.PNG)

Paulo's case is the entry point to this chapter's theme: the biochemical panel that precedes the symptom. Hormones, the reasoning around male and female replacement, and specific decisions about bone health enter here — all read together, because modern diagnosis doesn't come from an isolated number, it comes from the pattern multiple numbers form together.

### The Hormonal Panel: More Than Testosterone

Evidence-based hormonal management — not fashion-based — starts from a simple premise: hormones are biochemical signals that coordinate the entire body. When they fall, rise, or fall out of balance, the effects spread across systems that are rarely connected in the conventional office.

The minimum panel I consider essential for men over 40 includes: total and free testosterone, SHBG (sex hormone-binding globulin), estradiol, DHEA-S, morning cortisol, TSH with free T3 and free T4, and IGF-1. For women over 40, I add estradiol in the follicular phase or postmenopause, progesterone when applicable, and FSH.

**Testosterone and SHBG.** Total testosterone measures everything circulating in the blood. But the fraction that matters is the free one — that which isn't bound to transport proteins and can enter cells. SHBG is the main one of those proteins; when elevated, it sequesters testosterone and reduces the active fraction, even with "normal" total testosterone. The work of Yeap and colleagues, published in *JCEM* in 2021, demonstrated that testosterone inversely and SHBG directly associate with all-cause mortality in men — meaning risk grows both when testosterone falls and when SHBG rises, and an isolated number can hide the other. Ordering one without the other is reading half the report.

**Estradiol.** A hormone usually thought of as "female," but men need it. Very low estradiol in men is associated with bone loss, mood worsening, and sexual dysfunction; very high, with gynecomastia and fluid retention. The optimal range for men sits between 20 and 40 pg/mL. Most physicians don't order it. When testosterone is prescribed without monitoring estradiol, the result is an optimization that can become a problem.

**DHEA-S.** DHEA is a hormonal precursor produced by the adrenals. Its sulfate (DHEA-S) is the most stable form in the blood, and falls predictably with age. Low levels are associated with worse cognitive function, mood, and body composition. Replacement has weaker evidence than for testosterone, but measuring it is useful for understanding the overall state of the adrenals and for monitoring response to interventions.

**Morning cortisol.** Cortisol follows a circadian rhythm: high in the morning (to wake up), low at night (to sleep). Measuring just the morning value gives a good read of the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. Low morning cortisol can indicate functional adrenal exhaustion; chronically high cortisol, persistent stress-axis activation. Both matter — and context counts. In Chapter 12, we'll see how the HPA axis is central to integrating mind and body; here, it enters as an objective biomarker.

**Thyroid: why TSH isn't enough.** Conventional medicine orders only TSH and considers that, if it's between 0.4 and 4.5 mIU/L, all is well. But TSH is a pituitary hormone that stimulates the thyroid to produce T3 and T4. What matters to the body is the final thyroid hormones — especially free T3, the most active in the cells. There are patients with "normal" TSH and persistently low free T3, with all the symptoms of subclinical hypothyroidism: fatigue, cold intolerance, hair loss, constipation, difficulty losing weight. Ordering TSH alone is like measuring pressure at the faucet without checking whether water is coming out. For a complete thyroid evaluation, the minimum is TSH + free T4 + free T3. Anti-TPO and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies enter when there is suspicion of autoimmune thyroiditis.

**IGF-1: the inverted U-curve.** Insulin-like growth factor 1 is produced primarily by the liver in response to growth hormone. Very low levels are associated with frailty, sarcopenia, and functional decline. Very high levels are associated with accelerated proliferative processes — including greater risk of some cancers and, in centenarian studies, exceptional survival appears to correlate with IGF-1 in the medium-low range. Unlike testosterone, whose goal is always to reach the optimal range, IGF-1 follows an inverted U-curve: neither too low nor too high. The target range varies with age, but the principle stands.

![Figure 10.2 — IGF-1: the curve's exception — neither too low nor too high. U-curve of relative longevity risk against IGF-1 (horizontal axis, 40–230 ng/mL). High risk at both ends (frailty/sarcopenia below 80; proliferative acceleration and oncologic risk above 200) and the optimal longevity zone in the central range (120–160 ng/mL). Unlike other biomarkers, here the "lower is better" logic does not apply.](../Cap10%20Fig02.PNG)

**Prolactin.** A pituitary hormone usually remembered only in lactation contexts. In clinical practice, elevated prolactin outside pregnancy — in men or women — can suppress the gonadal axis, lower testosterone, cause sexual dysfunction, menstrual changes, and even signal a pituitary microadenoma. In patients with unexplained erectile dysfunction, unexplained low libido, or associated thyroid changes, measuring prolactin is essential. The normal range goes up to about 20 ng/mL; persistently above that, without pregnancy or medications that explain it (antipsychotics, some antidepressants), warrants investigation.

**DHT (dihydrotestosterone).** An active metabolite of testosterone, converted by the enzyme 5-alpha-reductase. DHT is responsible for classic androgenic effects — libido, body hair growth, prostate effects — and measuring it helps interpret specific pictures: early androgenetic alopecia, symptomatic prostatic hyperplasia, persistent acne in adult women, or pre-treatment evaluation with 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors. It isn't a routine test, but when the clinical picture points to excess or deficient androgenic action, DHT is the measure that closes the diagnosis.

**Progesterone — not just a reproductive hormone.** In women with preserved cycles, luteal-phase progesterone (days 19–21) indicates whether ovulation was adequate: below 3 ng/mL suggests anovulation; above 5–10 ng/mL confirms ovulation. In women in the menopausal transition, the drop in progesterone often precedes the drop in estradiol by several years and can cause early symptoms — fragmented sleep, rising anxiety, shortened cycles, breast tenderness. Recognizing that pattern is fundamental for therapeutic decisions in women between 38 and 50 starting to have "nonspecific" symptoms. In postmenopausal women on hormone therapy, progesterone is also part of the decision — always in an individualized context with clear risk-benefit reasoning. In men, progesterone is rarely measured — only in specific contexts.

The general logic of the hormonal panel: it isn't about treating isolated numbers, but about recognizing patterns. Low testosterone with high SHBG and low DHEA-S draws a different picture from isolated low testosterone. TSH at the upper limit with low free T3 and positive anti-TPO is another picture. Low morning cortisol with low DHEA-S suggests functional adrenal fatigue, which calls for a completely different intervention from chronically elevated cortisol. Isolated elevated prolactin changes the entire workup. Each combination has a different treatment — and that is why measuring one hormone without measuring the others leads to wrong decisions.

### The Replacement Dilemma: When to Treat, When Not To

Testosterone has dropped. The other markers line up. The patient is in the suboptimal range. Treat or not?

The decision isn't automatic. For decades, testosterone replacement was sold as an "elixir of youth" or feared as a cardiovascular villain. Both extremes are wrong.

The study that finally gave the most solid answer was **TRAVERSE**, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2023 — a randomized trial with more than 5,200 men with hypogonadism and elevated cardiovascular risk, followed for nearly three years. The primary endpoint: testosterone replacement did not increase the risk of major cardiovascular events (heart attack, stroke, cardiovascular death) compared to placebo. Replacement is cardiovascularly neutral in men with appropriate indication and adequate monitoring.

But TRAVERSE also showed that replacement slightly increased the risk of atrial fibrillation (3.5% vs. 2.4%, p=0.02), acute kidney injury, and pulmonary embolism. And earlier studies had already demonstrated rises in hematocrit — which requires regular monitoring and, in some cases, phlebotomy. It is not a cosmetic treatment. It is a medical intervention requiring careful indication and close follow-up.

**When do I consider replacement?** When there are clinical symptoms of hypogonadism (persistent fatigue, sexual dysfunction, refractory loss of muscle mass) and consistently low laboratory values on at least two morning measurements, and other causes have been excluded (depression, hypothyroidism, severe obesity, opioid use, sleep apnea). Never on a single measurement. Never without first trying to optimize what can be optimized without medication — sleep, body composition, strength training, chronic stress reduction, correction of vitamin D and zinc deficiencies.

**When do I not consider replacement?** When the patient is in the suboptimal range but without symptoms, or when symptoms can be explained by other factors. In Paulo's case, the first intervention was not a testosterone prescription. It was a sleep restructuring — he averaged six hours per night, with frequent micro-awakenings — optimization of vitamin D (which was at 24 ng/mL, rose to 52 in four months), and an adjustment of strength training to include compound movements with progressive load. In four months, total testosterone climbed from 310 to 420 ng/dL and free from 4.8 to 8.1 pg/mL. No medication. Sometimes optimizing the soil resolves things before you need to touch the hormone.

The same logic, with important contextual differences, applies to hormone replacement in women in the menopausal transition and postmenopause — and that is the next theme of this chapter, because it deserves its own space.

### The Decision a Woman Has to Make in the Window

Fernanda came back to the office at 44. The metabolic biomarkers were still good — the structured work we did three years earlier was holding firm: insulin at 7, ApoB at 78, no liver fat. But she came in for a different reason.

*"Doctor, something is different."*

She spent the next ten minutes listing it. She had been sleeping poorly for over a year — waking three or four times a night, sometimes drenched in sweat. Her mood swung without explanation. She would get irritated by small things that didn't used to bother her. Her libido, always steady, had gone into free fall. Her menstruation, until then constant as a clock, had begun to shorten and skip. *"And my mother went through menopause at 42. I thought I still had time."*

Fernanda didn't. The age at which menopause arrives is one of the most heritable traits in existence — large-scale studies estimate that between 44% and 65% of variation among women is explained by genetics. If the mother entered menopause before 45, the daughter has a substantially greater chance of following the same path. It was the second inheritance Fernanda carried: first the metabolic one, now the reproductive one.

Her FSH was at 38 mIU/mL, a pattern of ongoing ovarian insufficiency. Estradiol at 28 pg/mL. Progesterone, measured in the luteal phase of a cycle that still appeared, barely reached 2 ng/mL. It wasn't the start of the transition. It was the transition installed — and early.

*"My biggest fear isn't the symptom,"* she said. *"It's what comes after. My mother fell at 67 and broke her wrist. At 68, her hip. The next fifteen years were pain and dependence. I don't want that path."*

This is where the medicine of the last two decades wrote, and then rewrote, one of the most important — and most poorly told — pages of women's health.

In 2002, a study called WHI (*Women's Health Initiative*) was stopped early because it showed that hormone therapy increased the risk of breast cancer, cardiovascular events, and stroke in postmenopausal women. The headline ran around the world. Doctors stopped prescribing. Patients stopped asking. An entire generation of women crossed menopause with the implicit message that hormones were dangerous.

Twenty years later, we know where the study erred — not in its findings, but in its sample. The average age of women in WHI was 63. Most had been postmenopausal for more than a decade. Many already had atherosclerotic plaques formed, hypertension, established dyslipidemia. The therapy was oral and used synthetic progestogens, which we now know have a worse risk profile than transdermal therapy with natural progesterone. And, more importantly: starting replacement late is biologically different from starting early.

Two later trials changed the conversation. ELITE, published in 2016, tested replacement in two groups — one that started within six years of the last menstrual period, the other that started only after ten. In the first group, estradiol reduced atherosclerosis progression. In the second, it didn't reduce it and, in some analyses, made it worse. KEEPS, with follow-up published in 2024, reached the same place: in the window, the hormone protects; outside it, it doesn't.

That is the central idea of modern hormonal medicine — the **window hypothesis**. A roughly ten-year period from the last menstrual period during which estradiol is protective. After that, the same molecule that would protect begins to do the opposite.

![Figure 10.3 — The window lasts about 10 years. Horizontal timeline in years since the last menstrual period with three bands: green (0–10 years, window of protection), amber (10–15 years, individual decision zone), red (15+, outside the window). Points with landmark trials: WHI (2002), ELITE (2016) in both cohorts, KEEPS (2024). Fernanda positioned within the green zone, at 44.](../Cap10%20Fig03.PNG)

With Fernanda, the decision was shared, as it always has to be. Risks were discussed; benefits too. I told her how I think about each piece of that conversation.

Estradiol today comes through the skin, not by mouth. Patches twice a week (Climara, Vivelle-Dot, Estradot) or gels applied daily (Divigel, EstroGel). Skin absorption skips first-pass metabolism in the liver, which substantially reduces thrombosis risk compared with the oral estradiol WHI used. It is, today, for the vast majority of patients starting HRT, the standard way to begin.

Progesterone comes separately, at night, in natural form — micronized, identical to what the body produces (Prometrium in the U.S., Utrogestan elsewhere; both available as generics). Modern studies have shown it is the formulation with the lowest signal of breast cancer risk, and at night it has a welcome second effect: it improves sleep. Never the synthetic progestogens used in WHI.

When the main symptom is vaginal — dryness, burning, pain with intercourse, recurrent urinary infections, things women almost never mention unless asked, and that today the international literature groups under the name **genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM)** — there is a more specific alternative: **local estrogen**. Vaginal creams or tablets applied two to three times a week: vaginal estradiol (Estrace, Vagifem, Imvexxy) or vaginal estriol (available outside the U.S.). Systemic absorption is minimal; the effect on the vaginal tissue is direct. It can be used alone, when only the genital area bothers, or added to systemic therapy.

What is not prescribed includes any replacement for women with active or recent breast cancer, endometrial cancer, active or severe past venous thrombosis, unexplored vaginal bleeding, or acute liver disease. These are absolute contraindications. In situations like migraine with aura, distant DVT history, or severe hypertriglyceridemia, the conversation is case by case, with risk-benefit calculation on the table — not an automatic blanket prohibition.

The rest is follow-up: symptoms, control serum estradiol, metabolic labs, mammography, and pelvic ultrasound per screening. More frequent review in the first year, annual after that.

With Fernanda, we decided together. A 0.05 mg estradiol patch, micronized progesterone 100 mg at night. Three months later, sleep had returned. Hot flashes, almost none. Mood, stable. The window was still open. We walked through it.

### Testosterone, Pellets, and Alternatives When Replacement Isn't an Option

An important detail we haven't yet discussed. Women also produce testosterone — at doses about ten times lower than men, but enough to matter for sexual desire, energy, muscle mass, and mood. Like estradiol, female testosterone falls in the transition. In some women, replacement of estradiol and progesterone resolves the vasomotor symptoms, sleep, and mood — but persistently low libido continues, even with estrogen well adjusted. That picture, technically called **hypoactive sexual desire disorder** (HSDD), is where female testosterone has evidence-supported indication.

A 2019 global consensus, endorsed by menopause and sexual medicine societies in several countries, set the ground: low-dose transdermal testosterone can be used in women with HSDD after confirming that estradiol and progesterone are well adjusted and other causes have been excluded. The dose is approximately one-tenth of the male dose. The serum target stays within the physiological range of women of reproductive age — not above it.

In the U.S., as in Brazil, **there is no testosterone formulation specifically approved for women** — neither by the FDA nor by Anvisa. When indication exists, the transdermal male form can be prescribed at fractioned dose — about one-tenth of the male dose, on a small area of skin — or compounded gel from a regulated compounding pharmacy. Quarterly serum monitoring is mandatory. Excess testosterone in women causes acne, hair loss, facial hair, and voice changes — and some of those effects, once installed, are difficult to reverse.

There are also compounded vaginal formulations — creams, suppositories, or thin films with low-dose testosterone, alone or combined with estradiol or estriol. Systemic absorption is lower than from pellets, and some patients report benefit in vulvovaginal atrophy associated with low libido. These remain compounded formulations, without a randomized clinical trial testing the formula — standardization caveats apply, even if the systemic risk here is lower than that of an implant.

A word about two formats that occupy space in the cosmetic market — **subcutaneous hormone pellets** and **gestrinone**. In both, the principle is the same: implants compounded at a pharmacy, with promises of energy, libido, and body composition. The regulatory context matters: **the FDA has never approved estradiol or testosterone pellets for menopause**; ACOG (2023 consensus), the Endocrine Society, and NAMS (the North American Menopause Society) converge that compounded bioidenticals should not be used routinely, especially due to dose variability and standardization differences across pharmacies. A recent cohort recorded about 58% adverse effects in pellet users versus 15% in those who used regulated products. **In Brazil**, the regulatory landscape changed twice in a few weeks at the end of 2024: in **October 2024**, Anvisa Resolution 3.915 broadly suspended the compounding, marketing, advertising, and use of compounded hormonal implants; on **November 22, 2024**, Resolution 4.353 revoked the previous ruling and established the current regime — **the use of implants based on anabolic steroids or androgenic hormones for cosmetic, athletic, or muscle-gain purposes remains prohibited** (the scenario in which gestrinone is typically offered), while compounded implants may be used exceptionally for legitimate clinical indication, with an active ingredient already evaluated by Anvisa, a medical prescription registered in the SNGPC, an ICD code, a responsibility waiver, and adverse-event reporting through the VigiMed system. For these reasons, the position I take in clinic is to opt for regimens with dose control and standardization (regulated transdermals); when a patient arrives already using a pellet or gestrinone, I prefer to measure levels, openly discuss the current regulatory landscape, and frequently offer migration to a regulated regimen.

And when HRT isn't an option?

Some women arrive with significant symptoms — intense hot flashes, fragmented sleep, swinging mood — but with a clear contraindication to hormone replacement. The most common is prior breast cancer. For these patients, recent medicine has opened two doors.

The first is a new class of **non-hormonal** medication, called NK3 receptor antagonists. The first representative, **fezolinetant** (brand name Veozah), was approved by the FDA in May 2023 specifically for vasomotor symptoms of menopause. It acts on the hypothalamus — the brain region that regulates temperature — and reduces about two to three hot flashes per day, with no hormonal action. One pill a day. It requires monitoring of liver enzymes, because there have been reports of liver toxicity with prolonged use. A second drug from the same family, elinzanetant, was approved more recently. In Brazil, the arrival of this class is still in the regulatory process.

The second door is older and more accessible: **low-dose antidepressants**, especially paroxetine and venlafaxine, have solid evidence of reducing hot flashes. Paroxetine in low dose was actually the first non-hormonal medication FDA-approved (as Brisdelle) specifically for menopausal vasomotor symptoms. They don't treat the entire menopause — they don't preserve bone, don't help skin, don't modify cardiovascular risk — but they relieve the vasomotor symptom in women who can't take HRT. They are inexpensive, widely available, and frequently underused for this indication.

For women who can't take hormone replacement, these tools don't substitute for HRT in everything — they don't preserve bone, don't modulate cardiovascular risk — but they ease the symptom that most disrupts daily life. It is the opposite of what medicine could offer twenty years ago.

### The Bone Decision: When to Treat and for How Long

One of the least-discussed chapters of women's preventive medicine is bone. Fernanda's mother, who appeared in earlier chapters as diabetic at 62 and whose early menopause at 42 was never treated, followed the path of many women of her generation: fell at 67, broke her wrist; at 68, her hip; spent the next fifteen years in pain and functional dependence. Fernanda doesn't want that path — and an important part of avoiding it involves specific decisions about bone medication.

Two questions precede treatment.

**The first is when.** Bone densitometry is the exam that defines whom we are talking about — the result is expressed in two numbers, T-score and Z-score. T-score below -2.5 in spine, femoral neck, or total hip is diagnostic of osteoporosis. Between -1.0 and -2.5 is osteopenia. But the isolated number doesn't decide — it needs to be read with context: age, prior fracture type (if any), the FRAX score (which combines density with risk factors like smoking, corticosteroid use, and family history), and — increasingly — the TBS (*trabecular bone score*), a vertebral texture analysis that complements the T-score.

The practical rule: **pharmacologic treatment is indicated in densitometric osteoporosis, in patients with a prior fragility fracture (even with "normal" T-score), or in patients with osteopenia plus elevated FRAX.** A patient with osteopenia and low calculated risk can be observed — with monitoring every two years and consistent lifestyle work.

**The second question is what to do before medication.** Three tools enter as foundation: sufficient calcium (preferably from diet — dairy, sardines with bones, dark leafy greens), **vitamin D** corrected to the optimal range (Chapter 4), and **vitamin K2** directing mobilized calcium to the bone instead of the artery (Chapter 8). Add to that **progressive strength training** and exercise with controlled impact — the mechanical stimuli that signal to the bone that it needs to be reinforced (Chapter 7). In a patient with uncorrected vitamin D deficiency, any anti-osteoporosis medication has limited efficacy.

When medication enters, there are five central options.

**Bisphosphonates** — alendronate (weekly oral), risedronate (weekly or monthly), and zoledronic acid (annual IV infusion) — are first-line. They inhibit cells that resorb bone. Large trials (FIT, HORIZON-PFT) showed significant reduction in vertebral and hip fractures. Old, cheap, generic. Two rare but important alerts: osteonecrosis of the jaw (especially after dental extraction with chronic use) and atypical femur fractures after prolonged use — hence the "drug holiday" after 5 years of oral alendronate or 3 years of IV zoledronic, with reassessment.

**Denosumab (Prolia)** is a monoclonal antibody injectable, given every six months subcutaneously. It blocks a protein called RANKL that commands resorptive cells. The FREEDOM trial and its ten-year extension showed robust fracture reduction. **Critical caveat that few patients know:** denosumab **cannot simply be stopped**. When withdrawn, there is a rebound phenomenon causing rapid bone loss and multiple vertebral fractures in the first year after discontinuation. Anyone finishing denosumab must transition to a bisphosphonate to lock in that rebound. This information is underdocumented in the office and justifies, on its own, a careful conversation before starting.

**Romosozumab (Evenity)** is an anti-sclerostin antibody, with a different mechanism from the previous ones: it is **anabolic** — that is, it builds bone instead of merely slowing resorption. Applied monthly subcutaneously, with treatment limited to twelve months. The ARCH and FRAME trials showed fracture reduction superior to alendronate. There is a signal of a slight increase in cardiovascular events in one of the trials, still under debate — which makes romosozumab an option for patients with recent fractures and low-to-moderate cardiovascular event risk, used for a short period.

**Teriparatide (Forteo)** and its newer variation, **abaloparatide (Tymlos)**, are also anabolics — analogues of parathyroid hormone, applied daily by subcutaneous injection. Typically used for 18 to 24 months in patients with severe osteoporosis or multiple fractures. High price and more restricted access.

**Raloxifene (Evista)** is a selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM) — acting as estrogen on bone and as an antagonist on breast tissue. It reduces vertebral fracture, with the additional benefit of reducing breast cancer risk in high-risk patients. It does, however, increase venous thrombosis risk. A reasonable option in postmenopause with a specific profile: osteoporosis with parallel breast concerns and without thromboembolic factors.

The logic of duration and sequencing is a fundamental part of treatment, and the physician experienced with osteoporosis has a trained eye for that choreography. One usually starts with an antiresorptive (bisphosphonate or denosumab) in most cases. In a recent fracture or severe osteoporosis, one considers starting with an anabolic (romosozumab or teriparatide) for a short period — and following with an antiresorptive to consolidate. What is never done is starting denosumab without a transition plan.

The bone responds to consistent care with method. What it doesn't respond to is the passive observation that arrives at the office after the first fracture.

The hormonal and biochemical panels are what separates the medicine that waits for the diagnosis to appear from the medicine that reads the signals before. But there is still a layer upstream — the genes that shape how the body responds to everything we've discussed so far, and the environmental exposures that, silently, dysregulate the entire panel. That is what the next chapter covers.
