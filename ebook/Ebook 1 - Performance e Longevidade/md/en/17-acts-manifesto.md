# PART V — CLOSING

## Chapter 17 — The ACTS Manifesto: A Letter to My Future Self

I have the same biological age as my patients.

That sounds obvious, but it took time to become a lived truth. When I started practicing medicine, the patients were "them" and I was the doctor — the one who guides, prescribes, follows up, but who observes aging from a different place, as if the process didn't apply to whoever wears the coat. It took years for me to understand that the HPA axis I measure in my patients also operates in me. That the cortisol that rises when Ana receives her boss's email on Sunday night rises when I receive the hospital's call on Saturday morning. That the sleep I monitor in Paulo with polysomnography and a circadian protocol is the same sleep I sacrifice when preparing a graduate-level lecture after a full day of clinic.

I am a nephrologist and clinical physician. I am also technical lead of a hemodialysis unit, coordinator of a nephrology residency, writer, lecturer, father, and husband. Each of those roles has its own calendar, its own pressure, its own deadline. If someone had asked me, ten years ago, whether I was caring for myself with the same diligence I demand of my patients, the honest answer would have been *no*.

The reason I write this book — and not only see patients in the office — is that "no" frightened me. Because if I, who know exactly what happens when sleep is insufficient, when training is irregular, when nutrition gives way to routine, and when stress becomes the permanent background landscape — if I know all that and still let the practice slip for months — I imagine what happens to those who don't have that information.

This chapter is a letter. Not to the reader — to my future self. But I write knowing you will read it. And perhaps recognize yourself.

---

### To my future self twenty years from now:

You will be around seventy. If the numbers I track today continue on the trajectory I chose, your arteries will be cleaner than those of most men your age. Your muscle mass will be preserved. Your cognition will be intact — or at least as intact as biology allows.

But none of that is guaranteed. It is construction. And construction requires that today's self do the work that tomorrow's self cannot do retroactively.

And here I need to name what, without a name, stays vague. In the longevity literature, Peter Attia draws a distinction that has stayed with me since I read it: there is fast death and there is slow death. Fast death is the heart attack in the parking lot, the stroke at breakfast, the accident — bad, yes, but acute, with no protagonist. Slow death is the other kind: ten, twelve, fifteen years of progressive decline. It begins with the difficulty of climbing stairs that used to be automatic. It moves through the small fall that breaks a hip. It becomes the dementia diagnosis that changes the address to a nursing home. It ends without anyone knowing exactly when it ended. Between the last conscious birthday and the end, the person and the family live a shrunken version of life — and, in practice, spend years burying someone who is still breathing.

Standard medicine almost never treats that second kind of death. We treat acute heart attack, acute stroke, acute cancer. But slow death happens in slow motion, without emergency, outside the urgency statistics. And it is the most common outcome today for those who live to 80 — exactly what this entire book tries to avoid.

The goal, then, isn't only to live longer. It is to live whole until the end, and to die fast. It is to compress the time between the last good day and the last day. Everything I wrote in the previous chapters — expanded panel, exercise, alimentation, sleep, mind-body integration — serves that final goal. The numbers are a means. The destination is the shape of the end.

I know — because I see it in my patients every day — that the body forgives a lot between 30 and 45. That the labs stay "normal" for years while insulin rises, inflammation grows, plaques deposit, sleep fragments, cortisol becomes chronic. I know that most people only wake up when the body stops compensating — and at that moment, the cost of reversing is ten times the cost of having prevented.

Ricardo almost died in a parking lot because no one asked the right questions in time. Marcos spent six years on a low-dose statin without anyone ordering a CAC — and when the result came, his arteries were nearly eighty. Fernanda was forty-one with a fatty liver without knowing. André trained ten hours a week and was aging faster on the inside than people who didn't train. Paulo lost months of progress because an apnea was hidden behind an apparently good panel. Ana optimized everything biochemistry allowed and CRP still wouldn't drop — because what needed treatment was the anxiety she called "personality."

I know those cases because I have been on the other side of the desk. But when I close the office and go home, I am a patient of the same processes. The same ApoB that needs monitoring. The same cortisol that rises with the multiple demands. The same sleep that needs the regularity routine doesn't always allow. The same need for exercise that competes with the fatigue of a day that began at six and ends at eleven.

The distance between knowing and doing is the gap this book tries to cross — not only for the reader, but for me.

---

What I have learned in practice — seeing patients over years, adjusting plans, erring, correcting, revisiting — fits in a few sentences. They are simple sentences. But they took a decade to become conviction.

**The first: the body does not wait for the diagnosis to fall ill.** Disease begins ten, fifteen, twenty years before the label. By the time glucose rises, insulin resistance has been there a decade. By the time the heart attack happens, the plaque has been growing two decades. The intervention window exists — but it closes. And it closes in silence.

**The second: no one changes everything at once.** The Rule of Two — two focuses per quarter, with a measure and a deadline — isn't only clinical technique. It is the only way I have seen work sustainably, in myself and in my patients. Whoever tries to change ten things on Monday usually has changed none by Friday. Whoever chooses two and holds them for three months builds habit. Built habit is changed trajectory.

**The third: the pillars don't work in isolation.** Alimentation without sleep is wear. Exercise without recovery is overtraining. Hormones without mental health is optimization on sand. The strength of the work is in the integration — and in the humility to recognize that no pillar alone saves when the others are collapsing.

**The fourth: partnership matters.** No patient I follow has done it alone. Ricardo needed cardiologist, internist, nutritionist, exercise specialist. Ana needed psychologist and psychiatrist alongside the clinician. Marcos needed a coach who understood the clinical context. Preventive medicine isn't the soliloquy of an enlightened physician — it is networked work, with the patient at the center and different professionals contributing what each one knows.

**The fifth, and perhaps the most important: the best moment to start was ten years ago. The second best is today.** It isn't a motivational phrase. It is clinical data. Reversibility decreases with time. The cost of inaction increases with age. Each year that passes without monitoring, without training, without sleeping properly, without caring for mental health, the body deposits one more layer of damage that will be harder to reverse later. But "harder" isn't "impossible." Marcos, at 57, with CAC of 412, changed his trajectory in eight months. Paulo, at 48, with undiagnosed apnea, recovered and exceeded the previous testosterone peak in six months of treatment. Age is never a reason not to start. It is a reason to start now.

---

To my future self twenty years from now: if you are reading this with a clear mind and a functional body, it is because today's self did the work. If you are not — if something gave way, if some window closed too early — it wasn't from lack of knowing. It was from lack of doing.

To the reader who has reached this point: what happens inside your body in the next ten years depends more on what you do now than on the genetics you received. You have the map. You have the method. You have the criteria to choose who will walk with you. What is missing is the decision.

> It doesn't have to be dramatic. It doesn't have to be perfect. It has to be today.
