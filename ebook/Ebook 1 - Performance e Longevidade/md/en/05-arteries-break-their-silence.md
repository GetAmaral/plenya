## Chapter 5 — The Arteries Break Their Silence

Marcos was 57, an engineer, and considered himself an exemplary patient. He had been on rosuvastatin 10 mg per day for six years. His LDL of 148 mg/dL had dropped to 78. Total cholesterol from 240 to 168. The cardiologist was satisfied. So was Marcos.

When I referred him for a non-contrast coronary CT — the coronary calcium score, a test he had never had — Marcos didn't see the need. *"But my cholesterol is controlled, doctor. The statin took care of it."*

The result came back with an Agatston score of 412. Marcos didn't know what that number meant. I did: his coronary arteries carried a calcified plaque burden equivalent to that of a man almost 80 years old. He was 57. His arteries were nearly 80.

The statin had lowered his LDL — but underneath that "controlled" number, the plaques were still there. Decades of accumulated atherogenic exposure before treatment began had left marks the lipid panel could not show.

Marcos went silent for almost a minute. Then he asked:

> *"So what I did over the last six years was for nothing?"*

It wasn't. The statin probably kept the score from being even higher and stabilized part of the existing plaques. But the question no one had asked was: what was the starting point? How much damage already existed when treatment began? Without that information, Marcos and his cardiologist were navigating without a map — treating a number in the blood without knowing what was inside the arteries.

The "controlled" cholesterol had created an illusion of safety. The calcium score revealed the reality.

---

### The Test That Photographs Your Arteries

Chapter 4 showed how blood biomarkers draw the map of what is happening inside your body. But blood tells the indirect story — it measures the forces that drive disease. The coronary calcium score does something different: it photographs the result. It shows directly the plaque already formed in the arteries.

The exam is simple: a non-contrast CT scan of the chest, no special prep, less than ten minutes. What it detects is the calcium deposited in atherosclerotic plaques in the coronary arteries. Where there is calcium, there is plaque. And where there is plaque, there were decades of silent atherosclerotic process — exactly the fire inside the arteries we described in Chapter 2.

The score is calculated by the Agatston method, developed in 1990 by American cardiologist Arthur Agatston. It is a single number summarizing total calcified plaque burden in the coronaries. That number has become one of the most validated tools in preventive cardiology, endorsed by American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association guidelines for risk stratification in asymptomatic adults.

---

### What Each Number Means

The calcium score isn't a "yes or no" test. It's a continuous scale — and each band tells a different story.

![Figure 5.1 — Coronary Calcium Score: What Each Band Means. Risk gradient scale (CAC=0 to ≥400) integrated with coronary CT images showing calcification progression, positioning of Ricardo (CAC≈187) and Marcos (CAC=412), and clinical management summarized by band.](../Cap05%20Fig01.PNG)

**CAC = 0: The Power of Zero.** No calcified plaque detected. This is the most powerful result in preventive cardiology. A meta-analysis of more than 29,000 patients demonstrated that people with CAC zero had a cardiovascular event rate of 0.47% over about four years — versus 4.14% in those with any calcification. Relative risk was reduced by 85%. If your CAC is zero, the probability of a cardiovascular event in the next 5 to 10 years is very low. In patients at intermediate risk who are undecided about starting a statin, a zero CAC may be the missing information that allows them to delay the medication and focus on lifestyle — safely. But pay attention: CAC zero does not mean absence of disease. Soft plaque — non-calcified, younger, potentially more unstable — does not show up on this exam. CAC zero enormously reduces risk. It does not eliminate it.

**CAC 1–99: Atherosclerosis Has Begun.** Mild plaque. Disease is present, but in early stage. Cardiovascular risk is modest, but no longer negligible. In this range, management depends on context: age, other risk factors, percentile relative to people of the same sex, age, and ethnicity. In young adults, a CAC of 30 may be more concerning than the same CAC of 30 in someone over 70 — because it indicates the disease started earlier.

**CAC 100–399: Significant Plaque Burden.** Moderate to extensive plaque. ACC/AHA guidelines recommend a statin for patients with CAC above 100, regardless of other risk factors. Cardiovascular event risk rises proportionally — each time the score doubles, risk rises about 14%.

**CAC ≥ 400: Equivalent to Secondary Prevention.** Extensive plaque. The National Lipid Association recommends intensive statin therapy for scores above 300. Above 400, management is comparable to that of a patient who has already had a cardiovascular event. Marcos, with his score of 412, was in this group. The statin was correct — but the dose, the intensity of treatment, and the therapeutic targets needed to be radically different from what he had been doing.

### Percentiles and Arterial Age: The Number the Patient Understands

The absolute Agatston score is important, but it doesn't tell the whole story. A CAC of 150 in a 45-year-old man is much more serious than the same score in a 75-year-old — because it indicates the disease progressed much faster than expected for age.

That is where percentiles from the MESA study come in — the *Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis*, which followed more than 6,800 asymptomatic adults across four ethnicities for over a decade. The MESA calculator compares a patient's calcium score to the expected distribution for people of the same sex, age, and ethnic group. If the result is above the 75th percentile, risk is significantly elevated. Above the 90th, it is markedly elevated.

For the patient, the most powerful concept is **arterial age**. *"Your calcium score is 412"* is a technical sentence. *"Your arteries look 20 years older than you"* is a sentence that changes behavior.

Marcos understood the CAC the moment I translated the number into age. *"You are 57. Your coronaries look almost 80."* No further explanation was needed.

### When to Do It — and for Whom

The calcium score is recommended for adults between 40 and 75 with intermediate cardiovascular risk — when there is doubt about starting or intensifying a statin. But there are populations who benefit especially: patients with strong family history of premature coronary disease, women whose risk is often underestimated by traditional calculators, and anyone whose treatment decision depends on more information than blood alone can provide.

It is not a test to repeat every year. In clinical practice, intervals of 3 to 5 years are reasonable for follow-up, unless a previous result demands closer monitoring. And it is not recommended for patients who have already had a cardiovascular event or who already have a clear indication for a statin — in these cases, the CAC result wouldn't change management.

---

### The Limitations You Need to Know

No exam is perfect, and the calcium score has two limitations worth knowing.

**The first: it detects only calcified plaque.** Soft plaque — lipid-rich, with a thin fibrous cap, potentially unstable — does not appear. That is precisely the type of plaque that causes most heart attacks, as we discussed in Chapter 2. A young patient with chronically elevated ApoB may have soft plaques accumulating in the arteries with no signal on the calcium score. That is why CAC zero is no license to ignore blood biomarkers. The power of zero is real — but it is the blood that anticipates what the CT cannot yet see.

**The second: statins can increase calcification.** This sounds contradictory, but it has logic: statins stabilize plaque, transforming inflammatory and lipid components into dense, stable calcium. Plaque calcified under statin is safer — less prone to rupture. But the Agatston score may rise, even with real risk reduction. In patients already on a statin, like Marcos, the CAC needs to be interpreted with that nuance. The number may be higher than it would be without treatment — but the plaque may be more stable.

### When CAC Isn't Enough: Coronary CT Angiography

The first limitation we just discussed — soft plaque invisible to the calcium score — has an available solution, with trade-offs that need consideration. It is **coronary CT angiography** (CCTA). A different exam from the calcium score: performed with intravenous iodinated contrast, it generates a three-dimensional image of the coronaries with a level of detail comparable to invasive coronary angiography — without the catheter and without admission.

What CCTA shows that CAC does not: **soft plaque.** That lipid-rich plaque, with a thin fibrous cap, that hasn't yet calcified. The plaque that worries us most, precisely because it is the one that ruptures most. CCTA also quantifies the degree of luminal stenosis — how much of the blood pathway is compromised — and characterizes plaque composition (predominantly lipid, fibrotic, mixed, calcified). It is the most complete anatomical evaluation of the coronaries available outside the cath lab.

The magnitude of the discordance between the two exams is striking. In the *Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis* (MESA) cohort, about a third of all cardiovascular events occurred in participants whose initial CAC was zero. In the *CAC Consortium*, 43% of cardiovascular deaths occurred in people with CAC zero at the time of the exam. And in a recent analysis of asymptomatic patients undergoing CCTA despite CAC zero, a significant fraction showed relevant coronary disease — soft plaque silently accumulating that calcium had not yet registered. Zero protects much. It does not protect everything.

**When to indicate CCTA in addition to — or instead of — CAC:**

- **Strong family history of premature coronary disease** (first-degree relative with an event before 55 in men or 65 in women), especially if CAC came back zero but the pretest probability remains high.
- **Women at intermediate risk.** CAC underestimates risk in women because coronary calcification begins later and at lower density in them. CCTA captures the soft plaque CAC misses — and, in women, that plaque represents a proportionally greater share of the total than in men.
- **Young patients (under 50) with chronically elevated ApoB**, where the decision to intensify pharmacological treatment depends on more anatomical information.
- **Persistent clinical discordance** — patient with multiple risk factors, CAC zero, and the picture isn't closing.

**When NOT to indicate:**

- CAC already elevated — what matters is acting on that damage, not on luminal anatomy.
- Clearly low risk by all criteria.
- Symptomatic patient — this one goes straight to functional evaluation (exercise stress test, myocardial scintigraphy, or stress cardiac MRI) or catheterization, depending on the case. CCTA here is in the context of chest pain workup, not prevention.

**What to consider before ordering.** CCTA uses iodinated contrast — contraindicated in advanced kidney failure, requires caution in those with allergies and in diabetics on metformin. The radiation dose is higher than CAC, although dramatically lower than on the previous generation of CT scanners. Insurance coverage and access vary widely. There is also a less obvious risk: CCTA detects non-obstructive lesions that can generate anxiety and unnecessary follow-up if poorly interpreted. It's an exam that demands specialized reading — the radiology report alone isn't enough; it requires a cardiologist integrating it with the complete clinical picture.

**The practical conclusion:** CCTA does not replace CAC. It complements it. CAC remains the first-line exam for most adults in primary prevention — simple, inexpensive, no contrast, highly validated. CCTA is the second-line exam, reserved for when CAC doesn't answer the question that matters.

### Blood Pressure: The Most Measured and Most Mismeasured Marker

Few exams are as banal as a blood pressure reading. Automatic device, two minutes, number on the screen. Almost every patient has had blood pressure measured dozens of times in life. Few have ever had it measured **correctly**.

The trials that define today's treatment targets — especially **SPRINT**, published in 2015 — used a rigorous protocol: three consecutive measurements after five minutes seated in silence, no conversation, in a quiet environment. The average of the three is what counts. When the patient arrives running from the parking lot, hurried, with blood pressure measured once over a shirt sleeve, the number can read 10 to 20 mmHg above the real value. That everyday distortion is responsible for a large share of hypertension diagnoses — and decisions to adjust medication — made on incorrect data.

For the adult who takes prevention seriously, measurement depends on three combined tools. The **office reading**, done correctly, is still useful — but alone, in any adult over 40, it is insufficient. The second is **home monitoring**, or **HBPM** in clinical shorthand: the patient measures at home, two to three times per day, for seven consecutive days, with a validated automatic upper-arm device. The average of those readings replaces the isolated office measurement in practice. The third is **24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM)** — a small device worn at the waist, connected by a thin tube to the arm, measuring blood pressure automatically at regular intervals across an entire day, including during sleep. Beyond the average, ABPM shows whether blood pressure drops at night as it should. When it doesn't, the pattern is called *non-dipper* and is associated with worse cardiovascular prognosis and treatable causes such as sleep apnea, as we'll see in Paulo's case in Chapter 14.

Two pitfalls completely change the interpretation of these measurements.

**White-coat hypertension** occurs in patients whose blood pressure rises only in a medical environment. The office records 150 over 95; home and ABPM return 128 over 80. This patient does not have true hypertension — treating them as if they did is medicating without need, with real risk of low blood pressure, dizziness, and falls.

**Masked hypertension** is the exact mirror, and it is clinically more dangerous. The office records 125 over 78; at home and on ABPM, 142 over 92. The patient has real hypertension, with the same cardiovascular risk as classic hypertension, but goes undetected in the conventional checkup because the office doesn't see it. It is especially common in young adults, smokers, and people under chronic stress. The only way to identify it is to order home or ambulatory measurement in every patient with other cardiovascular risk factors, even with an apparently normal office reading.

**Before medication, two basic reviews.** Entering the antihypertensive conversation without looking at what the patient already eats, drinks, and takes is skipping a step.

The first is **salt**. Average sodium intake in Brazil is about 12 grams of salt per day — nearly double the WHO ceiling of 5 grams. Average U.S. intake is somewhat lower at about 8 to 9 grams per day, still well above the recommended ceiling. And that quantity rarely comes from the salt shaker on the table. It comes from bread, processed meats (deli ham, sausage, bacon), cheeses, instant broths, ready-made meals, and ultra-processed foods generally. In a patient with blood pressure at the high end or above, reducing salt to around 5 grams per day usually drops systolic by 5 to 8 mmHg — a number comparable to the effect of a low-dose antihypertensive. It doesn't replace everything, but it is the foundation from which everything else starts.

The second review is the list of **medications and substances that raise blood pressure** that the patient often forgets to mention:

- **Anti-inflammatories** like ibuprofen, diclofenac, and naproxen, used regularly for muscle or headache pain. They raise blood pressure and, with regular use, blunt the effect of antihypertensives.
- **Nasal decongestants** with pseudoephedrine or phenylephrine — present in many over-the-counter cold and sinus medications.
- **Corticosteroids**, whether tablet, cream, inhaler, or injection.
- **Birth control pills** with higher estrogen doses.
- **Some antidepressants**, especially venlafaxine.
- **"Pre-workout" supplements** with concentrated stimulants like high-dose caffeine, synephrine, or similar.
- **Licorice** in regular quantity — one component inhibits the enzyme that regulates cortisol and can cause significant hypertension in some people.
- **Recreational substances** like cocaine, amphetamines, and ecstasy — mentioned for completeness, because they are a frequent cause of hypertensive urgency in younger adults.

None of these alone usually causes severe hypertension in a healthy patient. But in someone whose pressure is already at the limit, any of them can be the tipping point. Reviewing this list is one of the first steps before intensifying or changing medication.

The **treatment targets** went through an important reformulation in 2017. The American College of Cardiology and the American Heart Association, drawing on SPRINT, redefined hypertension as blood pressure at or above 130 over 80 mmHg — what was previously called "prehypertension." SPRINT, with more than nine thousand adults at elevated cardiovascular risk, showed that taking systolic below 120 reduced heart attacks, strokes, and deaths by about a quarter, compared with the traditional target of 140. The 2023 European guideline accepted slightly more conservative targets, but the direction is the same.

Individualization matters. A young adult at high vascular risk, with elevated CAC or high Lp(a), benefits from blood pressure below 120 over 75. A frail older patient with a history of falls or orthostatic drops benefits from a more permissive target — around 140 over 85. The decision isn't mathematical; it is clinical.

**When to treat, and with what.** The decision to start medication weighs three things: blood pressure measured correctly, integrated cardiovascular risk (ApoB, CAC, diabetes, Lp(a), age), and signs of silent end-organ damage — the heart growing beyond normal on echo, traces of protein in the urine, retinal vessel changes. A patient with 135 over 85 and a CAC of 300 is in a very different decision scenario than the same blood pressure with CAC zero.

The first-line classes are four. **ACE inhibitors** (enalapril, ramipril). **Angiotensin receptor blockers** (ARBs — losartan, valsartan). **Calcium channel blockers** (CCBs — amlodipine). And **thiazide-class diuretics** (hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone). In most cases, the final choice combines two or three of these classes at low doses. The refinement — who benefits most from what — is the work of the physician with the panel in hand, and the details enter in Chapter 9, alongside newer tools like finerenone in associated kidney disease.

A practical note: studies suggest that taking at least one antihypertensive **at night**, instead of in the morning, may reduce events in patients with the *non-dipper* pattern. It is not a universal recommendation — recent trials have been conflicting — but it is a legitimate conversation when ABPM reveals the pattern.

**When blood pressure isn't only blood pressure.** There is a group of patients in whom hypertension doesn't come from the common combination of genetics and lifestyle, but from a treatable underlying condition. Signs that raise that suspicion: young adult, under 40, with new hypertension; pressure resistant to three drug classes at optimized doses; low blood potassium without explanation; sudden bursts of high pressure with sweating and palpitations. The most common causes are sleep apnea (Chapter 14), narrowing of the artery feeding the kidney, excess aldosterone production by the adrenal gland, chronic kidney disease, and pheochromocytoma — a rare tumor producing excess adrenaline. Correcting the cause, when it exists, changes everything. And missing it condemns the patient to years of treatment that addresses the symptom, not the source.

The practical takeaway is short. Office blood pressure alone no longer serves for decisions. Home or ambulatory monitoring is the modern foundation. Targets have shifted — 130 over 80 is the new ceiling in adults at cardiovascular risk, with individualization at the edges. Reducing salt and reviewing what the patient already takes comes before the next prescription. And the pharmacological choice, made on the integrated panel, is the fine work of a physician with time. It is the opposite of "I'm taking my mother's pill because she had high blood pressure."

### Integration: Blood + Imaging = Complete Map

The calcium score does not replace the biomarkers — it complements them. Alone, it shows the calcified plaque already there. But it doesn't tell whether the process is active, whether inflammation persists, whether new plaques are forming. For that, you need the blood numbers. And when CAC isn't enough to give the anatomical answer, CCTA fills the gap — also showing the soft plaque calcium has not yet made visible.

### Beyond CAC: Other Tools in the Cardiac Panel

The coronary calcium score is the most powerful photograph in cardiovascular prevention, but it is not the only image that matters. Three complementary exams complete the cardiac map — and none of them needs to be reserved for when symptoms have already arrived.

**The first is carotid Doppler with intima-media thickness measurement (CIMT).** The carotids are accessible arteries, near the surface of the skin, that can be examined by ultrasound in the office, with no radiation and no contrast, in less than twenty minutes. The intima-media thickness measurement of these arteries translates, with good precision, the state of systemic atherosclerosis. Thickness above 0.9 mm is already a warning; above 1.3 mm indicates established plaque; above 1.5 mm is advanced atherosclerosis. The Doppler also identifies and quantifies visible plaques — and allows measurement of stenosis if there is flow obstruction. For young patients with elevated ApoB and CAC still zero, CIMT can detect soft plaque before it calcifies. For older patients with high CAC, it is the way to track progression in a parallel vascular territory. It does not replace CAC — it complements it.

**The second is the transthoracic echocardiogram with Doppler.** While CAC photographs the arteries, the echocardiogram photographs the heart itself — chambers, valves, walls, contractile function, filling pressures. The central parameter is left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), which should normally be between 55% and 70%. But there are even more sensitive measures: global longitudinal strain (GLS), which detects contractile dysfunction before LVEF drops, and the average E/e' ratio, which reveals diastolic dysfunction — the earliest and most common form of heart failure in patients with hypertension, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome. A patient with LVEF of 62% may have altered GLS at -16% and E/e' at 13, indicating that the heart is stiffening and losing relaxation capacity — even before any symptom. It is the cardiac equivalent of what fasting insulin does for metabolism: detecting the problem years before the conventional diagnosis appears.

**The third is the 12-lead electrocardiogram.** The ECG seems banal — it's cheap, fast, available everywhere. But when read with attention to detail, it reveals valuable information: the corrected QT interval (QTc) identifies vulnerability to serious arrhythmias; the duration of the QRS and changes in the ST segment signal conduction problems or silent ischemia; the Sokolow-Lyon and Cornell criteria detect left ventricular hypertrophy — a silent consequence of long-standing hypertension. And, of course, the ECG is the most direct way to identify atrial fibrillation, a common arrhythmia in adults over 50 and one that multiplies stroke risk when untreated. In a preventive checkup, the ECG isn't redundancy. It is cheap information that deserves careful reading.

### A Vascular Sign the Patient Feels Before the Doctor

There is a cardiovascular signal that reaches a man before any image, and that, when it appears, has predictive power comparable to the best markers in the panel: **erectile dysfunction.**

The penis is a vascular organ. Erection depends on dilation of the corpus cavernosum, which depends on functioning endothelium, which depends on the same mechanisms that keep coronaries healthy — nitric oxide, endothelial function, absence of chronic inflammation. The penile vascular network is finer than the coronary. When systemic endothelial dysfunction begins, the penis signals first. Consolidated meta-analyses show that erectile dysfunction precedes cardiovascular events by an average of 3 to 5 years in men aged 40 to 70. In men with new ED and no obvious local cause — no medication, no local trauma, no overt depression — the 10-year risk of heart attack is comparable to that of active smoking.

The clinical implication is direct: erectile dysfunction in men under 70, without local explanation, is a formal indication for expanded cardiovascular evaluation — full panel, coronary calcium score, sometimes cardiopulmonary exercise testing. It is not vanity, nor a secondary complaint. It is a sentinel symptom.

In clinic, this is one of the questions I learned to ask with conversational space — not in passing. Men frequently minimize it (*"it's age," "it's stress"*), and the honest answer only comes when there is time and confidentiality. When it comes, it reconfigures the entire plan: a patient who came in with a question about a statin and leaves with a CAC scheduled is a different patient.

The female counterpart — female sexual dysfunction assessed by the *Female Sexual Function Index* (FSFI) — has a less robust literature on vascular correlation, but it exists. In women in the menopausal transition, what is often attributed only to "hormonal decline" can be a combination of treatable hormonal, vascular, and psychological factors. Sexual function is a biomarker of the entire endothelium, not only the genital.

**The integrated reading** is where real preventive medicine happens:

- **ApoB** says how many atherogenic particles are bombarding your arteries right now.
- The **ApoB/ApoA1 ratio** shows whether the balance between arterial attack and defense is favorable.
- **hs-CRP** says whether the inflammatory environment is fueling plaque instability.
- **Blood pressure** says how much mechanical force the arteries are taking.
- And **CAC** says how much accumulated damage all those forces have already caused over a lifetime.

These are different dimensions of the same problem. Blood shows the process in motion. Imaging shows the accumulated result. A patient with CAC zero and ApoB of 120 is building plaques that haven't yet calcified. A patient with CAC of 200 and ApoB of 70 on treatment has accumulated damage, but the process may be under control. Management is different in each case — and without both sets of information, the decision is blind.

Ricardo — the patient from Chapter 1 — had ApoB of 118, CRP of 2.4, and a CAC score of 187 discovered during admission, after the heart attack. If someone had integrated those numbers five or ten years earlier, the story might have been different.

The same logic applies to Marcos: the CAC of 412 showed that accumulated damage was extensive. But the biomarkers — which now included insulin of 11, CRP of 1.6, and ApoB of 82 on a statin — showed there was still room to optimize. The plaque was there. But the soil that fed it could still be modified. With the complete panel in hand, we adjusted Marcos's strategy: we intensified the statin, added ezetimibe to bring ApoB below 70, started structured exercise focused on VO₂ max and strength, revised nutrition to address the insulin resistance no one had investigated, and began monitoring biomarkers every four months.

The CAC won't drop — deposited calcium does not dissolve. But the trajectory can change. And it is the trajectory that determines prognosis.

### When the Statin Isn't Enough

With Marcos, the math worked. We intensified the statin, added ezetimibe, and ApoB dropped below 70 in four months. But it doesn't always work. There are patients who do everything right, take the pill every day, and the number doesn't drop as far as it needs to.

Three situations come up regularly in clinic. The first is the patient who feels real muscle pain on a higher statin dose — a minority, smaller than the internet suggests, but real. The second is the patient already on combined statin and ezetimibe whose ApoB still sits above target. The third is the patient who inherited elevated Lp(a) or familial hypercholesterolemia — a genetic load that pushes cholesterol upward even when everything else is optimized.

For these patients, the medicine of the last ten years has built new tools. Worth knowing — not for self-medicating, but for knowing what to ask the cardiologist when the math doesn't work.

**Bempedoic acid.** A pill that works in the same direction as a statin, but one layer above — and with an important difference: the molecule does not reach muscle. People who get muscle pain from a statin generally tolerate it without complaint. A large trial published in 2023 showed that, added to a statin, bempedoic acid reduces heart attacks and strokes over long-term follow-up. The price is a small rise in uric acid, with some risk of gout flare in those already prone. It is FDA-approved as Nexletol (and as a fixed-dose combination with ezetimibe, Nexlizet) and widely available in the U.S. since 2020. In Brazil, it has been available since 2025 as a fixed-dose combination with ezetimibe under the brand Nustendi. In practice, it's like taking a statin in the morning and, instead of two separate pills of ezetimibe and bempedoic acid, a single tablet doing both jobs.

**Evolocumab and alirocumab.** Here the form changes: not a pill, but a self-injection the patient gives at home, every two weeks or once a month. They neutralize a protein produced by the liver that, by a paradox of biology, destroys the receptors responsible for clearing cholesterol from the blood. With that protein blocked, the liver captures more LDL and cholesterol drops dramatically: an additional 50% to 60% on top of the statin the patient is already taking.

The evidence is settled. Two large trials — FOURIER with evolocumab, ODYSSEY OUTCOMES with alirocumab — showed real reductions in heart attacks, strokes, and deaths. The FOURIER extension, with eight years of follow-up, showed that the benefit grows with time: the longer LDL stays low, the safer the trajectory. The old fear that "lowering cholesterol too far harms the brain" was tested and discarded. In the U.S., these two medications have been FDA-approved since 2015 (Repatha and Praluent); in Brazil, since 2016.

**Inclisiran — the twice-yearly injection.** This is the new medication, and it is the one that most surprises patients hearing about it for the first time. It works differently from the previous two: instead of neutralizing the protein already circulating in the blood, it silences the instruction in the liver itself so the protein is no longer manufactured. The result is striking — one injection, another three months later, and from then on **one injection every six months**. Two doses a year solve the biggest obstacle in cardiovascular medicine: the difficulty of sustaining treatment over the long haul. The patient who starts the medication and forgets, lets it run out, interrupts, returns, interrupts again — that patient, with inclisiran, stays protected with two office visits a year. FDA-approved in December 2021 (Leqvio in the U.S.); in Brazil, approved by Anvisa in June 2023 as Sybrava.

Here I need to pause and be honest with the reader the same way I'm honest with the patient in clinic: **the proof that inclisiran prevents heart attack and death is still in progress.** The studies published so far have shown, with confidence, that it lowers LDL. The largest trial, called ORION-4, with 15,000 patients followed for years, only completes in mid-2026. Until then, we use logic: if LDL drops, and if that drop in other medications prevented cardiovascular events, it is reasonable to expect the same here. Reasonable — but not yet proven the way evolocumab and alirocumab are already proven.

That changes how I decide, case by case. For the adherent patient who accepts the biweekly injection and has access, evolocumab or alirocumab go first — because the proof of benefit is closed. For the patient I've known for years and know will interrupt any long course, or for whom the simplicity of two annual doses is what separates treating from not treating, inclisiran enters — with the frank conversation about what ORION-4 still needs to show.

There is no "statin or nothing." There is a ladder of options, from the cheapest and most established to the newest and most promising. Knowing which step each patient needs to stand on is the work of someone reading the entire panel — not someone aiming only at the cholesterol number.

### The Hope Coming for Lp(a)

Among the three groups I just described, the third — those with inherited high Lp(a) — was the one that most needed something new. For decades, Lp(a) was the piece of the cardiovascular panel that the doctor measured, the patient knew the number for, and no one had much specifically to do about. Statins don't move Lp(a) (some even raise it slightly); ezetimibe and bempedoic acid have minimal effect. PCSK9 inhibitors — evolocumab and alirocumab — reduce Lp(a) by about 25% to 27% (median observed in FOURIER and ODYSSEY OUTCOMES), and inclisiran by about 20% to 25%. It is a relevant class side effect, but still modest: insufficient to normalize markedly elevated Lp(a). The traditional strategy, therefore, has been to control the other factors more rigorously — ApoB below 60, blood pressure well managed, weight cared for — and live with the residual Lp(a).

That story is changing. Four medications — pelacarsen, olpasiran, lepodisiran, and muvalaplin (the last in pill form) — are in advanced research, all aiming directly at Lp(a) production by the liver. The first results on heart attack and stroke reduction are expected in the next few years.

For the patient who has been told for years that the number on the test was an inheritance to merely watch, this turn matters — and if confirmed, changes what is possible for one in five people in the world.

### Residual Inflammation: The Third Target Few Patients Know About

The chapter so far has discussed two forces shaping disease in the arteries: atherogenic particles (ApoB, Lp(a)) and accumulated anatomical damage (CAC). There is a third force that, for decades, sat at the margins of cardiovascular medicine and over the last seven years returned to the center of the board: **residual inflammation.**

The idea is direct. Even the patient who manages to bring ApoB to target, take the statin regularly, and control blood pressure and glucose may continue with hs-CRP persistently above 2 mg/L. That elevated CRP is not random. It is a signal that the environment inside the arteries is still inflamed — and inflamed plaque is the plaque that ruptures. The patient "at target" on every other parameter has, because of that background inflammation, a residual risk that no additional statin will resolve.

The first trial to prove that treating inflammation specifically reduces cardiovascular events was **CANTOS**, published in 2017. Researchers used an expensive monoclonal antibody — canakinumab — in patients who had already had a heart attack and still showed elevated CRP. Result: significant reduction in new cardiovascular events, independent of any change in cholesterol. Canakinumab never became a standard tool, due to cost and increased infection risk. But the concept it established — inflammation as an independent therapeutic target — opened the door to what comes next.

**Low-dose colchicine** is the practical tool on this front. An old medication, traditionally used in gout flares, prescription-only and widely available in pharmacies in both countries. At 0.5 mg per day, two large trials — COLCOT in patients shortly after a heart attack and LoDoCo2 in patients with chronic coronary disease — showed reductions of 25% to 30% in major cardiovascular events. In 2023, the FDA formally approved colchicine for this indication in the U.S. under the brand Lodoco. In Brazil, this use for cardiovascular prevention is still classified as *off-label* — preventive cardiologists prescribe based on international evidence, outside the official Brazilian indication.

Who benefits? Patients with documented coronary disease (post-heart-attack or elevated CAC) who maintain CRP above 2 mg/L despite all the other pillars in order. Who should not use it: patients with seriously compromised kidney function, patients on certain antibiotics or antifungals that raise blood colchicine levels, and patients with persistent diarrhea — the most frequent side effect, around 10%.

Colchicine is today the specific pharmacological arm of the cardiovascular anti-inflammatory front. But there are two broader tools that cut across several of this book's pillars — and whose value isn't in rivaling colchicine, but in delivering background anti-inflammatory effect combined with other concrete benefits.

**Omega-3** — EPA and DHA, the fatty acids found primarily in oily fish like salmon, sardines, and tuna — is the dietary tool with the broadest evidence package in the entire book. It consistently reduces triglycerides. It lowers CRP and IL-6 in meta-analyses. It has moderate evidence in depressive symptoms and in joint pain from rheumatoid arthritis. It shows signal in cognitive preservation, endothelial function, and dry eye. Chapter 8 will discuss the smart-supplementation dose — 1 to 2 grams of combined EPA+DHA per day, preferably as part of nutrition. For hard cardiovascular outcomes specifically at supplement dose, the large trials (VITAL, ASCEND, STRENGTH) were mixed or negative — it serves as an adjunct, not as standalone cardiovascular therapy. There is a high-dose pharmacological formulation, icosapent ethyl (4 g of pure EPA per day), which in the REDUCE-IT study reduced cardiovascular events. It is FDA-approved as Vascepa; not yet registered in Brazil.

**Turmeric** (in Brazil, *açafrão-da-terra* — not to be confused with true saffron from *Crocus sativus*) is the second tool. The active principle, **curcumin**, has broad anti-inflammatory effects in meta-analyses: it reduces CRP, relieves osteoarthritis pain (effect comparable to low-dose NSAIDs in some trials), lowers hepatic enzymes in steatosis, and shows signal in depressive symptoms and insulin sensitivity. The absorption of pure curcumin is poor — it only makes sense in formulations with validated bioavailability (Theracurmin, Meriva, Longvida). Two cautions: curcumin with piperine interacts via the liver with immunosuppressants (tacrolimus, cyclosporine) and anticoagulants — it requires evaluation before starting; and there are rare reports of idiosyncratic hepatitis with prolonged use, especially with piperine-containing formulations — worth monitoring liver enzymes.

The lesson cardiology has written over the last seven years is simple to summarize. Lowering ApoB solves part of the problem. Caring for the anatomy solves another part. For the patient who maintains persistently elevated CRP despite everything, residual inflammation calls for its own target — and low-dose colchicine is the practical tool with outcome evidence that target finally has. Omega-3 and turmeric act on the marker and on several other pillars of health; only colchicine (and, at pharmacological dose, purified EPA) have shown direct reduction in cardiovascular events.

### A Myth That Fell: "Preventive" Aspirin for Everyone

For decades, the daily baby aspirin — 81 or 100 mg — was almost a sacrament of preventive medicine. Patients in their 50s, 60s, 70s took it "to prevent a heart attack" without ever having had a cardiovascular event, with no documented coronary disease, just on the logic that aspirin thins the blood and that must be good.

The evidence of recent years has dismantled that logic in primary prevention. **ASPREE** (2018, more than 19,000 adults over 70 without cardiovascular disease) showed that low-dose aspirin **did not reduce** events or mortality and **increased** major bleeding. **ARRIVE** and **ASCEND**, in the same year, reached the same direction: marginal benefit, real bleeding, unfavorable risk-benefit.

In 2022, the USPSTF withdrew the routine recommendation of aspirin in primary prevention above 60; AHA and ACC followed. Between 40 and 70, the use can be considered on an individualized basis only in very high cardiovascular risk with low bleeding risk.

In **secondary** prevention, however, the story is different and unchanged. A patient who has already had a heart attack, angioplasty, ischemic stroke, or has confirmed coronary disease still has solid indication for low-dose aspirin, barring contraindication.

The practical message is short: aspirin to prevent "the first event" is a strategy of the past. Aspirin to prevent the second remains active medicine.

### What If It Were You?

You are between 40 and 75. Maybe your cardiologist has said your tests look fine. Maybe you take a statin and think the problem is solved. Maybe you take nothing because LDL was never "high enough."

The question isn't whether your cholesterol is controlled. The question is: how much damage has already accumulated?

The calcium score is the only widely available exam that answers that question directly. No admission needed. No contrast. No fasting. Less than ten minutes. And the result — a single number — can completely change how you and your doctor think about your cardiovascular risk.

If the CAC comes back zero, you gain valuable information: your risk factors, whatever they are, haven't yet translated into detectable plaque. That is power. If it comes back above zero, you gain something equally valuable: the truth about what has already happened inside your arteries. And with that truth, the chance to act before the body decides to act for you.

### The Essential in 60 Seconds

- The coronary calcium score is a simple CT that quantifies calcified plaque in the heart's arteries. It is the only widely available exam that photographs atherosclerosis directly.

- CAC zero is the most powerful result in preventive cardiology — it cuts estimated risk by up to 85%. But it does not exclude soft plaque, especially in young patients with elevated ApoB.

- CAC above 100 generally indicates the need for a statin. Above 400 is equivalent to secondary prevention.

- When CAC zero isn't enough — strong family history of premature CAD, women, young patients with chronically high ApoB — coronary CT angiography (CCTA) complements, showing the soft plaque calcium does not register. Second-line exam, with contrast, higher cost and radiation. Doesn't replace CAC; fills its gaps.

- Arterial age — *"your arteries look 20 years older"* — is the most effective translation of the score for the patient. It changes behavior in a way no lab number can.

- Statins can raise CAC through plaque stabilization, without raising risk. The number rises, but the plaque is safer.

- The complete cardiovascular map integrates blood and imaging: ApoB (atherogenic particles) + hs-CRP (inflammation) + blood pressure (mechanical force) + CAC (accumulated damage).

- Office blood pressure alone is insufficient for diagnosis or treatment decisions. Home monitoring (HBPM) and 24-hour ABPM are the modern foundation — they also catch masked hypertension (normal office, real elevated pressure) and the *non-dipper* pattern. Since 2017, the target is 130 over 80 mmHg in adults at cardiovascular risk. Reducing salt and reviewing the medications already raising blood pressure (anti-inflammatories, decongestants, corticosteroids, some antidepressants, pre-workout supplements) come before the next prescription.

- When a statin and ezetimibe aren't enough — due to intolerance, target not reached, or elevated Lp(a) — there is a growing arsenal: bempedoic acid (Nexletol; Nustendi as a fixed-dose combination with ezetimibe in Brazil), evolocumab and alirocumab (biweekly injections with proven outcomes — Repatha and Praluent), and inclisiran (two injections per year as Leqvio in the U.S. and Sybrava in Brazil, with outcomes still being tested through 2026).

- For those with high inherited Lp(a), four medications in advanced research (pelacarsen, olpasiran, lepodisiran, and the oral muvalaplin) target Lp(a) production directly. Event results expected in the next few years.

- Residual inflammation is the third force in cardiovascular risk, alongside ApoB and anatomical damage. For patients with coronary disease and persistent CRP, low-dose colchicine reduces events (FDA-approved as Lodoco; off-label in Brazil). Omega-3 and turmeric have broad benefits across joints, mood, liver, and inflammatory markers — but don't replace colchicine when colchicine is what the evidence calls for.

- The era of "baby aspirin for everyone" is over. In primary prevention, the ASPREE, ARRIVE, and ASCEND trials showed marginal benefit and real bleeding — the USPSTF and AHA/ACC withdrew the routine recommendation. In secondary prevention (post-MI, post-stroke, confirmed CAD), aspirin remains indicated.
