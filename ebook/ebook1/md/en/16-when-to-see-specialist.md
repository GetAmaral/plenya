## Chapter 16 — When to See a Specialist — and What to Ask

When we finished the joint reading of the labs in that visit from Chapter 15, Ricardo went silent for a moment. Then he asked a second question — different from the first, and perhaps harder.

> *"Doctor, if I had wanted to find a physician like you before the heart attack — a doctor who would have ordered those exams, read the numbers like this, made this kind of plan — how would I have found one? I didn't even know this existed."*

It is a question I hear frequently. And the honest answer is that Ricardo would not have found one easily — not because such practitioners don't exist, but because the field of preventive and longevity medicine is, at this moment, in a phase of disorderly growth. There are excellent practitioners with solid training and real clinical practice. There are average practitioners with good intentions and little experience. And there is a growing number of people without relevant clinical practice who learned the language of longevity through Instagram and sell standardized protocols with the authority of someone who has never followed a patient over five years.

The problem isn't that there are few good practitioners. The problem is that the reader has no criteria to separate the three groups — because they all use the same words. They all say "precision medicine." They all say "biomarkers." They all say "prevention." The difference lies in what happens after the first visit, and that difference the reader only discovers once already inside the process.

This chapter exists to shorten that path. Not to recommend names or clinics — but to deliver to the reader a set of criteria that works in any city, in any context, with any practitioner.

---

### The Minefield

Longevity medicine has gained traction in recent years for a legitimate reason: the demand is real. People in their 40s, 50s, and 60s are realizing that the conventional checkup isn't enough — and they are seeking alternatives. That movement is healthy. What isn't healthy is how the market responded.

On the positive side, clinics and practitioners have emerged that integrate the tools this book describes — expanded biomarkers, iterative medicine, early intervention against the four silent killers. On the side to watch, the same interest fed a growing supply of generic protocols sold as personalization: IV infusions prescribed without clear indication, testosterone started without an established diagnosis, dozens of supplements stacked without biochemical rationale, genetic panels without clinical interpretation that changes management. It isn't about demonizing a category — it is about recognizing that one-size-fits-all isn't personalized medicine, and it is up to the patient to be able to distinguish one from the other.

The confusion is amplified because the patient generally arrives at the longevity field through one of two doors. The first is the door of **frustration**: the conventional doctor said "everything is normal," but the patient feels something isn't right, or has a worrying family history, or has read enough to know that "normal" isn't optimal. The second is the door of **aspiration**: the patient wants to optimize, wants performance, wants to age better. Neither door is wrong. But at both, the patient arrives vulnerable — without criteria to distinguish the practitioner who will truly investigate from the one who will apply the same protocol they apply to everyone else.

Fernanda, from Chapter 4, came to the office at her husband's insistence — he was already my patient and knew what to expect. Not everyone has that reference. Most arrive through Google, friend recommendations, or a social media profile that seemed convincing. This chapter is for that majority.

### Five Criteria for Evaluating a Practitioner

There is no universal certification of "longevity physician." There is no medical specialty officially registered with that name — neither by Brazil's Federal Council of Medicine (CFM) nor by the American Board of Medical Specialties (ABMS) in the U.S. That means any physician can present themselves as a longevity specialist without a regulatory body having validated that competence. The lack of regulation doesn't invalidate the field — it simply transfers the responsibility of evaluation to the patient.

The five criteria below are the ones I would use if I were a patient looking for a preventive medicine practitioner for myself. They aren't perfect. But they filter most of the problems.

**First: medical training and verifiable clinical practice.** Is the practitioner board-certified? In Brazil, that means CRM (state medical board) registration; in the U.S., active state medical license plus ABMS specialty certification. Do they see patients regularly — not only give talks, not only produce content? There is a difference between someone who studies longevity and someone who practices longevity in clinic. Both are legitimate, but the patient's question is clinical — and clinical decisions require clinical experience. A practitioner who follows thirty, fifty, a hundred patients over years has a caliber of judgment no online course reproduces. Ask how long they have practiced in this area. Ask how many patients they follow. The answer doesn't have to be an exact number — it has to be plausible.

**Second: the first visit is investigation, not prescription.** A practitioner who prescribes a protocol on the first visit — before seeing your labs, before knowing your history, before hearing what you eat, how you sleep, how you train, what stresses you — is applying a recipe. The good first visit is long, curious, and ends with test orders, not with supplement prescriptions. The exams ordered should include most of what Chapters 4 to 6 of this book describe. If the practitioner only orders CBC, glucose, and total cholesterol, they are operating on the conventional checkup model with longevity packaging. If they order ApoB, fasting insulin, hs-CRP, homocysteine, complete hormonal profile, thyroid function beyond isolated TSH, and can justify why they ordered each one, that's a good sign.

**Third: the plan is biomarker-individualized, not ideology-driven.** There are longevity physicians who prescribe intermittent fasting for every patient. There are those who prescribe testosterone for every man over 45. There are those who prescribe ten supplements on day one. None of those approaches is necessarily wrong — but when it is universal, it is mass protocol, not personalization. The competent practitioner prescribes based on what that patient's panel revealed, not on what they believe works in general. The question worth asking at the return visit, when the labs come in: "why this treatment for me specifically, based on these numbers?" If the answer references your labs, good sign. If the answer is generic ("this is good for everyone"), warning sign.

**Fourth: integration across pillars, not one-dimensional approach.** The fragmentation of conventional medicine — the cardiologist who only sees the heart, the endocrinologist who only sees the hormone — is one of the problems this entire book documents. But longevity medicine can reproduce the same fragmentation in another way: the practitioner who only talks about supplementation, who only talks about hormones, who only talks about training. If the practitioner doesn't ask about your sleep, about your mental health, about your relationships, about your stress level — if they treat the body as a marker panel without considering the person who carries those markers — a piece is missing. Ana's case, in Chapters 12 and 13, is the clearest example in the book: eighteen months of impeccable biological optimization couldn't move CRP and cortisol because the psychological pillar was sabotaging everything. The practitioner who would have continued prescribing more supplements and more exams without stopping to ask how she was living would have failed — not from lack of biochemical knowledge, but from lack of integrative vision.

**Fifth: transparency about what they don't know.** Longevity medicine is under construction. There are no thirty-year randomized clinical trials proving that optimizing ApoB at 40 reduces mortality at 80 — the evidence is strong, the biological logic is robust, but definitive proof has not yet arrived. The same applies to epigenetic clocks, to most supplementation protocols, to metformin prescription in people without diabetes. The practitioner who presents everything with absolute certainty is simplifying. The practitioner who says *"the evidence points to this, but we don't know everything; the decision is yours, and I will follow up"* is being honest. In medicine, epistemic honesty — knowing how to distinguish what is well known from what is reasonably inferred from what is speculation — is one of the hardest and most important competencies. When the practitioner admits uncertainty without being paralyzed by it, that is a sign of clinical maturity.

### What to Expect from a Complete Evaluation

For the reader who has never been through a complete preventive medicine evaluation, it is worth describing what a good evaluation typically involves. Not as a rigid prescription — there are legitimate variations among practitioners — but as a reference so the reader knows whether what they are receiving is reasonable or incomplete.

The **first visit** should last at least one hour. Some last two. Long time isn't physician vanity — it is real necessity. A history that covers personal and family history, eating habits in detail, training pattern, sleep quality, stress level, medications in use, supplements the patient already takes (and why), history of previous tests, and the patient's expectations regarding the process doesn't fit in twenty minutes. If the first visit ended in half an hour and already came with a prescription, the process was rushed.

The **test orders** that come out of that first visit should include, at a minimum, the complete metabolic panel described in Chapters 4 and 6 (glucose, HbA1c, fasting insulin, lipid panel with ApoB, hs-CRP, homocysteine, vitamin D, B12, ferritin, renal function with cystatin C, complete thyroid function with free T3 and T4) and the basic hormonal panel described in Chapter 10, adapted by sex and age. Lp(a), if never measured. Body composition by quality bioelectrical impedance. Depending on the clinical history, CAC score, 24-hour ABPM, polysomnography, echocardiogram, and other imaging exams may be added.

The **return visit**, when the labs come in, is where the work really begins. The practitioner should read the panel with the patient — not hand over a ready prescription sheet. The return visit is the moment when target ranges are discussed, findings are contextualized (an ApoB of 95 in a 38-year-old patient without family history is different from an ApoB of 95 in a 58-year-old whose father died of a heart attack), and the plan is built — with prioritization, not with ten fronts at once. The Rule of Two from Chapter 15 applies here: two principal focuses for the next months, with a date set to retest.

The **follow-up cycle** is quarterly at the start (when there are active adjustments underway) and tends to space out to semiannual or annual as the patient reaches and maintains the target ranges. At each return, the panel is reassessed, the plan is adjusted, and new priorities are set. It is an iterative process — test, intervene, retest, adjust — and not a static prescription delivered once. The practitioner who hands the plan over at the second visit and schedules the next return for a year later is operating on the conventional model in different clothing.

### Red Flags

There are situations that justify the patient rethinking the choice of practitioner. None alone is proof of incompetence — but each deserves attention.

- The practitioner prescribes **testosterone, growth hormone, or other hormones on the first visit**, without prior labs and without investigation of causes. This happens with disturbing frequency. Chapter 10 of this book showed, with Paulo's case, that low testosterone can have treatable causes without replacement — and that the first intervention should be to investigate and optimize the soil, not automatically replace the hormone.

- The practitioner prescribes **dozens of supplements at once**, without individual justification based on measured deficiencies or specific needs of that patient. Chapter 8 of this book described rational, evidence-based, biomarker-guided supplementation — and the consolidation logic that reduces the number of capsules to the minimum necessary. If the patient leaves the first visit with twenty supplements prescribed, the legitimate question is: based on what?

- The practitioner **doesn't order labs before prescribing** — or orders only the basic conventional checkup panel and calls it a "complete evaluation."

- The practitioner **discourages the patient from seeking a second opinion**, or reacts poorly when the patient questions a recommendation.

- The practitioner **doesn't track side effects** of the interventions they prescribed. Any intervention — pharmacological, hormonal, supplemental — can have unintended effects. Monitoring is part of the work, not optional.

- The practitioner **sells their own supplements at the visit**, without giving the patient the option to purchase from a pharmacy or independent compounding pharmacy. This isn't illegal, but it creates a conflict of interest the patient has the right to know about and to avoid.

### The Principle That Matters More Than Any Protocol

If this chapter had to reduce to a single idea, it would be this: **personalize, don't generalize.**

Protocols exist and are useful — as maps. But the map isn't the territory. The same exercise protocol that worked for Marcos wouldn't be appropriate for André, because the two had opposite problems (Marcos didn't train; André overtrained). The same hormonal investigation that revealed Paulo's diagnosis wouldn't apply the same way to Fernanda, because female and male hormonal panels follow different logics. The same psychological intervention that moved Ana's CRP wouldn't be enough for Paulo, whose problem was apnea, not stress.

Each patient is a case. Biomarkers are the panel. The pillars are the guide. But the clinical decision — which marker to attack first, with what tool, at what dose, on what reassessment timeline — is individual. That is why the clinical partnership matters. And that is why choosing the right practitioner isn't a detail — it is foundation.

The reader who reaches this point of the book knows how to read their own panel. Knows how to identify the alert points. Knows that concentrated action beats dispersed action. Knows that habits and markers talk. But there is a category of decision that requires someone on the other side of the table: the decision that weighs risk against benefit with real data on the table. When to intensify a statin and when to wait. When to investigate low testosterone and when to treat it. When persistent CRP calls for psychotherapy and when it calls for rheumatologic workup. When a CAC of 400 changes the entire plan and when a CAC of zero allows some relaxation.

Those decisions don't fit in the reader alone. And they shouldn't fit in a practitioner the reader doesn't trust — or in a practitioner who doesn't know them.

The good clinical partnership is the one that combines the reader who learned to read with the practitioner who knows how to interpret. Neither alone is enough.

### The Essential in 60 Seconds

- The field of preventive and longevity medicine is in disorderly growth — there are excellent practitioners with solid training and real clinical practice, and there are those who sell standardized protocols with the language of personalization. The patient needs criteria to distinguish the two.

- Five criteria help: medical training with verifiable and continued clinical practice; first visit as investigation, not prescription; plan individualized by biomarker, not by ideology; integrative vision that considers all pillars, not only the biological; and transparency about what the evidence supports and what is still reasonable inference.

- A typical complete evaluation begins with a long, curious first visit, follows with an expanded panel order, proceeds with a return visit for joint reading of the results and construction of a prioritized plan, and is sustained by iterative cycles of follow-up — quarterly at the start, spacing out as the patient stabilizes in the target ranges.

- Red flags include prescription without prior labs, dozens of supplements without individual justification, resistance to patient questioning, absence of side-effect monitoring, and sale of own products without an independent alternative.

- The principle that holds it all together is to personalize, not generalize. Protocols are guides, not recipes. Each patient is a case. The good clinical partnership combines the patient who learned to read their own panel with the practitioner who knows how to interpret what the numbers are saying — and who knows how to say "I don't know" when they don't.
