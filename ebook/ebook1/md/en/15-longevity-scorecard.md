# PART IV — THE PLAN

Across the previous fourteen chapters, we built two things. First, the map: what is killing people after 40, how the body ages on the inside, which numbers matter, what the arteries and the metabolism reveal. Then, the method: the four pillars of ACTS — Activity, Alimentation & Smart Adjuncts; Clinical Optimization; Tending Mind, Body & Bonds; Sleep, Rhythm & Recovery.

But map and method aren't enough. What's missing is the gesture this part of the book exists to provoke — the reader leaving the passive role of someone who reads about prevention and taking the active role of someone who applies it. The two chapters that follow address that transition. Chapter 15 shows how to read your own panel. Chapter 16 deals with how to evaluate a preventive medicine practitioner and what to ask in the first visit.

---

## Chapter 15 — Your Longevity Scorecard: Where Are You?

Five years after the heart attack, Ricardo came back to the office with a folder. It wasn't a folder of routine tests — it was his folder, of five years, with the entire history: every lipid panel, every CRP, every vitamin D, every CAC, every ABPM. He was 57. He was well. And he wanted, in that visit, to do something different.

He sat down, opened the folder on the desk, and said: *"Doctor, today I want to do it myself. You guide me, but I want to read my own labs. I want to understand what each number says and what each number is asking me to do."*

I went silent for a moment. Not because the question was hard — because it was exactly the question I wanted all my patients to ask at some point. The question of someone who stopped being a passive patient and became a co-author of their own plan. The question of someone who understood that preventive medicine isn't a service rendered by another person — it is continuous work the physician facilitates but the patient drives.

In that visit, we spent two hours doing what Ricardo asked.

---

### Why Self-Assessment, and Why Now

There is a difference between knowing and knowing how to apply. The reader who has gotten this far already knows that ApoB matters more than LDL, that fasting insulin rises before glucose, that CAC is a five-minute exam, that sleep regularity predicts mortality better than duration, that persistent CRP above 1.5 frequently points to the HPA axis even when all other pillars seem in order. They know a lot. But knowing isn't enough — because, in the real office, the person who translates all that into decisions is the patient themselves, with the physician as guide.

The patient who arrives at the visit with the folder already organized, knowing what to ask, knowing which number rose and which fell since the last visit, knowing which pillar needs attention this quarter — that patient extracts ten times more from the visit than the patient who presents fresh labs without context and waits for the physician to read them.

The tool for that is what I will call from here on the **scorecard** — a structured way of revisiting your own panel every three months, identifying the two points that matter most this quarter, and working on exactly those two. Not ten, not twenty. Two.

Before detailing the scorecard, three principles are worth fixing — without them, it becomes anxious score-keeping instead of a compass.

**The scorecard isn't a grade.** There is no "perfect score" on the scorecard. There is "where I am," "where I want to be," "the shortest path between the two." The person with three items in the optimal zone and seven in alert is not worse than the person with eight optimal and two in alert — they are at a different moment of the work. The scorecard doesn't compare; it orients.

**Habits and numbers talk to each other.** Throughout the book, we saw patients in whom the contradiction between apparent habit and real marker revealed the problem. André trained ten hours a week, ate without ultra-processed foods, and still had CRP of 2.1 and homocysteine of 14 — because the training was poorly structured and the supplementation his vegetarianism required had never been prescribed. Fernanda ate "well by typical middle-class standards," exercised, and had insulin of 13 and a fatty liver. When apparent habit and real marker don't talk, it is the marker telling the truth — and the habit that needs revising.

**Concentrated action beats dispersed action.** Trying to change ten things at once is the most predictable way of changing none. Marcos, in Chapters 7 and 8, changed training, alimentation, supplementation, and sleep in parallel — but with a plan that respected the order of clinical priorities, not ten simultaneous fronts competing for attention. That is why it worked.

With those three principles in mind, the rest of the chapter is the application.

### The Four-Pillar Scorecard

The form I use in clinic organizes the scorecard as **four simple questions**, one per pillar of ACTS. Each question summarizes what the reader already saw in the previous chapters and forces an honest diagnosis of the current situation.

#### Pillar A — The Question of Activity, Alimentation & Smart Adjuncts

In one sentence: **is your body being built or being worn down by the routine of your last six months?**

The answer is in three objective markers and three behaviors. The markers are the ones from Chapter 4 that depend most on lifestyle: hs-CRP (target below 1.0 mg/L), fasting insulin (target below 8 µIU/mL), and vitamin D (target between 40 and 60 ng/mL). The behaviors are those of Chapters 7 and 8: structured training across the four complementary domains (Zone 2, progressive strength, short high-intensity, mobility), consistent eating pattern based on real food, and biomarker-individualized supplementation, not by trend.

The honest reading of this pillar is the simplest of the scorecard. If the three markers are in the optimal zone and the three behaviors are established, this pillar is healthy. If either column is in alert — abnormal marker or absent behavior — this is a place to concentrate attention next quarter. When both sides are in simultaneous alert, Pillar A is probably where the reader needs to begin, before any other.

Body composition enters as a complementary reading, and it is worth being specific here, because most people weigh themselves and ignore what matters. Weight on the scale isn't relevant clinical data. What matters is the waist-to-height ratio (above 0.55 signals risk-laden abdominal accumulation), waist circumference (above 35 inches/90 cm in men and 31 inches/80 cm in women by Latin American population criteria — slightly higher cutoffs apply by NHLBI/AHA in the U.S. at 40 inches/102 cm in men and 35 inches/88 cm in women), the appendicular skeletal muscle index obtained by quality bioelectrical impedance or DEXA (below 7.0 kg/m² in men and 5.5 in women suggests sarcopenia), and grip strength measured with a dynamometer (below 30 kg in men and 20 kg in women over 50 is a sign of early functional fragility). Those four numbers, together, tell more about the relationship between your body and your plan than any scale.

#### Pillar C — The Question of Clinical Optimization

In one sentence: **what is your expanded panel saying, and how long has it been since you measured it?**

Chapter 4 established what needs to be on that panel. Chapters 9 to 11 showed how to read systems, hormones, pharmacology, and genomics as an integrated system. The Pillar C scorecard question is direct: do you have, from the last twelve months, ApoB, hs-CRP, HbA1c, fasting insulin, complete hormonal profile (total and free testosterone with SHBG in men; estradiol, progesterone, and FSH per reproductive stage in women), TSH with free T3 and T4, morning cortisol, IGF-1, vitamin D, B12, ferritin, homocysteine, and renal function with cystatin C and microalbuminuria? And, of the once-in-a-lifetime exams, do you already have Lp(a), MTHFR, and APOE?

If the answer is no for more than half the items, Pillar C is where the work begins — because without the panel, you are acting in the dark on the other pillars too. If the answer is yes for the majority, the next question is whether the book's target ranges are being applied (the ApoB/ApoA1 ratio below 0.6, homocysteine below 8, TSH in the functional range of 1.0 to 2.5, free testosterone above 9 pg/mL in men, Lp(a) at a value that defines whether the ApoB target needs to be even more aggressive), or whether you are settling for the "normal" range from the lab, which is the average of a population that includes many people in disease formation.

Imaging enters as essential complement. If you are between 40 and 75, with intermediate cardiovascular risk and no measured CAC score, this is the under-ten-minute exam that can change the entire prevention strategy — exactly as it did for Marcos. If there is clinical suspicion of masked hypertension, blood pressure oscillation between normal and elevated, or resistant pressure, 24-hour ABPM enters as a complement to the visit. Bone densitometry enters from menopause onward in women and in men with a suggestive history or prolonged use of medications that affect bone.

#### Pillar T — The Question of Tending Mind, Body & Bonds

In one sentence: **when your body shouts inflammation or cortisol and the other pillars are reasonably addressed, are you looking at the psychological dimension?**

This was the pillar Ana, in Chapter 12, discovered last — and that changed everything the other pillars had been unable to move. That is why the Pillar T scorecard has a specific structure: it isn't an isolated question, it is a question you ask when a combination appears.

The classic combination is CRP persistently above 1.5 mg/L and morning cortisol above 20 µg/dL despite Pillars A, C, and S reasonably worked. When that pattern appears, it is time to look at what the body is saying on the other side: how is your average level of perceived stress in recent months; how is your sleep quality subjectively (do you wake rested or do you wake with the day already weighing); how many people do you have whom you can count on in a real crisis, and do those people know it; when was the last time you spoke with a mental health professional, even preventively; and is there regular practice of nervous system regulation (structured breathing, MBSR, exercise, nature exposure) or is that part on the "someday I'll start" list?

The honest reading of this pillar is usually uncomfortable — and that is its function. Most patients I see with resistant CRP after months of careful biological work discover, in conversation, that the psychological dimension was never formally addressed. Recognizing that isn't weakness. It is the scorecard working.

#### Pillar S — The Question of Sleep, Rhythm & Recovery

In one sentence: **is the soil in which the other three pillars stand stable?**

Chapter 14 established why this is the right question. Sleep isn't a pillar like the others — it is the substrate. When the substrate gives way, the other three give way with it, even if they appear in order for months or years. That is what silently regressed in Paulo from twelve to eighteen months after the first visit, and what was recovered — and exceeded the previous peak — when apnea was finally identified and treated.

The Pillar S reading combines three levels. The first is habit: do you go to bed and wake at consistent times, with variation under sixty minutes between weekdays and weekends? Do you have morning sun exposure in the first sixty minutes after waking? Reduce screen light in the two hours before bed? Cool, dark bedroom? Last meal two to three hours before lying down? Caffeine contained to the first half of the day?

The second is specific sleep marker: is there any sign of obstructive apnea — persistent snoring, pauses observed by a partner, morning fatigue even with eight hours in bed, blood pressure resistant to treatment, unexplained low testosterone, new-onset atrial fibrillation, frequent middle-of-the-night bathroom trips? If yes, polysomnography is not an optional exam. If done, the AHI should be below 5; between 5 and 15 is mild apnea, 15 to 30 moderate, above 30 severe.

The third is the blood pressure pattern on 24-hour ABPM, when indicated: systolic pressure should fall at least 10% during sleep compared to wakefulness. The *non-dipper* pattern, where that fall doesn't happen, is frequently the first clinical clue of apnea or circadian disruption that hasn't been recognized.

When Pillar S is well worked, the other pillars work with greater effect. When Pillar S is poorly addressed, any gain in the other pillars tends to be temporary.

### Know Thyself — The Adherence Profile Changes the Strategy

Before choosing the two focuses of the quarter, there is a question that precedes and that affects all execution: **what kind of patient are you when it comes to adhering to the plan?**

Across years of clinic, I have learned to recognize four profiles that, despite individual variations, appear with enough frequency to help calibrate strategy from the start.

**The very disciplined patient** executes the plan with rigor regardless of close follow-up. They make the checklist, measure progress, review on their own. With this profile, my work is to avoid excess — the temptation to change ten things at once because the capacity exists. Paradoxically, the Rule of Two protects this patient from themselves.

**The patient disciplined in specific areas** has perfect execution in some domains and zero execution in others. It can be the athlete who trains rigorously and eats anything, the executive who cares about food and doesn't sleep, the person who has been in therapy for years but hasn't moved a metabolic marker. With this profile, the strategy is to prioritize **always the neglected domain** — because that is where the largest marginal gain lives.

**The moderately disciplined patient** starts well and loses traction after 4 to 6 weeks. With this profile, the solution is structural: more frequent returns at the start (every 4 to 6 weeks), explicitly smaller goals ("two strength workouts a week" instead of "four"), and short check-ins by message between visits. The problem isn't lack of will; it's that the distance between one return and the next is too large to sustain a forming habit.

**The patient who needs someone to "hold their hand"** can't execute alone for reasons ranging from mental overload to genuine difficulty with self-regulation. For this profile, an ambitious autonomous plan is doomed before it starts. What works is an ecosystem of support: a nutritionist with regular contact, a present personal trainer, a *coach* or habit mentor, a support group, an engaged family. Recognizing this profile early — in oneself — saves months of frustration.

The question to ask, with brutal honesty: of the four profiles above, which is closest to me *in practice*, not in self-image? The answer determines not only what to choose as focus, but how to organize for execution.

### The Rule of Two

This is where the scorecard stops being a checklist and becomes a plan.

The question the reader should ask after reading the four pillars isn't "in how many items am I in the alert zone?". It is another: **of the items in alert, which are the two with the greatest potential to move trajectory in the next three months?**

Two. Not ten.

Marcos arrived at the office in Chapter 5 with an entire panel in alert — CAC of 412 already functionally secondary prevention, ApoB of 82 still high despite a low-dose statin, insulin of 11, CRP of 1.6, vitamin D of 28, dispersed eating pattern, training without structure, and a sleep he didn't know was bad (five and a half hours on average, with two middle-of-the-night awakenings). If I had handed him a list of fifteen things to change, three months later he would have changed nothing. What we did in Chapters 7 and 8 was the opposite: **two principal attacks**. Pharmacological attack on ApoB, with statin intensification, addition of ezetimibe, and a target below 60. Behavioral attack on the three engines of Pillar A (Mediterranean alimentation with eating window, structured training across the four domains, specific vitamin D correction, and introduction of magnesium L-threonate for sleep). Eight months later, ApoB at 58, insulin at 6, CRP at 0.6, vitamin D at 52, exercise stress test with a 1.8 MET gain, body composition transformed. The CAC of 412 didn't change — deposited calcium does not dissolve. What changed was the speed of the process from then on.

Ana, in Chapter 12, when we finally addressed Pillar T, followed the same logic in another direction. Everything that depended on biological body had already been optimized in the previous eighteen months, and the gains were documented — vitamin D, insulin, ApoB, homocysteine, all in the optimal range. What was missing was a new front: structured treatment of the psychological dimension, with cognitive behavioral therapy as the principal track and daily nervous system regulation practice (MBSR and twenty minutes of morning mindfulness) as the parallel track. The low-dose antidepressant for six months entered as a shared decision with the psychiatrist, supporting both fronts. Six months later, CRP fell from 1.8 to 0.7 and morning cortisol from 22 to 14. What changed was the missing pillar — and the logic there wasn't to add ten psychological interventions at once. It was to address Pillar T with focus, while the other already-worked pillars stayed worked.

Paulo, in Chapter 14, when apnea was identified, also began with two fronts: titrated CPAP and complete circadian protocol (rigid schedule regularity, morning light, eating window, reduced caffeine and alcohol). Everything else — alimentation, training, supplementation — stayed as it was. Two fronts in the missing pillar recovered the entire trajectory in six months and brought testosterone to 540 ng/dL, above the previous peak.

The message is the same in all three cases: action concentrated on two points per quarter is what produces measurable movement. Action dispersed across ten fronts is what produces frustration. When you revisit your scorecard every three months and identify the two points to work on, write them down somewhere (a notebook page works, a notes file works), define how you will measure the result in three months, and focus. The other points of the scorecard stay there, awaiting their quarter. They don't disappear.

### Calibration by Decade

The composition of the scorecard shifts in weight by decade, even when the items remain the same.

**From 40 to 49**, the scorecard is mainly a tool of **early detection**. It is the window where intervention has the greatest return at the lowest cost, because the silent killers of Chapter 2 are already operating but most of them are still reversible with consistent work. Fernanda, in Chapter 6, is the clear example: at 41, with insulin of 13, steatosis, ApoB of 108, and vitamin D of 22, she reversed all the markers in six months with structured lifestyle and biomarker-guided supplementation. That window of reversibility is the rule in this decade, not the exception. The anchor exams to secure here: complete annual expanded panel; ApoB and Lp(a); body composition by quality bioelectrical impedance or DEXA; CAC between 40 and 45 if there is intermediate risk or family history of premature cardiovascular disease; colonoscopy from 45 onward (recommendation updated by the ACS in 2018 and by the USPSTF in 2021); annual mammography from 40 in women per ACS recommendation, with the USPSTF currently recommending biennial screening from 40 — both options worth discussing with your own doctor; basic mental health screening.

**From 50 to 59**, the scorecard becomes a tool of **maximum action**. Approximately half of premature cardiovascular deaths occur in this band. What is preserved here can be preserved for the following decades; what is lost here is harder to recover. Marcos, at 57 in Chapter 5, is the prototype patient of this decade — when the CAC of 412 forced recognition that primary prevention was no longer the right category, the entire strategy changed. To the items of the previous decade, add: CAC if not yet done; bone densitometry (postmenopausal women routinely; men with a suggestive history — prolonged corticoid use, hypogonadism, antiandrogen therapy for prostate cancer, or fragility fracture); annual low-dose chest CT in adults aged 50 to 80 with a tobacco load of at least 20 pack-years who are current smokers or have quit within 15 years (USPSTF 2021 recommendation); complete hormonal panel with attention to menopausal transition in women and testosterone decline in men; baseline cognitive evaluation for future comparison; fundoscopy every one to two years; transvaginal ultrasonography per gynecological guidance.

**From 60 onward**, the scorecard shifts axis: from preventing disease to **preserving function**. All previous items remain applicable, but the specific emphasis of this decade is functional evaluation — sit-to-stand from a chair, gait speed, hand grip strength with dynamometer, all with cutoffs validated for age. Body composition with special attention to sarcopenia: ASMI takes priority. Regular bone densitometry, annual or biennial, depending on initial T-score and treatment. Periodic cognitive reassessment. Rigorous sleep control, because apnea is more prevalent and more frequently underdiagnosed in this age band. Screening for hearing and vision disorders, because both impact cognition and balance. Periodic fall-risk evaluation, with review of medication use to remove what is unnecessarily sedative or hypotensive.

### Vaccination: The Adult Schedule No One Delivers

There is a category of preventive intervention that combines two rare characteristics: robust population evidence and below-reasonable adherence. It is adult vaccination.

The childhood immunization schedule is one of the most comprehensive in many countries — Brazil's and the U.S.'s among them — and most readers passed through it. The adult schedule, on the other hand, is nearly invisible. No patient of mine in recent years has come into the office with the adult schedule up to date without my pulling the question — and the reaction is almost always the same: *"Vaccines? I didn't know I needed any."*

You do. There are four vaccines that make a measurable difference in the trajectory of an adult over 40 — and one of them deserves specific emphasis.

**Herpes zoster (Shingrix).** What this vaccine prevents directly was already known: herpes zoster (shingles) and post-herpetic neuralgia, neuropathic pain that can last months or years. But recent studies have shown something unexpected: **those who take Shingrix have significantly lower risk of developing dementia** — a 20 to 30% reduction in two large studies published in 2024 and 2025. The mechanism is still being debated, but the magnitude of the finding placed Shingrix in another category: it isn't only prevention of incapacitating pain, it is one of the tools with the best available signal today for cognitive risk reduction. FDA-approved in the U.S. since 2017 (ACIP-recommended for adults 50+); Anvisa-approved in Brazil since 2022.

The other three of the adult schedule that matter:

**High-dose influenza**, recommended from age 65 — reduces post-flu hospitalization and mortality in an age band where severe viral infection can trigger heart attack, stroke, and functional decline.

**Pneumococcal conjugate 20 (Prevnar 20)**, single dose in adults over 50 — reduces severe pneumonia and pneumococcal bacteremia in an age band where these events frequently turn into prolonged hospitalization.

**Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)**, FDA-approved in 2023 for adults over 60 — prevents viral pneumonia previously confused with severe flu and that in many cases required admission.

If your scorecard has everything in green but your vaccine schedule is from 2018, a piece is missing.

### The Signs That Don't Wait

There is a category of symptom that takes the reader out of quarterly-scorecard mode and puts them in "call now" mode. It is worth separating clearly.

They are **cardiovascular**: chest pain triggered by effort or emotion, new-onset dyspnea on usual exertion, syncope (sudden fainting), persistent palpitations or palpitations associated with dizziness, claudication (calf muscle pain that appears on walking and resolves on stopping). **Metabolic**: unexplained weight loss (more than 5% in six months without dieting), persistent excessive thirst and urination, profound fatigue with abnormal glucose tests. **Neurological**: sudden loss of motor or sensory function, abrupt speech changes, new facial asymmetry, acute mental confusion, progressive memory loss that worries you or your family. **Renal and urinary**: sudden reduction in urine volume, persistent foam in urine, lower-extremity edema that doesn't improve with elevation. **Oncologic**: unexplained bleeding in any territory, new nodules that grow, skin changes (the ABCDE rule for pigmented lesions — asymmetry, irregular borders, color heterogeneity, diameter greater than 6 mm, evolution), profuse night sweats without obvious cause, persistent fever. **Mental health**: suicidal ideation, recurrent panic attacks, depression that doesn't respond to initial strategies, substance abuse.

For all of these, the scorecard doesn't apply. What applies is seeking specialized evaluation now. The rule is simple and absolute: **if you are in doubt about the severity of any of these signs, the doubt itself is the answer.**

### The Essential in 60 Seconds

- The scorecard is the self-assessment tool that organizes what the book delivered into a form that fits in your hand. It isn't a grade or a judgment — it is an honest reading of where you stand on each of the four ACTS pillars every three months, with the goal of identifying the points where concentrated action produces measurable movement.

- Habits and markers talk to each other: when an apparent good habit lives alongside an abnormal marker, it is the marker telling the truth — and the habit that needs revising. That was the clinical lesson of André in Chapter 3 and Fernanda in Chapter 6.

- The practical rule that organizes action is the Rule of Two: every quarter, choose the two items in the alert zone with the greatest potential to move trajectory — and focus on exactly those. That is how the treatment of Marcos in Chapters 7 and 8, of Ana in Chapters 12 and 13, and of Paulo in Chapter 14 worked. Concentrated action beats dispersed action.

- The composition of the scorecard shifts weight by decade: from 40 to 49 it is early detection in the window of maximum reversibility; from 50 to 59 it is maximum action in the window where most premature deaths can be averted; from 60 onward it is functional preservation, with emphasis on strength, mobility, body composition, and cognition.

- There are signs that don't wait for the next scorecard — new chest pain on exertion, syncope, sudden neurological deficits, unexplained bleeding, ABCDE skin changes, suicidal ideation, among others. For these, the rule is simple: if you are in doubt about severity, the doubt is the answer.

- Adult vaccination is one of the most effective and most neglected preventive interventions. Shingrix (herpes zoster), beyond preventing shingles, has accumulated recent evidence of cognitive risk reduction. High-dose influenza, Prevnar 20, and the RSV vaccine complete the essential schedule after 50–60.

- Whoever has reached this point in the book has the vocabulary and context to read their own panel. What is missing is the habit — revisiting the scorecard every three months, choosing two points, and working on exactly those two.
