## Chapter 6 — Metabolic Health: The Invisible Foundation

You already know Fernanda. In Chapter 4, she was the 41-year-old patient with all "normal" labs — and all of them far from optimal. Fasting insulin of 13. HOMA-IR of 3.0. ApoB of 108. CRP of 1.9. TG/HDL ratio of 3.9.

You saw the numbers. Now we'll see what they meant — what was happening inside her body to produce that pattern, why the standard checkup couldn't see it, and why the reversal she achieved in six months wasn't luck — it was biology.

When I ordered an abdominal ultrasound as part of the expanded evaluation, the result wasn't surprising: grade I hepatic steatosis. Mild fatty liver. And her GGT was 34 U/L — within the lab reference for women (up to 40), but above what we consider optimal (below 30).

Fernanda did not drink alcohol. She wasn't obese. She exercised. She ate "well." And she had fat in her liver. When I told her, the reaction was genuine confusion:

> *"Fatty liver? But I'm not overweight. I don't even drink."*

That confusion is exactly what we need to resolve.

### The Liver: Where Metabolic Dysfunction Materializes

If there is one organ that summarizes a person's metabolic health, it's the liver. It processes insulin. Manufactures cholesterol. Regulates triglycerides. Stores and releases glucose. When metabolism works well, the liver operates in silent equilibrium. When insulin resistance settles in, the liver is one of the first organs to register the impact.

Chronic hyperinsulinemia — that persistently elevated insulin the standard checkup doesn't measure — activates a process in the liver called *de novo lipogenesis*: the manufacture of fat from carbohydrates (the sugar and starch in bread, rice, pasta, fruit, and sweets — all broken down into glucose during digestion). The liver starts producing more triglycerides than it can export. Fatty acids accumulate inside hepatocytes. When more than 5% of liver cells contain fat, the diagnosis is steatosis.

This condition — now called **MASLD** (*Metabolic dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease*, the nomenclature formally adopted by AASLD, EASL, and ALEH (the Latin American Association for the Study of the Liver) in June 2023, replacing "non-alcoholic fatty liver disease") — affects roughly 38% of the world's adult population. Projections put it above 55% by 2040. It is the most common liver disease on the planet.

But the more revealing data isn't the prevalence — it's what steatosis means for the rest of the body. The most common cause of death in patients with MASLD is not liver disease — it's cardiovascular disease. Fatty liver isn't an isolated liver problem. It is the hepatic manifestation of a systemic metabolic dysfunction that is, simultaneously, inflaming arteries, fueling atherosclerosis, and creating the soil for diabetes.

This was exactly what was happening with Fernanda. Her fatty liver wasn't the disease. It was the most visible alarm signal of a process that had been underway for years — the same process that took her father to a heart attack at 58 and her mother to a diabetes diagnosis at 62.

And the cruelest part: up to 20% of normal-weight people develop steatosis — the same TOFI phenotype from Chapter 2, now manifest in the liver. Fernanda, with a BMI of 23 — slim by any conventional measure — was one of them.

### Why BMI Lies — and What to Measure Instead

Fernanda's case exposes a flaw that goes beyond blood tests: the way we measure the body is also wrong.

BMI — body mass index — divides weight by height squared. It is the most-used metric in the world for classifying weight. And it is profoundly misleading. BMI does not distinguish fat from muscle. It does not differentiate subcutaneous fat (the kind under the skin, relatively harmless) from visceral fat (the kind that deposits around organs and produces chronic inflammation). A muscular athlete may have an "overweight" BMI without any excess fat. A slim woman like Fernanda may have a "normal" BMI with visceral fat accumulated around her liver and pancreas. BMI classifies both incorrectly.

![Figure 6.2 — Same BMI, Different Bodies. Two abdominal cross-sections with identical BMI (24): on the left, predominantly subcutaneous fat (outer ring, organs free); on the right, TOFI pattern with visceral fat deposited between the organs.](../Cap06%20Fig02.PNG)

There is a better, simpler, and more predictive metric: **the waist-to-height ratio**. Divide your waist circumference by your height, both in the same unit. If the result is greater than 0.5 — that is, if your waist measures more than half your height — cardiometabolic risk is elevated.

A meta-analysis of more than 300,000 adults demonstrated that the waist-to-height ratio is superior to BMI for predicting diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and metabolic syndrome. The message is so simple it fits on a sticky note: **keep your waist below half your height.** It works for men and women, young and old, any ethnicity — without needing reference tables by sex or age.

Waist circumference alone is also useful. The most-used risk thresholds are: above 90 cm (35 inches) for men and 80 cm (31 inches) for women in Latin American populations; U.S. cutoffs (NHLBI/AHA) sit slightly higher at 102 cm (40 inches) for men and 88 cm (35 inches) for women. But as a proxy for visceral fat, the waist-to-height ratio is more precise.

For those who want to go further, **DEXA** (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry) — described in detail in Chapter 4 as the exam that delivers three biomarkers in one — is the most precise exam available for body composition: it quantifies total fat, visceral fat, muscle mass, and bone density in a single evaluation. Worth repeating here for the centrality it has in metabolic health: it isn't essential for everyone, but for patients like Fernanda, where BMI says "normal" and the body tells another story, DEXA reveals exactly where the fat is and how much muscle is there to protect metabolism.

A more accessible alternative is **bioelectrical impedance** — present in many home scales and offices. It estimates fat percentage and lean mass, but is less precise than DEXA and varies with hydration, time of day, and recent meals. Its value is in tracking trends: if fat percentage rises or falls over months, the direction is reliable even if the absolute number isn't.

### The Timeline: Where Fernanda Was — and Where She Was Heading

Metabolic dysfunction does not begin with diabetes. It begins with insulin resistance — and between the two there can be a decade or more of silent deterioration that the standard checkup does not detect.

The sequence is predictable:

![Figure 6.3 — From Insulin Resistance to Diabetes: The Timeline the Checkup Doesn't See. Progression bar with 4 phases (compensated IR → manifest dysfunction → prediabetes → type 2 diabetes) showing what happens in the body versus what the standard checkup says at each phase. Intervention window highlighted in green over Phases 1 and 2.](../Cap06%20Fig03.PNG)

**Phase 1 — Compensated insulin resistance.** Muscle, hepatic, and adipose cells start responding less to insulin. The pancreas compensates by producing more. Fasting insulin rises — to 10, 12, 15 µIU/mL. But glucose stays normal. No standard test detects the problem. This phase can last years.

**Phase 2 — Manifest metabolic dysfunction.** Chronic hyperinsulinemia begins to cause systemic effects: triglycerides rise, HDL drops, blood pressure increases slightly, visceral fat accumulates, the liver begins to accumulate fat. GGT rises. CRP rises. But fasting glucose is still between 90 and 105 — "borderline, monitor." This phase lasts more years.

**Phase 3 — Prediabetes.** The pancreas begins to lose the ability to compensate. HbA1c crosses 5.7%. The prediabetes diagnosis appears. But the process has already been underway for 5 to 10 years.

**Phase 4 — Type 2 diabetes.** HbA1c crosses 6.5%. The formal diagnosis arrives as a "surprise." But the process has been in motion for a decade or more.

What makes this timeline so dangerous is that in each phase, the metabolic dysfunction does not stay confined to glucose metabolism. It radiates into all other systems — simultaneously. In Phase 1, when insulin is already high but glucose is still "normal," ApoB may already be rising, the arteries are already being bombarded by atherogenic particles, chronic inflammation is already settling in, and the liver is already accumulating fat.

In Chapter 2, we described how insulin resistance connects the four silent killers. Now, with the biomarkers from Chapter 4 and the calcium score from Chapter 5, you have the tools to detect each stage of that cascade — and to intervene before it turns into a diagnosis.

Fernanda was in the transition between phases 1 and 2. Insulin of 13. HOMA-IR of 3.0. Glucose of 94 — perfectly "normal." HbA1c of 5.5 — two-tenths from prediabetes. Triglycerides of 180, HDL of 46, TG/HDL of 3.9 — all signs that lipid metabolism was already being distorted by hyperinsulinemia. Mild steatosis on ultrasound. CRP of 1.9 confirming that inflammation was accompanying the process.

If no one intervened, the likely trajectory was her mother's: prediabetes in her early 50s, diabetes by 60. With the added burden that the atherogenic dyslipidemia — elevated ApoB, small dense LDL particles — simultaneously raised cardiovascular risk. The same path as her father, who had a heart attack at 58.

Fernanda was carrying the metabolic inheritance from both sides of the family. But inheritance isn't destiny — it's predisposition. And predisposition, when identified, can be confronted.

### Why the Checkup Doesn't See — and What It Sees Too Late

Metabolic syndrome is defined by the presence of three or more of the following: elevated waist circumference, triglycerides above 150 mg/dL, HDL below 50 mg/dL in women, blood pressure above 130/85, and fasting glucose above 100.

Fernanda did not meet the criteria. Her waist circumference was at the limit. Her glucose was 94 — below 100. Her blood pressure was 118/76. By conventional criteria, she did not have metabolic syndrome.

But the insulin resistance that feeds all of this began years before any criterion was met. Metabolic syndrome criteria are disease thresholds, not risk thresholds. It's like requiring water to reach the roof to declare a flood — ignoring the entire period when the basement was already underwater.

The early biomarkers from Chapter 4 — fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, TG/HDL ratio, GGT — measure the level of the basement. And all of them were elevated in Fernanda's case. Each "normal" by the lab. None optimal. Together, unmistakable.

### When Fasting Lies: The André Case

Fernanda was caught by the fasting markers — insulin of 13, HOMA-IR of 3.0. But fasting doesn't always tell the whole truth.

André, whom we met in Chapter 3, came to the office with fasting insulin of 8.5 µIU/mL and HOMA-IR of 1.9. By conventional criteria, both normal. The lab printed the results in green. Any physician looking only at those numbers would say glucose metabolism was preserved.

But André had CRP of 2.1 and homocysteine of 14 — signs of chronic inflammation that didn't fit someone who exercised ten hours a week and didn't eat ultra-processed foods. Something was off. I ordered an oral glucose tolerance test with insulin curve — the OGTT with insulin measured at five time points: fasting, 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes after a 75 g glucose load.

The result was revealing. Fasting glucose was 92 — normal. At 30 minutes, 148. At 60 minutes, 162. At 90 minutes, 154. At 120 minutes, 131. Glucose had come back down, and the 120-minute value was within range. Most physicians would look only at fasting and 120 minutes and say: glucose tolerance preserved. But the insulin curve told another story. Fasting insulin: 8.5. At 30 minutes: 78. At 60 minutes: **124 — peak.** At 90 minutes: 118. At 120 minutes: 89. Insulin had shot to fourteen times baseline within an hour — an exaggerated response to keep glucose under control — before starting to drop. The pancreas was working much harder than it should, and only because of that did glucose look orderly.

In technical language, André had a **Kraft type II pattern** — early and exaggerated insulin peak (between 30 and 60 minutes) with significant compensatory hyperinsulinemia. His pancreas was producing far more insulin than it should to keep glucose in check. Glucose looked normal because insulin was screaming. And since no one had measured the scream, no one knew.

![Figure 6.4 — André's OGTT: when fasting lies. Dual curve of glucose (blue, left axis) and insulin (red, right axis) at times 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes after 75 g glucose. Glucose appears normal at fasting (92) and at 120 min (131), with moderate rise to 162 mg/dL at 60 min; insulin rises from 8.5 (fasting) to 124 µIU/mL at 60 min — disproportionate response, Kraft II pattern.](../Cap06%20Fig04.PNG)

As we saw in Chapter 4, Kraft's work on "diabetes in-situ" had already shown that most people with normal OGTT glucose have abnormal insulin patterns. Insulin resistance is, from the outset, a postprandial disorder. The body handles the problem after eating, not while fasting. By the time the disturbance shows up in fasting blood — as in Fernanda's case, with insulin of 13 — the process has been going on for years. André was at a stage where fasting still hid the problem. Only the glucose challenge revealed the truth.

I don't order an OGTT with insulin curve for every patient. But I order it when fasting raises suspicion without confirming — insulin between 6 and 10, HOMA-IR between 1.5 and 2.5, but with other discordant markers (elevated CRP, rising TG/HDL, strong family history, or simply a patient who "does everything right" while the numbers don't add up). André was the classic case. And without the curve, we would have lost years of intervention window.

### CGM — When the OGTT Doesn't Fit Daily Life

The OGTT reveals what fasting hides, but it is a laboratory provocation with a liquid glucose load. It does not show how **your** metabolism responds to your breakfast, to your rice and beans (a Latin American staple), to the wine at dinner. For that, there is the **continuous glucose monitor** (CGM): a coin-sized sensor applied to the arm that measures interstitial glucose every few minutes for 10 to 14 days. In the U.S., over-the-counter options like Stelo (Dexcom) and Lingo (Abbott) are widely available without prescription; FreeStyle Libre 3 and Dexcom G7 remain prescription products. Both are also available in Brazil.

The PREDICT study (Berry et al., *Nature Medicine* 2020) followed 1,002 healthy adults eating standardized meals and found **68% variability in glycemic response** to the same food across individuals — microbiome, sleep, genetics, and timing weighing more than macronutrients. In other words: the coffee that's benign for a neighbor may spike your glucose. Only your personal CGM shows it.

The use I recommend is **occasional**: two to four weeks per year, in patients with borderline markers or motivated to map individual triggers and adjust routine. It is not for continuous use.

An honest caveat: the American Diabetes Association's 2024 position is that **there is insufficient evidence** for CGM as screening in non-diabetics, and no trial has shown its use reduces heart attack or diabetes. It is a tool for metabolic self-knowledge and behavior change — not a tool of outcome medicine. That is the ruler with which I prescribe it.

### When the Problem Is in the Gut — An Underestimated Diagnosis

There are still patients with a reasonable metabolic panel and careful nutrition who keep symptoms — postprandial bloating, fatigue after eating, cognitive fog after meals, bowel changes that don't fit a clear intolerance. With the usual causes ruled out, a diagnosis to consider is **small intestinal bacterial overgrowth** (SIBO).

The small intestine normally has a bacterial density much lower than the colon. When that balance breaks, colonic bacteria migrate to the small intestine and ferment carbohydrates that should only be absorbed there. The most common causes are prolonged use of proton pump inhibitors (low stomach acid), reduced intestinal motility, post-bariatric surgery, and chronic dysbiosis after repeated antibiotics. The result is gases — hydrogen, methane, sometimes hydrogen sulfide — local low-grade inflammation, and metabolic interference that spills over into systemic inflammation. Yet another contribution to the persistent CRP this book has been treating.

The diagnosis is the **breath test for exhaled hydrogen and methane** with lactulose or glucose. Peaks above the cutoffs (ΔH₂ > 20 ppm within 90 min, or baseline CH₄ > 10 ppm) confirm the diagnosis. Treatment usually involves rifaximin (sometimes with neomycin) combined with motility and gut-barrier strategies.

SIBO is not a fad diagnosis — it is the frontier where gastroenterology meets metabolic medicine. In a patient with persistent GI symptoms, resistant CRP, and a history of antacids, it's worth investigating before adding a tenth supplement.

### What We Did — and Why It Worked

In Chapter 4, I mentioned that in six months Fernanda reversed the markers: insulin from 13 to 7, CRP from 1.9 to 0.8, vitamin D from 22 to 48. But I didn't explain why. Now, with the metabolic map in hand, we can understand the logic. The intervention attacked the root — insulin resistance — not the branches.

**Restructured nutrition.** Not a temporary "diet," but a permanent reorganization of eating pattern. Reduction of ultra-processed foods and added fructose — the main drivers of hepatic lipogenesis that was filling Fernanda's liver with fat. Increase in quality protein, vegetables, fiber, and monounsaturated fats. A 10-hour eating window aligned with the active phase of the day, which by itself improves hepatic insulin sensitivity and reduces triglycerides — even without calorie reduction.

**Strength training added.** Fernanda exercised — but it was light cardio. We included three weekly resistance training sessions. Skeletal muscle is the body's largest glucose consumer. The more functional muscle, the greater the capacity to take up glucose without depending on elevated insulin. Strength training improves insulin sensitivity through a direct mechanism: it increases the translocation of GLUT4 transporters to the muscle cell membrane. In simple terms: more muscle means less insulin needed to do the same work.

**Vitamin D corrected.** Fernanda had 22 ng/mL — "sufficient" by the lab, but far from optimal. As we saw in Chapter 4, when vitamin D is below 40 ng/mL, parathyroid hormone (PTH) stays chronically elevated to compensate for impaired calcium absorption. That elevated PTH not only compromises bone health but is also associated, in observational studies, with greater insulin resistance and worse beta-cell function in the pancreas. By correcting vitamin D to 48 ng/mL, PTH suppresses, calcium stabilizes, and an aggravating factor of insulin resistance is removed. It isn't the principal intervention — but it is a missing piece.

**Sleep and stress assessed.** Fernanda averaged six hours of sleep per night and described her stress level as "normal for someone with a job and two kids." Insufficient sleep and chronic stress raise cortisol — and chronically high cortisol directly worsens insulin sensitivity, promotes visceral fat accumulation, and increases inflammation. These are not "emotional" variables — they are metabolic variables. They will be deepened in the chapters ahead.

The plan combined lifestyle restructuring with biomarker-guided smart adjuncts — vitamin D corrected to optimal, omega-3 at supplement dose, and magnesium in glycinate form, items Chapter 8 will detail and which entered from the first month. Everything monitored every three months. The protocol was lifestyle — but lifestyle with precision.

In six months, the numbers told a different story. Insulin dropped from 13 to 7 — within the optimal range. HOMA-IR from 3.0 to 1.7. Triglycerides from 180 to 108. HDL rose from 46 to 54. The TG/HDL ratio dropped from 3.9 to 2.0. CRP from 1.9 to 0.8. GGT normalized to 22 U/L. The follow-up ultrasound: no steatosis.

Fernanda reversed the process. Six years before any conventional diagnosis. No surgery. Different from her father. Different from her mother. Because someone looked early.

### Adjuncts for When Lifestyle Wasn't Enough

Fernanda reversed her metabolic markers in six months on lifestyle alone. That is the protagonism this book defends — and with good reason, because for most patients in the early phase of insulin resistance it's what actually works. But there are cases where structured work on nutrition, training, and sleep reduces the markers partially, without bringing them to target. For these patients, there are some supplemental tools with reasonable evidence of added effect — adjuncts, not substitutes for structured work.

**Berberine.** It is the nutraceutical with the most robust evidence package on this front. It activates a cellular enzyme called AMPK through the same pathway metformin uses — which is why some authors already refer to berberine as "natural metformin." Recent meta-analyses show HbA1c reductions of 0.5 to 0.7% in patients with insulin resistance or mild diabetes, with magnitude comparable to low-dose metformin in some studies. It also modestly reduces LDL and triglycerides. The Achilles' heel is the bioavailability of pure berberine, which is poor — so it only makes sense in formulations with validated absorption: **dihydroberberine** or **berberine-phytosome**. Usual dose: 500 mg twice a day. The most frequent adverse effects are gastrointestinal (discomfort, bowel changes) and tend to diminish with dose adjustment. **Important alert in nephrology practice:** berberine interacts via CYP3A4 with immunosuppressants like tacrolimus and cyclosporine, and with statins — combinations that require evaluation before starting in transplanted or polymedicated patients.

**Inositol.** Here the context is more specific. Inositol — in the myo-inositol + D-chiro-inositol combination at a 40-to-1 ratio — has solid evidence in polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), where it reduces HOMA-IR and improves ovulation in meta-analyses. In insulin resistance without PCOS, the evidence is more modest. It has well-established safety. Usual dose: 2 g of myo-inositol + 50 mg of D-chiro-inositol twice a day. A particularly useful tool in women with IR and altered hormonal patterns. It is worth noting a reasonable clinical synergy: magnesium — already part of the basic supplementation in Chapter 8 — is a cofactor at the insulin receptor, while inositol acts as a second messenger of the same pathway. Direct trials of this specific combination are still scarce, but the biological logic is sound and the safety profile allows the association in selected patients.

**Silymarin (milk thistle).** It is the most-studied hepatoprotectant in metabolic-associated steatosis. Meta-analyses show ALT reductions of about 9 U/L in controlled studies. Not a miracle, but it can work as a lifestyle adjunct in patients with mild liver enzyme elevation — and as an economical bridge while resmetirom isn't yet available in Brazil. Standard dose is based on silybin, the active component, around 140 mg two to three times a day.

Three other tools deserve a brief mention. **N-acetylcysteine (NAC)**, a glutathione precursor — smaller studies show ALT reduction in steatosis; 600–1,200 mg/day. **Alpha-lipoic acid**, with consolidated evidence in diabetic peripheral neuropathy but modest data in IR proper; 600 mg/day on an empty stomach. **Taurine**, with a 2023 *Science* study suggesting an association between low levels and accelerated biological aging — hard human evidence still weak, a promising frontier, outside the routine clinical repertoire.

**Curcumin** also belongs in this metabolic conversation — it reduces hepatic enzymes in steatosis in small studies and improves insulin sensitivity in modest meta-analyses. But since it was already discussed in Chapter 5 in cardiovascular inflammation territory, here I only reiterate the two main alerts: formulations with validated absorption (Theracurmin, Meriva, Longvida) and care with the hepatic interaction with immunosuppressants when combined with piperine.

The general logic of this arsenal is the same logic as the rest of the book. None of these replaces nutritional restructuring, strength training, sleep correction, or stress management. But in selected patients, biomarker-guided, with lifestyle work already underway and markers still off-target, they are legitimate adjuncts — the precision-medicine toolbox includes these items, with judgment.

### When the Reversal Doesn't Arrive in Time

Fernanda came early. Her liver had fat, but it wasn't yet inflamed to the point of starting to scar. That stage — when liver cells, saturated for years, begin to be replaced by scar tissue — has its own name: **MASH**, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis. It is fatty liver that has become active disease.

Not everyone arrives in time. The same process Fernanda reversed in six months, in a patient who only finds out ten or fifteen years later, has already passed the point where nutrition and exercise alone can do the job. The fat has settled, the liver has inflamed, scarring has begun. At this point, weight loss continues to be essential — but it may not be enough to undo the damage already done.

Until March 2024, no medication was approved specifically to treat MASH. None. The approach was to treat around the disease — insulin resistance, weight, glucose, cholesterol — and hope scarring stopped advancing.

That month, the FDA approved **resmetirom** (brand name Rezdiffra), the first medication in history for MASH at intermediate-to-advanced fibrosis stage. In the trial that supported the approval — nearly a thousand patients, biopsy before and after — between a quarter and a third of people resolved the liver inflammation, and a quarter improved by at least one fibrosis grade, against about 10% on placebo.

In the U.S., Rezdiffra has been available since the 2024 launch. In Brazil, in 2026, resmetirom is not yet registered with Anvisa. The only access route is individual importation with prescription and documentation, at high cost.

Even when it arrives, resmetirom won't be first-line for everyone. For those at the start of the process, like Fernanda, the intervention remains the same one you just read: restructured nutrition, strength training, correction of what's deficient, sleep, stress management. For those with insulin resistance and elevated weight, the GLP-1 analogues that will be discussed in Chapter 9 — semaglutide and tirzepatide — also reduce steatosis significantly and are already available. Resmetirom enters later: for the patient whose scarring has begun, in whom lifestyle reversal has lost some of its power, and for whom a liver-specific therapy finally exists.

For decades, those who arrived late to the liver got the same message: *"lose weight, come back in six months."* It was honest, but insufficient. For the first time, there is a second message.

### What If It Were You?

If someone once told you your liver "is a little fatty" and recommended "lose weight and avoid fried foods" — without ordering fasting insulin, without calculating HOMA-IR, without looking at TG/HDL, without measuring GGT against an optimal range — the workup was half done. And the half that was missing is the half that tells the real story.

Metabolic dysfunction does not announce itself with symptoms. It doesn't hurt. It doesn't itch. It doesn't bother you — until the day it shows up as diabetes, as a heart attack, as advanced steatosis, or as a diagnosis that could have been avoided.

The diabetes diagnosis arrives when the pancreas gives up compensating. The opportunity to intervene arrives five to ten years earlier — at the point where insulin is already screaming, but glucose is still obeying.

The biomarkers from Chapter 4 answer that question. And the answer, as Fernanda demonstrated, can arrive a decade before the conventional diagnosis.

### The Essential in 60 Seconds

- Metabolic dysfunction is the foundation on which chronic diseases are built. Insulin resistance is the central event — and it begins years or decades before the diabetes diagnosis.

- Fatty liver (MASLD) affects 38% of the world's adult population and is the hepatic manifestation of insulin resistance. It isn't a liver problem — it is a sign of systemic metabolic dysfunction. The leading cause of death in these patients is cardiovascular.

- The standard checkup detects dysfunction too late. Metabolic syndrome criteria are disease thresholds, not risk thresholds. Fasting insulin and HOMA-IR detect the problem 5 to 10 years before glucose leaves the range.

- Metabolic dysfunction is reversible when identified early. Nutrition, strength training, vitamin D correction, sleep, and stress management act in synergy on the same root — insulin resistance.

- When the intervention attacks the root (insulin resistance) and not isolated branches, seemingly independent markers — insulin, inflammation, steatosis, vitamin D — move together. That's what happened with Fernanda in six months.

- For patients who have started lifestyle work but whose markers haven't yet hit target, there are nutraceutical adjuncts with reasonable evidence. Berberine (in formulations with validated absorption) is the most robust — reducing HbA1c at a magnitude comparable to low-dose metformin. Inositol is particularly useful in women with PCOS, with reasonable clinical synergy with magnesium. Silymarin helps in mild steatosis. None replaces structured work.

- When steatosis progresses to MASH (inflamed liver beginning to scar), lifestyle remains central, but reversal becomes harder. Since March 2024, resmetirom (Rezdiffra) has been the first medication in the world approved specifically for MASH at moderate-to-advanced fibrosis — FDA-approved in the U.S. and now widely available; not yet registered by Anvisa in Brazil. GLP-1 analogues (semaglutide, tirzepatide) also reduce steatosis and are already available.
