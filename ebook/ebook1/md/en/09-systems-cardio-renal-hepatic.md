***Pillar C — Clinical Optimization***

## Chapter 9 — Systems — Heart, Kidney, Liver, and Metabolism as One

Clinical optimization is the part of The ACTS Method that the patient cannot do alone. This is where biomarker-guided pharmacology, integrated systems reading, and the logic of iterative medicine come in — test, intervene, retest, adjust. It is the part of the work where the physician stops being a lifestyle counselor and becomes the pilot of a data panel.

This chapter opens Pillar C with the four systems that, for a long time, were treated in separate compartments — heart, kidney, liver, and metabolism — and that, over the last five years, medicine has begun to read as a single system. The next chapter enters the biochemical and hormonal panels that feed the decisions. The third enters the deepest layer: genomics, epigenetics, and the hidden exposures that sabotage the rest.

### Precision Pharmacology: Medications as Longevity Tools

The first front of clinical optimization is the strategic use of medications — not as a last resort when everything fails, but as tools that, guided by biomarkers, can change the risk trajectory when lifestyle alone isn't enough.

**Statins: guided by CAC and ApoB, not just LDL.** As we saw in Chapters 4 and 5, ApoB is a marker superior to LDL for estimating real atherosclerotic risk, and the CAC score reveals the presence of plaque when it's still invisible to conventional calculators. The combination of those two pieces of information changes the decision about a statin. A patient with a "borderline" LDL of 135 mg/dL but CAC of 0 and ApoB of 72 probably doesn't need a statin at this moment — risk is low. Another patient with the same LDL, CAC of 220, and ApoB of 110 is in a completely different scenario: plaque is already there, ApoB shows real atherogenic load, and aggressive treatment is clearly indicated. Marcos's plan, in Chapter 5, included intensification of his statin and the addition of ezetimibe not because of the isolated LDL number, but because of the combination: CAC 412, ApoB 82 on a low-dose statin, and the need to drive ApoB to 60 or below.

**Metformin and the longevity horizon.** Metformin, the first-line medication for type 2 diabetes, has been studied for over a decade as a possible "geroprotective" — that is, a drug that acts directly on the mechanisms of aging. The TAME (*Targeting Aging with Metformin*) clinical trial, proposed by Nir Barzilai in 2015, was designed to test whether metformin would reduce a composite endpoint (cardiovascular, cognitive, oncologic, and mortality) in people without diabetes. More than a decade later, TAME has yet to be fully funded — in part because the FDA does not recognize "aging" as a formal medical indication. Meanwhile, observational data and small trials suggest that metformin modestly reduces biological age measured by epigenetic clocks and has favorable effects on multiple metabolic and inflammatory markers. But there are contradictory signals — a 2025 review in *Ageing Research Reviews* noted that recent trials in people without diabetes (like MET-PREVENT) found no benefit on physical function. Medical honesty here is to recognize that the evidence is still insufficient to prescribe metformin only for longevity in people without insulin resistance or prediabetes. For patients with documented insulin resistance, borderline HbA1c, or metabolic syndrome, the indication is clearer — and frequently justified.

**The geromedicine frontier: what's being researched, what's not yet prescribed.** Some fields of preventive medicine operate at the boundary between research and clinical practice. It is where the science of aging has generated the most noise in recent years — and where the reader who follows longevity news has probably heard the loudest promises. It's worth honestly separating what is reasonable to consider under careful protocol from what still belongs exclusively to research territory.

**Low-dose rapamycin** is a medication originally developed to prevent transplant rejection. At high continuous doses it is a strong immunosuppressant; at low intermittent doses (5 to 10 mg once a week), it partially inhibits the mTOR pathway — one of the few mechanisms with consistent evidence of life extension in animal models. In humans, the phase 2 **PEARL** trial (2024–2025) showed reasonable safety with intermittent dosing, but the effect on hard outcomes is not yet established. Possible side effects: mouth ulcers, lipid changes, transient drops in immune cells. I don't prescribe it routinely. Outside of trials, it is off-label and experimental use.

**Senolytics** are molecules that selectively eliminate cells that have stopped dividing but stay in the tissue, secreting inflammatory compounds that age the surroundings. The most-studied combination in humans is **dasatinib + quercetin** — an oncology medication paired with a plant flavonoid, in low intermittent doses. A 2019 pilot trial in patients with diabetic kidney disease showed reduced senescence markers after brief cycles. Other compounds under study include fisetin (another flavonoid) and molecules still in preclinical phases. It is still research territory. There is no established clinical indication, and prescribing in the office outside of protocol is irresponsible. The reader following the topic should expect results from larger trials over the coming years.

**NAD+ precursors** also deserve context. NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) is a molecule essential for mitochondrial function and DNA repair pathways. Its levels fall substantially with age. Two molecules are sold as precursors to raise NAD+ inside cells: **nicotinamide riboside (NR)** and **nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN)**. A 2021 study showed that NMN improved insulin sensitivity in postmenopausal women with prediabetes. Other human studies, generally small and short, show elevation of blood NAD+, but have yet to demonstrate clear effects on functional outcomes or longevity. They are molecules of low known risk, expensive, and with marketing far ahead of the science.

**Intravenous longevity therapy ("drips").** A note on the expansion of "IV therapy" clinics. Intravenous infusions of NAD+, vitamin cocktails (the "Myers"), glutathione, high-dose vitamin C, and other combinations are offered with promises of energy, cognitive performance, and antiaging. Hard human evidence remains limited: small trials, subjective endpoints, hard-to-discount placebo effects. Intravenous NAD+ does not, today, have a randomized trial showing functional benefit in healthy adults; the Myers cocktail is an empirical formulation from the 1970s, still without clinical outcome support; intravenous high-dose vitamin C has experimental oncology data, but doesn't sustain routine preventive use. As with any parenteral route, there are practical concerns: product quality, application technique, and attention to anyone with borderline kidney function. In my office, I do not include these infusions in the preventive routine — I use the IV route only for specific indications, such as IV iron replacement in documented deficiency or parenteral B12 in malabsorption. I recognize that some patients report subjective well-being after sessions; what is still missing is hard human evidence to place them in the clinical longevity plan.

**Urolithin A, spermidine, *Akkermansia*.** Three tools that crossed the lab-to-market boundary in recent years. **Urolithin A** — produced by some intestinal bacteria from compounds found in pomegranate and walnuts — induces mitochondrial renewal and showed improvement in muscle strength in a clinical trial published in 2022. **Spermidine**, present in wheat germ, aged cheeses, and mushrooms, induces cellular autophagy; observational cohorts suggest an association with lower mortality, but randomized trials in humans are still modest. ***Akkermansia muciniphila***, a bacterium of the gut microbiota, showed benefit on metabolic markers when supplemented in a 2019 trial. None of the three has, today, evidence strong enough to be a routine recommendation.

How to think about that frontier. The rule I apply to myself — and therefore propose to the reader — is epistemic honesty: what has hard outcome evidence in humans enters the clinical plan. What has animal signal and small human studies stays on the table, in debate, without automatic prescription. And what is still mechanistic promise without solid clinical data stays on this side of preventive care — on the radar to follow, not in the office to prescribe. Longevity marketing doesn't wait for science; the physician must.

### The Cardio-Renal-Metabolic Axis: The Quiet Pivot

One of the most important transformations in internal medicine over the last five years happened almost discreetly. No newspaper headlines. No campaign posters. But anyone working with chronic patients saw, in real time, an entire generation of medications change their label. Drugs that began life as "diabetes medication" became transversal tools of cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic protection — used in patients who never had elevated glucose.

The pivot is informally called the **cardio-renal-metabolic axis**. The idea is simple and difficult at once. Simple: type 2 diabetes, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and obesity are not separate diseases. They are different manifestations of a single process of metabolic dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and mechanical overload, feeding each other in cycles. Difficult: for decades, medicine treated each one in airtight compartments — the endocrinologist handled diabetes, the cardiologist handled failure, the nephrologist handled kidneys, and each prescribed their own list. The pivot of recent years has been recognizing that three classes of medications, each with robust trials, act simultaneously across all three territories.

**The SGLT2 inhibitors** — dapagliflozin (Farxiga in the U.S., Forxiga in Brazil; FDA-approved since 2014, available in Brazil since 2012, and distributed free of charge through Brazil's "Farmácia Popular" public pharmacy program since February 2025) and empagliflozin (Jardiance) — were born as antidiabetics. Trials like DAPA-HF, EMPEROR, and DELIVER showed they reduce hospitalization for heart failure in patients **without diabetes**. DAPA-CKD and EMPA-KIDNEY showed they reduce the progression of chronic kidney disease, again in patients with and without diabetes. In 2024, a review in *Nature Reviews Nephrology* consolidated the role of this class as broad cardio-renal protective therapy. The mechanism goes well beyond glucose: the drug offloads mechanical stress on the heart, improves endothelial function, reduces inflammation, and shifts cardiac metabolism toward a more efficient fuel. In many patients today, prescribing an SGLT2 inhibitor is no longer a decision about diabetes — it is a decision about cardiorenal trajectory.

**Finerenone** — sold as Kerendia in the U.S. (Bayer; brand name Firialta in Brazil since 2023) — is a second piece of that board. It is a mineralocorticoid receptor blocker, a non-steroidal cousin of spironolactone, with a much cleaner side-effect profile. The FIDELIO-DKD, FIGARO-DKD, and FINEARTS-HF trials showed reductions in cardiac events, kidney progression, and heart failure hospitalizations in patients with diabetic kidney disease. In 2025, the FIND-CKD trial extended the benefit to **non-diabetic** kidney disease — which in my specialty is a milestone. Another rung where "diabetes drug" stops being the right label.

**GLP-1 agonists and dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists** — semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro/Zepbound) — make up the third piece. Beyond the effect on weight and steatosis already discussed, the study that changed the conversation was **SELECT** (*NEJM* 2023): more than 17,000 adults **without diabetes**, with obesity and established cardiovascular disease, followed for about three years. Semaglutide reduced the combination of heart attack, stroke, and cardiovascular death by ~20% versus placebo. We had never seen this in patients without diabetes — that was statin territory. Now it belongs to both.

These medications reduce systemic inflammation, improve insulin sensitivity, lower hepatic steatosis, and there are emerging signals on neuroprotective effects. In patients with refractory metabolic syndrome, obesity with comorbidities, or persistent difficulty losing weight despite structured lifestyle intervention, they can be decisive tools. They are not, and should not be, substitutes for nutrition, exercise, and sleep — they are catalysts in specific contexts. The off-label use in people without clear indication, just to lose a few pounds, is a phenomenon medicine is still learning to handle, and the long-term evidence in those contexts is still being built.

The next generation of this class is already in phase 3 trials: **retatrutide**, which acts on three receptors simultaneously (GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon), showed weight reductions near 24% in twelve months in a phase 2 study — magnitude close to bariatric surgery. The cardiovascular outcomes trials are still underway.

How does this change practice in the preventive medicine office? The question becomes different. Instead of "does my patient have diabetes?" — the old question of checking a single number — the question becomes "where is this patient on the axis?" Does the patient have fatty liver, abdominal obesity, blood pressure starting to climb, creatinine at the upper limit, any sign of cardiac overload? This is an SGLT2 patient, even if blood sugar is perfect. Does the patient have insulin resistance with obesity and established cardiovascular disease? This is a GLP-1 patient, even if they never crossed the diabetes line. Does the patient have chronic kidney disease and cardiac comorbidities, with or without diabetes? Finerenone enters the equation.

This doesn't mean medicating everyone. The logic of this entire chapter applies here too: these drugs are precision tools, not elixirs. The indication needs a biomarker, the use needs monitoring. But the decision space has changed. The intervention window this book defends — before the diagnosis, before the event — now has three new pharmacological classes inside it.

The quiet pivot has already happened. Anyone starting to read about prevention today needs to know that preventive medicine for the heart, the kidney, and the metabolism is, increasingly, one and the same.

The common logic across all these medications: they are tools, not solutions. Prescribed at the right moment, for the right patient, guided by the right biomarkers, they can change trajectories. Prescribed indiscriminately, as elixirs, they lose efficacy and gain adverse effects.

But for the right tool to be chosen, you need to read the right panel. And that is where the next chapter comes in — the hormonal and biochemical biomarkers that, correctly interpreted, separate one picture from another and turn assumption into conduct.
